# Lecture 28: Genres of History and the Great Debate at Samye

## Summary
The session had two halves. The first was a methods lesson: the same historical material (Trisong Detsen, his ministers, Samye monastery, his use of Buddhism) produces completely different books depending on whether you write dynastic, political, ethnic, Buddhist, border, material, architectural or spatial history. The instructor's point was that a historian never recovers all the facts, so every account is written from a chosen perspective, and that choosing and holding a perspective is exactly what you must do in your own reading and writing for this course.

The second half moved to new content: the Samye debate. After reviewing what Trisong Detsen did inside Samye once it was built (ordaining the first Tibetan monks, organizing teaching and tantric empowerment, launching a state-sponsored translation project lasting decades), the instructor introduced the debate as a three-way encounter between Tibet, China and India. Most of the time went to background on Chinese Buddhism: the anxiety of the final age of the Dharma, the proliferation of conflicting translated scriptures, the classification schemes of Tiantai and Huayan, the dispute over buddha-nature, and the contrast between Pure Land (reliance on other power) and Chan (reliance on self). The class ended mid-topic on the Chan lineage list, with Mahakashyapa's flower and Bodhidharma as the twenty-eighth patriarch. The actual debate between Moheyan and Kamalashila was deferred to the next session.

## Lecture notes

### Subgenres of history
History has subgenres. Ones named in class: dynastic history, ethnic history (if you place Tibetan people as the subject), Buddhist history, political history, border history, material history, architectural history, spatial history.

### The thought experiment: one figure, two books
The instructor asked what you would include if told to write a dynastic history of Trisong Detsen. Student answers: military affairs, state-level politics, how he consolidated and strengthened his power, and Buddhism as an instrument serving that consolidation.

Then the same figure as the subject of a Buddhist history. Student answers: he built Samye monastery, and he incorporated Buddhism into the state, which the instructor restated as how Buddhism as a tool serves the formation of the state.

His formulation: the ingredients are the same (ministers, Buddhism, Samye, his power), but we cook different dishes. A dynastic history analyzes the same material through political science and diplomacy. A religious history focuses on interactions between Buddhism and the state.

### The methodological conclusion
A student offered that the perspectives are tied to each other, and the instructor pushed for the "however." The answer he wanted concerned facts. Historians are obsessed with recovering, reshaping and rebuilding historical fact, but we cannot know all the factors and cannot hold all the facts at once. We can only approach from one perspective. He added his own doubt: when scholars produce competing accounts of the same "facts," they sometimes lack common ground to debate each other at all.

### What this means for your reading and writing
This was framed as the second stage of a plan that began earlier in the semester. Stage one was: when given a prompt, do not jump to the answer, stop and ask what the question is for and how you will design your analysis of it. Stage two is perspective:

- When reading, stay alert to the perspective from which the writer approaches the topic.
- When writing, design your own perspective deliberately (political, military, economic) and be clear about it in your own mind.
- The payoff is consistency. If your perspective is fixed, you will not drift from one frame to another, and your logic and method stay consistent.

### Border history as an example
He raised border history as a specific subfield he is interested in. Students suggested it studies how borders of countries or territories change over time: geography, bureaucracy, and people moving across borders. He added that the object is the shifting expression of the borderland, and that it looks abstract but scholars work on solid materials.

His substantive claim: borders are not necessarily the fixed state lines we inherited after the Second World War, when it became an assumption that every state has determined lines on a map. Many regions exist without clear boundaries marking them. He described these as flowing spaces.

### Samye as a writing object
If Samye monastery is your topic, the possible histories are:

1. **Material history.** Treat Samye as a material object and collect everything about its physical fabric.
2. **Architectural history.** Not only the style, including the three storeys discussed in earlier classes, but what happens in the building.
3. **Spatial history.** The architecture creates a space, and specific things happened in that space.

He summarized what the course had already covered: Samye's origin, who built it and under what dramatic circumstances; its layers as representations of religion and cosmology, with the axis mundi of Mount Meru; and its styles as a convergence of Buddhism from two sources, Indic and Chinese. What had not been summarized clearly was what happened inside Samye after construction.

### What Trisong Detsen did inside Samye
Three things:

1. **Ordination.** He ordained seven ministers, drawn from the aristocratic class, as the first Tibetan monks. The number seven generated a digression (below). Once there is a sangha, the sangha has work: study Buddhism, teach Buddhism, and because teaching requires texts, translate.
2. **Buddhist activities and tantric empowerment.** Teaching Buddhism, and inviting Padmasambhava as a tantric practitioner to bestow empowerment. The instructor recalled a small room inside Samye where Trisong Detsen is said to have received tantric empowerment from Padmasambhava together with his ministers and his consort. The consort's name in the audio is garbled; from the readings the figure associated with Padmasambhava as consort is Yeshe Tsogyel, a lady of the court.
3. **Translation.** Not a small undertaking. A large-scale, state-sponsored project lasting many years and decades, not one or two years.

### Digression on the number seven
Seven is auspicious and appears in multiple scenarios. Students and instructor assembled: seven buddhas of the past; the newborn Buddha taking seven steps in each direction before pointing to the heavens and declaring himself supreme between heaven and earth; and Queen Maya dying seven days after his birth and being reborn in a heaven rather than in one of the lower realms (hungry ghost, animal, hell). A student's correction that there are six realms, not seven, was accepted.

### The Samye debate: why it matters
The instructor called it a great event in Tibetan history and in Inner Asian history, with influence still felt in Tibet today. Monastic scholars still discuss it, and it is a live topic in Western academia with a substantial literature. Even if you forget the three names, you must know that it involved three parts: Tibet, China and India.

- **Tibet:** Trisong Detsen, the key figure and host.
- **China:** Moheyan. The instructor explained the name is a Chinese phonetic transcription of the Sanskrit "Mahayana," and that his original Chinese name is not known. It was not his ordination name in China.
- **India:** the Indian side, which the instructor said should be obvious given that Tibet received Buddhism from India and China. He noted one qualification, that a small amount came via Korea, which he treated as a minor matter.

From the readings, the Korean connection is the Zen teacher Reverend Kim, whom the nobleman Ba Selnang met in China, and the Indian representative at the debate is Kamalashila, Shantarakshita's student.

### How China enters: conquest of Dunhuang
Trisong Detsen was ambitious and his reign reached a peak. He expanded eastward by military force and seized Dunhuang. A student supplied the reason: it sat at the center of the Silk Road. Taking it meant taking an economic hub and a cultural hub, with tax revenue from commercial exchange and access to cultural resources.

On dates, the instructor hesitated aloud and gave something like 768 while qualifying it as approximate. Both assigned texts place the conquest in the 780s. He put the length of occupation at fifty to eighty years; Kapstein says roughly three-quarters of a century.

He also mentioned westward expansion, explicitly flagging that he had no historical material to prove it: that Trisong Detsen reached the Ganges and set a marker dividing what belonged to Tibet from what belonged to India. Note that in Kapstein this Ganges stele tradition is attached to Songtsen Gampo, not Trisong Detsen, and is described as a later tradition that cannot be dismissed out of hand.

Dunhuang was a Buddhist center because Buddhists traveled with merchant groups. Monks from India, China and across Asia gathered there and exchanged knowledge, and monasteries and libraries were established, which means books and monks from China were available. When Trisong Detsen took the region, he brought Chinese monks to Lhasa and his court and had them teach Chinese Buddhism in Tibet. The instructor was candid that "invited" is the polite version and that force was involved. Moheyan arrives this way.

### Background: the crisis of Chinese Buddhism
Set in roughly the sixth to eighth centuries. Two problems:

**Distance from the Buddha.** By then there was no direct access to the Buddha's teaching. Nobody could go and listen. People understood themselves to be living in the final age of the Dharma, which he gave in Chinese as *mofa*: hard to encounter the Dharma, hard to practice, and nearly impossible to attain awakening.

**Inconsistency among texts.** Many texts arrived in China from India and Inner Asia in Sanskrit and other languages, and Chinese translation projects rendered them into Chinese. The result was contradiction. One text says the best practice is reciting sutras. Another says recitation alone will never produce awakening and only meditation will, and then multiple meditation methods each claim to be the best. The instructor's analogy: if an instructor assigns many readings and says every one is essential and you fail without it, you get lost about which one actually matters.

**The response: classification of teachings.** Schools organized the canon hierarchically. Tiantai placed the Lotus Sutra as the ultimate teaching. Huayan placed the Flower Garland Sutra at the top. He explicitly said this material would not be tested.

### The buddha-nature dispute
He named this the most important dispute: tathagatagarbha, buddha-nature. The question is philosophical: does every being possess buddha-nature?

His definition by analogy, built with the class: buddha-nature is the main cause, the seed. To grow a tree you need soil, weather, sunshine and water, but without a seed there is no tree. Buddha-nature is the seed of becoming a buddha. Without it, no amount of practice produces buddhahood.

He then turned the question on the class: what kind of person would not qualify to possess buddha-nature? Answers included violent people. One student argued everyone has it, but bad actions create obstacles to the seed's growth. He pressed with Adolf Hitler as the test case. Most hands went up for yes; some students declined to answer or hoped the answer was no. The reasons offered against: such people caused enormous suffering and murdered many, so how could they qualify; and if they can practice and attain awakening, how do they make amends for their crimes, which offends the idea of fairness when good people suffer.

His point was not to settle it. The question itself registers social concerns: fairness, the justice of law, universal principle. And the position that everyone has buddha-nature says something about religion operating beyond social law.

### Pure Land and Chan
He classed Tiantai and Huayan as scholastic, meaning focused on texts, and set them against two schools that are not.

**Pure Land.** Reasoning in the voice of the practitioner: this is a degenerate age, I cannot attain awakening by my own practice, I have committed many wrongs, so I rely on Amitabha and aim for rebirth in Sukhavati, the Land of Bliss. No study. Chant the name, recite, pray. He compared it to Christian prayer to God.

**Chan.** Emphasizes a sutra and its treatises, and sudden, abrupt awakening. The sutra title is unintelligible in the recording.

The contrast he wanted: Chan relies on yourself, Pure Land relies on another. He gave the East Asian Buddhist terms, Japanese: *tariki*, other power, for Pure Land, and *jiriki*, self power, for Chan.

### Brief history of Chan
Three phases as given, which he said would not be tested:

- Seventh century: formative period, teaching about meditation, multiple factions, no single identity as "Chan."
- Eighth to tenth centuries: development of lineage and genealogy.
- Eleventh century onward: construction of Chan as a sect. He named a golden age spanning the seventh to tenth centuries.

He then asked whether this trajectory resembles another tradition. The answer he was after was tantric Buddhism. Tantric practice emerges in India in the middle of the seventh century; Chan emerges in the seventh to eighth; both have a high development period from the eighth to tenth centuries. Tantric Buddhism reaches its peak in India in the thirteenth century and then declines with the Islamic invasions. Chan peaks in the tenth century and lasts into the thirteenth and fourteenth, then declines, not disappearing but becoming one school among many rather than the dominant one. Two traditions beginning in different places with the same shape of timeline.

A student's earlier guess of Mahayana was corrected: Mahayana starts much earlier, around the first century BCE or first century CE.

### Chan's own account: the flower
Having described Chan from outside, he turned to how Chan describes itself, and the answer is a myth. He connected it to a pattern the class had already met: the myth of how the tsenpos came down to earth. The function in both cases is legitimacy. The first stage of building legitimacy in human society is to derive it from heaven.

The story: at a large gathering, the Buddha picked up a flower and held it in silence, showing it to everyone. His assembled disciples looked and understood nothing. Only Mahakashyapa saw it and smiled. The Buddha nodded and confirmed that Mahakashyapa had received the transmission of wisdom.

He then made the class argue about whether he could tell them what Mahakashyapa understood. Students said he could not. He pushed back: if it is a myth, a myth is made by human beings, and he is a human being, so why not? The useful distinction he drew out: either he does not know, which would mean he has not realized it, or he knows and cannot express it, which is not a limit of capacity but a lack of any instrument to deliver it. A student landed on the second: even if Mahakashyapa told everyone exactly what he experienced, it would not transfer, because it is an inexpressible experience that language, gesture and posture cannot carry.

What the myth establishes, in his summary:

1. Ultimate authority. The transmission comes from the Buddha himself, which qualifies and authorizes the Chan tradition.
2. Chan does not rely on words, language, speech or discourse. The Buddha said nothing, Mahakashyapa said nothing and only smiled, and the Buddha certified it immediately.

He stressed how sharply this cuts against the rest of Buddhism, where the canon is a compilation of discourse and the whole Chinese enterprise was translation, which means words. Chan says no words can convey the real Dharma, only something else, the flower.

### The lineage (started, cut off by time)
With two minutes left he began the Chan lineage of patriarchs, Indian section first:

- First: Mahakashyapa.
- Second: Ananda, the disciple whose hearing is the "I" in "thus have I heard" at the opening of sutras.
- Ashvaghosha, the supposed author of the *Buddhacarita*, the Buddha's life story.
- Nagarjuna, whom he called extremely important, mentioned alongside Asanga.
- Twenty-eighth and last of the Indian patriarchs: Bodhidharma, who is also the first patriarch in China.

Class ended here. The debate itself is next session.

## Key people, places and terms

- **Trisong Detsen** (transcribed variously as Chusong Diu-Tzen, Jesus, Justin Newton). Tsenpo enthroned 756, builder of Samye, conqueror of Dunhuang, host of the debate.
- **Samye** (Sanyei, Sanyam, Zion, Sun Yeh). Tibet's first Buddhist monastery, around 779, completed in the 780s, in the Tsangpo valley. Layout based on the Indian monastic complex of Odantapuri in Bihar.
- **Padmasambhava** (Panama San Bava). Tantric adept from the Swat valley in modern Pakistan, invited to subdue local spirits, bestower of the empowerment at Samye.
- **Shantarakshita** (Shantan Ushida). Indian abbot and philosopher, invited from Nepal, ordained the first Tibetan monks; died before the debate.
- **Kamalashila.** Shantarakshita's student, the Indian representative at the debate. Not named in this session's audio; the instructor stopped before reaching him.
- **Moheyan** (Mohoyin, Mark Mohoyin). Chinese Chan master who had been teaching in Dunhuang. The name transcribes Sanskrit "Mahayana." Tibetan *hwa shang ma ha yan*.
- **Yeshe Tsogyel.** The consort present at the empowerment. Audio garbled; identified from Kapstein.
- **Ba Selnang.** The nobleman sent to Nepal for Shantarakshita and to China for Chinese teachers. Not named in class, but he is the link that brings Chan to Tibet.
- **Reverend Kim.** The Korean Zen teacher Selnang met in China. This is the "little bit of Buddhism from Korea" the instructor mentioned in passing.
- **Dunhuang** (Dong Hwang, Gong Kong, Zhongkang). Silk Road city, economic and cultural hub, Buddhist center with monasteries and libraries, conquered in the 780s and held for roughly three-quarters of a century.
- **Lhasa** (Lassam). Where the Chinese monks were brought to teach.
- **Mahakashyapa** (Kashaka, Keshava, Hashaba). Recipient of the wordless flower transmission, first Chan patriarch.
- **Ananda.** Second patriarch, the hearer behind "thus have I heard."
- **Ashvaghosha.** Author of the *Buddhacarita*.
- **Nagarjuna.** Founder of the Middle Way (Madhyamaka, Tibetan *uma*), roughly second century.
- **Bodhidharma.** Twenty-eighth Indian patriarch, first Chinese patriarch.
- **Queen Maya** (Mayna). The Buddha's mother, died seven days after his birth.
- **tathagatagarbha** (Vekata Gaba). Buddha-nature, the seed or main cause of buddhahood.
- **mofa** (Mokfa). The final or degenerate age of the Dharma.
- **Sukhavati.** Amitabha's Land of Bliss, Tibetan *dewachen*.
- **tariki / jiriki.** Other power and self power.
- **Tiantai** (Tien Thai) **and Huayan** (Huan Yuan). Chinese schools that ranked the canon, placing the Lotus Sutra and the Flower Garland Sutra respectively at the top.
- **Mount Meru.** Axis mundi at the center of Samye's cosmological map, with four outlying continents in the four cardinal directions.

Note on sourcing: Mahakashyapa, Ananda, Ashvaghosha, Bodhidharma, the *Buddhacarita*, Tiantai, Huayan, *mofa*, *tariki* and *jiriki* do not appear in the assigned readings. They are lecture-only material, and the instructor twice said the Chinese Buddhist background would not be tested.

## Connections to the readings

**van Schaik, chapter on the Holy Buddhist Empire, 700 to 797.** The section titled "The Great Debate" is the direct assignment for this lecture. The instructor's complaint that students had not read it carefully points here. Key content the lecture presupposed:

- Ba Selnang's journey to China, his meeting with Reverend Kim, and the resulting invitation of Zen teachers to Tibet.
- Moheyan teaching in Dunhuang, "the Silk Route city conquered by Trisong Detsen in the 780s."
- The real doctrinal issue: both sides were Mahayana and agreed on emptiness, "form is emptiness, and emptiness is form," from the Heart Sutra. They disagreed on how that insight is realized. The Indian side required meditation combined with rational analysis and ethical conduct, summarized in the Six Perfections: giving, morality, patience, energy, meditation and wisdom. The Zen side held that recognizing the nature of mind made the Perfections dispensable.
- The escalation before the debate: self-mutilation among the Chinese faction, a teacher setting fire to his own head, and about thirty adherents arriving armed with knives and threatening to kill the Indian teachers before collective suicide.
- The debate's staging in one of the smaller temples at Samye, the tsenpo enthroned in the middle with Moheyan on his right and Kamalashila on his left.
- Moheyan's argument: only stopping ordinary thought ends samsara, and virtuous and sinful actions are both like clouds, black and white, both blocking the sun. Kamalashila's: wisdom comes through analytical insight, and abandoning analysis cuts wisdom at the root, leaving a state like fainting, "a practice of stupidity."
- Victory to the Indian side, and the consequence that scriptures from India were preferred over those from China.
- The scholarly caveats, which bear directly on the lecture's methods half: this is the Tibetan version; some ancient manuscripts show Moheyan held a more nuanced view; a Chinese version ends with the tsenpo blessing the Chinese teachers; and modern scholars have questioned whether a debate happened at all rather than a series of discussions and literary exchanges. Van Schaik's line is that "you can't keep a good story down," and that the narrative justified the position Tibetan Buddhism took.

**van Schaik on Samye's fabric**, for the architectural and spatial history options. The three-storeyed main building is said to have had its first storey in the Indian style, the second Chinese, the third Khotanese. The lecture mentioned two sources, Indic and Chinese; the reading adds the Khotanese third. Four temples in the four directions, a boundary wall, Mount Meru at the center. Note also the pillar just left of the main temple entrance recording the oath to protect Buddhism, with copies sent from Zhangzhung in the west to Amdo in the northeast. That pillar is a perfect object for the material-history approach the lecture described.

**van Schaik on the translation project.** After the debate, India became the source. Teachers from India and Nepal worked with Tibetans who had learned Sanskrit, building a canon that filled some three hundred volumes when collected centuries later, called among the greatest achievements of the medieval world. This is the state-sponsored, decades-long project the instructor described.

**van Schaik on Dunhuang.** The eleven-year siege, the murdered governor, the surrender terms. Then the transformation of daily life under Tibetan rule: land apportionment, taxation, contracts and letters all changed, Tibetan became the language of government, law and business, and dating shifted from Chinese reign years to the twelve-year animal cycle. Tibetans held the highest posts, Chinese inhabitants wore Tibetan clothes and learned the Tibetan alphabet, and Tibetans in the lowest government positions outranked Chinese in the highest. The Tang annals line about putting on Chinese clothes once a year to worship ancestors and weeping as they put them away. This is the background to why a Chinese Chan master ended up available to a Tibetan court.

**Kapstein, chapter three.** The short account of the debate, which is more skeptical than van Schaik's. Moheyan "attracted a following that included members of the royal family." The event is called "a debate or discussion," sources differ on who won, and it "may well have been in effect a ritual performance that ended in polite accolades for both sides." Crucially, although later historians insist Trisong Detsen banned Chan, "the evidence suggests that, on the contrary, it continued to thrive in parts of the Tibetan empire, perhaps even with some measure of imperial support." Kapstein also dates Samye to around 779 and notes Shantarakshita ordained the first Tibetan monks officially recognized by the court, with the qualification that some Tibetans may have been ordained earlier into the Khotanese sangha established by the princess of Jincheng.

**Kapstein on the economics of the sangha.** The Testament of Ba passage listing what the court assigned: 150 households as appanages of the religious community, and annual allocations of barley, robes, butter, a horse, paper, ink and salt, with separate provision for the twenty-five hermits of Chimpu. If you want the material history of Samye that the lecture proposed, this is where the evidence is.

**Kapstein on Nagarjuna and buddha-nature.** Nagarjuna as the famed dialectician of the Middle Way, and later Tibetan debate over whether buddha-nature or emptiness is the definitive Mahayana doctrine, with the resolving position that buddha-nature is a way of speaking of emptiness. The lecture's buddha-nature dispute recurs inside Tibetan intellectual history.

**Kapstein on the Ganges.** The memorial stele on the banks of the Ganges is attributed to Songtsen Gampo's troops, described as "a tradition that cannot be dismissed out of hand," alongside the Tibetan rescue of a stranded Tang mission that reached Tirabhukti in northern Bihar. The lecture attributed a Ganges marker to Trisong Detsen while explicitly flagging the absence of supporting material. Treat this as unverified and do not use it in a paper without the attribution problem stated.

**The Dunhuang library cave article.** Relevant to the lecture's claim that Dunhuang accumulated books and monks from everywhere. The article argues Mogao Cave 17 was not a waste repository but a book repository, a Buddhist library, and it works from Stein's records of "regular library bundles" of Chinese and Tibetan texts to reconstruct the cave's original state. The manuscripts sealed there at the end of the tenth century are, as van Schaik notes, the earliest extant Tibetan documents anywhere. They are also where the evidence for Moheyan's "more nuanced view" comes from.

**UNC, Evaluating Print Sources.** The closest handout match to the lecture's methods half. It asks whether an author is biased and whether there is an agenda in the selection of data or manipulation of evidence, then insists that "finding a bias does not necessarily mean you should discard the book. Take it in stride and use it," and ends with the point that judgments "must make from your own perspective." That is the lecture's argument in handout form: identify the writer's perspective, do not throw the source out for having one, and own your own.

## Writing and reading-skills guidance

1. **Do not jump to the answer.** Given a prompt, stop and ask what the question is for and how you will design your analysis of it. This was stage one of the semester plan and it still applies.
2. **Choose a perspective before you write.** Political, military, economic, religious, spatial. Decide which one, and be explicit about it in your own head before composing.
3. **Hold that perspective.** The stated benefit is consistency: you will not drift from one frame to another mid-paper, and your logic and method stay coherent.
4. **Read for the writer's perspective, not just the content.** Stay cautious about the angle from which an author approaches a topic. Van Schaik and Kapstein on the same debate is the obvious test case: one narrates the story and then undercuts it, the other leads with the skepticism and reverses the standard claim about Chan being banned.
5. **Treat "the facts" as a limit, not a goal.** You cannot recover all the factors, so write from an acknowledged vantage point rather than claiming total reconstruction.
6. **Pick your object and then pick your genre.** Samye alone supports material, architectural, spatial, dynastic and Buddhist histories, and they are genuinely different papers from the same evidence. The instructor's comment that students had read only enough to write their papers is a hint that this is also a warning about thin reading producing thin topics.
