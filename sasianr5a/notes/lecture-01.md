## Summary
This first session (Week 1, "Course Introduction" in the syllabus) was almost entirely about logistics. No Tibetan history was taught yet. The instructor, Laurence Zhou, introduced the readings: Sam van Schaik's *Tibet: A History* is the primary text, but the class will only cover roughly its first 5 or 6 chapters, and Matthew Kapstein's *The Tibetans* was uploaded as a supplement. ### General attitude toward the rules
- Taking the course is your choice. If you take it, you follow the rules, and the instructor "will not compromise for any reason."
- The course is **not curved**.
- Attitude and effort count. Students who aren't native English speakers or who struggle with writing can still earn a high grade through full attendance, discipline, and real effort on the assignments.
- You may disagree with the instructor's views on history, culture, or religion, in class and in your assignments, as long as your disagreement is reasoned rather than emotional.
- If after two or three weeks you find you don't like the course, you can drop it.

### Attendance (part of the 15%)
- Up to **two absences** are allowed without explanation.
- Any further absence needs **official written documentation**, such as a medical note. These do **not** count as valid reasons: feeling unwell without documentation, preparing for exams (a student once cited a midterm), visiting family, or similar. The instructor faces many students with excuses and cannot make exceptions for each one.
- Something was said about full attendance earning "an extra point" toward the final grade. The syllabus doesn't mention this, so treat it as unconfirmed.
- **Sign-in sheet:** a sheet with the date is passed around each class. Sign **only your own name**. Signing for someone else, even if they are sitting in the room, is **academic misconduct**. The syllabus adds that the attendance record for that class is then invalidated. The instructor keeps all sheets until the end of the semester, and you can ask to review your own record.
- **Berkeley Time:** class officially starts 10 minutes after the scheduled 11:00 a.m. start. (The transcript says "8am" at one point, but the syllabus lists Mon/Wed/Fri 11:00–11:59 a.m. in 2066 Valley Life Sciences.) Some grace is given to students crossing campus. Arriving **more than 15 minutes late** (11:16 or later) is a problem. The syllabus says you will be **marked absent**. The room has one entrance, so late arrivals disrupt the instructor and other students.
- The instructor warned that students tend to arrive later and later as the semester goes on. Repeated lateness will hurt your attendance grade.

### Participation (part of the 15%)
- Participation means active, meaningful engagement: answering the instructor's questions, joining discussions, and giving presentations.
- **Bring questions to class.** After reading and reflecting on the assigned material, come with questions. Basic or "naive" questions are fine. A question can answer your own concern and help others understand too.
- The instructor will also ask questions to check whether you actually did the reading. The syllabus calls these "in-class oral examinations based on the readings."
- There will be small-group discussions on specific topics.
- Every student gives an end-of-semester presentation (see Final Essay).
- Students who consistently don't participate get **zero** for participation. The instructor admitted this sounds subjective but said everyone in the room can tell who is actually engaged.
- **On AI and cheating:** some past students turned in "perfect papers" but couldn't explain their own topic when presenting, which suggests the papers came from AI or another person. The instructor has worked in this field for 10 to 15 years, will notice, and said AI tools produce wrong information in this field. The syllabus lists unauthorized AI use (ChatGPT, Gemini, other LLMs) under a zero-tolerance policy: a failing grade for the whole course and a report to the Center for Student Conduct.
- Attendance and participation are recorded **outside bCourses**. Grades shown on bCourses are for reference only. **CalCentral** holds the official final grade. Don't ask the instructor about the bCourses grade display.

### Weekly reading responses (40%)
- This is the largest part of the grade. Responses start **next week**, not this week.
- Each week a prompt is posted on bCourses under **Assignments**. The instructor aims to post it Thursday afternoon. The syllabus says "by Thursday at 11:59 p.m." The prompt gives the theme, requirements (such as word count), and deadline. Example given: a question asking your impression of Tibet from the week's van Schaik reading (partly garbled).
- **Submit early.** Give yourself at least 20 minutes, especially the first time. Students who click submit right at the deadline (the example was 7 p.m.) run into system problems and end up late.
- **Late work gets zero credit.** The instructor may still read and comment on a late paper, but it earns no points. One student who submitted one day late in the first week got comments and no credit.
- **Format:** Microsoft Word (.doc or .docx), 12-point font, **1.5 line spacing** (not double spacing, which the instructor dislikes), standard margins. **No Google Docs links or PDFs.** Google Docs don't enter the bCourses system, and PDFs can't take comments. The only exception is the Diagnostic Essay (see below).
- Feedback comes as margin comments and/or overall comments. **Read them.** Many students only look at the score. Use the comments to adjust your next response.

### Midterm essay (20%: draft 10%, diagnostic version 5%, polished version 5%)
- **Purpose:** to assess (a) your understanding of the assigned readings, (b) your ability to do close reading and engage critically with texts, and (c) the academic writing skills you built in the first half of the course. It covers only first-half material. You don't need to know everything or have fully developed writing skills yet.
- **Topic:** drawn **only** from course readings discussed before the midterm. Additional readings or material from after the midterm are not permitted (the garbled "after-return-out-of-return" passage).
- **Type:** a research-based analytical paper, **not a summary** of lectures, readings, or class discussion. Make your own points and arguments: agree, disagree, criticize. The syllabus says it should present "a new topic or a new analytical perspective."
- **Length:** at least **1,000 words**, same format as the reading responses. (A garbled remark about "4.5 A / 4.5 B" and 1,500 words may have been comparing the midterm and final lengths. It is unintelligible.)
- **Structure:** title, introduction, clear central argument (or set of arguments), supporting evidence, conclusion. The syllabus also requires a **bibliography**. The instructor promised to teach each of these parts in later classes.
- **Draft:** the core component. Due **Fri. Oct. 23 at 7:00 a.m.**
- **Diagnostic version:** pair up with a classmate, swap drafts, and edit your partner's paper. This switches you from writer to editor and gives you a new perspective on your own work. Comment on what makes the paper good or promising and where it falls short (grammar problems, non-academic style). The syllabus specifies Google Docs for this step (share the link, give the instructor editing access). Comments should address argument and structure, challenge unclear sections, and cover language and academic conventions. The grade depends on both the quality and quantity of your comments. Due **Wed. Oct. 28 at 7:00 a.m.**
- **Polished version:** weigh your peer's comments and the instructor's comments, make your own decisions, and revise into a final version. You don't have to accept every suggestion, but revisions must be visible. Minimal revision earns zero for this part. Due **Mon. Nov. 2 at 7:00 a.m.**
- This sequence imitates publishing in an academic journal: submit, get editor comments, revise, resubmit.

### Final essay (25%: draft 10%, presentation 10%, polished version 5%)
- Same process as the midterm, but at least **1,500 words**. The syllabus says it must rely **only on assigned course materials** (no outside sources) and may approach (Imperial) Tibet from any perspective developed in the course.
- **Draft** due **Fri. Nov. 27 at midnight**.
- **Presentation:** based on your draft, with PowerPoint slides, standing at the front of the class. Expect about **7 minutes per student**, roughly 5 minutes of talk plus Q&A, depending on class size. **Time control matters**: some students run too short, others too long. In academia, organizers set presentation lengths and scholars must fit them. Classmates and the instructor will ask questions you can't prepare for, which tests whether you can defend your argument. You don't need to know every answer. The skill is handling the questions.
- The instructor noted that quiet students often get very nervous and let their emotions distract them from the topic. To help, the instructor will set aside a couple of classes for practice. Presentations are scheduled for Nov. 30, Dec. 2, and Dec. 4. Missing your assigned slot earns zero for the presentation.
- Presentation skills are also useful for job hunting. If you hate presenting, treat this as practice.
- **Polished version:** revise using the instructor's comments and the questions raised during your presentation. Due **Fri. Dec. 18 at noon**.
- There is no midterm or final exam. The essays replace exams so you have less pressure during exam periods.

## Writing and reading-skills guidance
- Read all assigned material before class, reflect on it, and bring questions. Expect to be quizzed out loud on the readings.
- Essays are **analytical and research-based**, never a summary of the lectures or readings. Take a position: agree, disagree, criticize.
- Required structure: title, introduction, clear central argument(s), supporting evidence, conclusion (plus a bibliography, per the syllabus). Each part will be taught in later classes.
- Peer editing trains you to see your own writing the way an editor would.
- Revision is a judgment call. Weigh peer and instructor comments and decide what to change, but your changes must be visible.
- Read the instructor's comments on every response and apply them to the next one.
- Presentations: know your argument well enough to explain it without a script, keep to time, and practice answering questions you didn't prepare for.
- Disagreement is welcome in writing if it is reasoned.
