## Summary
Two instructors make the case that startups fail for lack of customers rather than lack of
technology, then teach the mechanics of customer discovery. The first half is evidence and
cautionary tales: CB Insights failure data, a hospital-infection product that was technically
perfect and commercially dead, Google's high-altitude internet balloon, and two success
stories where teams found real markets by leaving the building. The second half is
operational: the Business Model Canvas with customer segments and value propositions as the
only two blocks that matter right now, how to find and book interviews, how to run one
without pitching, and how to build an ecosystem map distinguishing users, buyers, decision
makers, influencers, saboteurs and early evangelists. Students owe 20 interviews, with five
to ten by the next class, plus a drafted ecosystem map.

## Lecture notes

### Why startups die
Funding is optional. The instructor says both her last two companies were funded off sales,
not venture capital, and describes the fundraising treadmill as a miserable way to live.
CB Insights (recommended for its newsletters and infographics) analyzed 111 startups; the
leading cause of death was running out of cash and failing to raise, which she reads as a
downstream symptom of not solving a problem anyone will pay for. The core claim: there are
no facts inside the building.

### The hospital infection product
A well-known figure on campus [name not intelligible in the recording] spent six years and
his family's life savings building a product for hospital-acquired infection. Two patents,
two or three successful clinical trials, worked across settings, technically perfect. The
company folded within a couple of months of launch. Hospitals agreed the problem was real
but said they had nine other problems to solve first. Same answer at large and small
hospitals across the US. A few weeks of customer conversations up front would have revealed
it.

### Google's balloon
A high-altitude balloon designed to deliver Wi-Fi to deserts, mountains and other areas
without infrastructure. Google spent nine years and $125 million (the instructor later
misstates this as $25 million) and staffed it with top people; it shut down within six
months of launch. Nobody would pay: governments said private industry should fund it,
private industry said governments should, and the pattern repeated worldwide. Google
reportedly changed its process after the Alphabet reorganization.

### Team 1050 (UC Santa Cruz)
Video case study. The team started believing pharmaceutical companies would pay them to
rapidly engineer microbes that produce drugs. First realization in Newark: companies are not
customers, people with job titles are. Their real customer was the process chemist, not the
biologist. Second realization: nobody wanted microbes, which were hard to work with and hard
to purify. A meeting with a scientist in the biocatalysis group at Merck opened a new segment
with pain points their technology could address. They then pivoted, re-ran the canvas in
Boston, and interviewed process chemistry leads including Francis Gosselin at Genentech
(pricing-model pain points) and a Richard Du[?] on short timelines. Outcome: two letters of
intent, $15 million raised before graduation, a successful proof of concept, then over $100
million more. The team had no biotech network and found interviews by standing outside
company buildings with clipboards and asking politely.

Two takeaways the instructor pulls out explicitly:
1. Industries are not customers. Companies are not customers. Customers are people with job
   titles. Get to that specificity fast so experiments are testable.
2. You do not need to be an expert or a PhD. These were undergraduates using the tools.

### Business Model Canvas
Nine blocks: key partners, key activities, key resources, cost structure, revenue streams,
customer segments, customer relationships, channels, and value propositions. Only two matter
for this program: customer segments and value propositions. Reframed as who cares, why do
they care, and how much do they care.

The canvas has a logic, not a random layout. Validate the right side first (who cares, why,
how much) to decide whether to build at all. Only then move to what it takes to build it
(resources, patents, manufacturing). The bottom row weighs cost against willingness to pay.
Evidence across every block is what investors want; pre-sales are the ultimate validation,
and documented evidence for who cares, why and how much is what she calls product-market fit.

### Testing the customer, not the product
A second video satirizes a founder who answers every question with the same sentence about a
chemical isomer that ruptures carbon bonds and wraps volatile compounds in a nanotube
coating, with no target customer and no articulated value. The point: customers do not care
that you spent nine years. They care about their own problems.

### The Doudna lab oil pivot
Jennifer Doudna, CRISPR co-discoverer and Nobel laureate, regularly sends projects to a class
the instructor teaches. A grad student, Raissa[?], and her team, all from Brazil, used CRISPR
to engineer high-quality oils and hypothesized cosmetic companies would buy, on the theory
that every woman would want Nobel-Prize-winning oil on her face. Requirement at the time was
15 interviews in 10 days; she got roughly 10 in a day and a half. All ten cosmetic companies
said no: no benefit, happy with existing solutions. Because she listened well, one
interviewee mentioned a friend with a fish farm desperate for high-quality oils for feed. She
drove to the farm, the farmer offered cash for everything she could produce, and that became
her first sale. Cosmetics to fish feed, found by following the evidence somewhere unexpected.

### Value proposition cheat sheet
Customers generally care about one of four things: making money, saving money, saving time,
saving lives, and making people happy (the first three are easy to recall, the fourth is
squishy). For the emotional category, Maslow's hierarchy of needs is offered as a tool:
functional needs first, then belonging and reduced anxiety, then self-transcendence. In a
business context this matters because everyone has a boss, nobody wants to anger their boss,
and reducing anxiety or improving promotion odds are real buying triggers.

Illustration: a basic work truck versus a Cybertruck. Same product category, radically
different value propositions. The first customer needs to move goods from A to B, a purely
functional job. The second is buying status and visibility. For your first ten customers,
pick the segment with both the greatest need and the greatest ability to pay.

### Mosquito bite versus shark bite
A shark-bite problem is one you drop everything to solve. A mosquito bite is annoying but not
worth paying for. The example given: an earthquake team whose app people would download but
not pay for, where insurance modelers losing tens of millions of dollars were the shark-bite
segment. Students will be pushed to name their shark-bite segment.

### Expect to be wrong
Quoting Steve Blank: no business model canvas survives first contact with customers (the
transcriber renders "customers" as "classroom"). A team of PhDs certain their lab mates
wanted their product listed five segments they expected to throw money at them. After 16
interviews in a week, every initial segment turned out not to have the problem. They did
discover different problems the same product could solve, and spent their next 100 interviews
on those. Being wrong the first time is healthy and means you are closer to the truth.

### In-class exercise: segments and value props
Teams spent roughly 15 minutes naming their top three customer segments and why each would
care, using sticky notes or slides. One recruiting-tool team worked through whether to target
recruiters only or also recruiting managers and leadership who hold budget, landing on value
props around saving time on repetitive recruiting work, zero setup, privacy, and personalized
output with no new tool to learn.

### Protein powder warm-up
Shouted-out customer segments: athletes, healthy people, gym goers, pregnant women, people
with nutrient deficiencies. Then the value propositions for each: pregnant women have
elevated protein requirements they cannot meet through diet alone; gym goers want muscle
gain, harder training and the feeling of visible progress, which he flags as an emotional
value. He then builds a persona: Larry, 23, works out a few times a week, wants muscle, does
not cook, busy schedule, maximizing protein on the go. The lesson is specificity about who
the customer is and what value they derive.

### Why 20 interviews and not five
A Dalmatian illusion image. Shown sparsely, students guess a Dalmatian, food under a
microscope, modern art, ink, a map. Shown with far more data points, the scene resolves: the
dog, a bench, a tree, water. More interviews fill in the picture of the market. Five or ten
data points leave you guessing.

### Finding people to interview
Suggested channels from the class and instructor: conferences and expos (the Bay Area has a
constant supply in San Francisco and Berkeley), industry nights, networking and happy hours
(under-21 students can usually still attend without drinking), LinkedIn, the Berkeley alumni
portal, SF Tech Week, malls, cafes, coffee shops, construction sites, festivals, and local
open-mic nights for a music-focused team. Apollo.io is recommended for lead generation, with
a free student trial, filterable by niche, company and region. For past or future conferences,
pull the speaker or attendee list off the website and use "I enjoyed your talk on X" or "I
missed it and want to learn more" as an opener; upcoming speakers are likelier to respond
because they are already willing to talk to a room. ChatGPT can surface forums and groups
where customers already talk: one team found local type 1 diabetes Facebook groups that ran
weekly Bay Area hikes, joined a hike, and interviewed seven or eight patients plus their
partners and friends about blood sugar monitoring.

Field-visit examples: a team converting banana waste into textiles that flew to a farm in
Thailand and only grasped the scale of the problem on site; a data-center cooling team that
found corporate offices closed on weekends and pivoted to interviewing breweries and wineries
around Berkeley about energy consumption; a toddler behavioral-tracking team whose founder
volunteered at Berkeley daycares and preschools to talk to parents at pickup; and Super
Spatial, an AR-for-historical-sites team and a competition first-place winner, who interviewed
tourists on location. Take photos and include them in final presentations.

### How to get the interview
Shoot your shot (Michael Scott quoting Wayne Gretzky). Lead with your student card, since
people like helping students. Research the person and reference something specific they
posted or a team they lead. Do not mention your technology. Follow up if there is no reply.
Use mutual connections and shared Berkeley affiliation as warm intros, including messaging a
shared second-degree connection for an introduction. Ask for 10 or 15 minutes even though the
conversation will likely run 30 to 60; people say yes to small asks. At companies, skip the
CEO and target a mid-level manager or senior individual contributor who sits closer to the
problem. Keep the email short: student, program, research project, would value your expert
opinion, do you have 15 minutes Wednesday at 2, Zoom or in person. AI can draft from a
LinkedIn profile, but AI-sounding emails are increasingly recognizable, so review before
sending.

### How to run the interview
Thank them (this is volunteered time, unpaid), introduce yourself and your team, frame it as
a research project. Do not lead with your technology, your solution or even the problem you
think you are solving. If they press, defer it to the end or to a follow-up call. Open with
roughly five broad questions and narrow toward the hypotheses on your canvas. Be an active
listener: do not debate, do not correct them, affirm their input. Watch body language, tone
and emotion and note where it shifts.

Question patterns given: tell me about the last time X happened; can you explain the problem;
help me understand why you chose this; why is this happening; what have you done to fix it so
far; what worked and what did not; what is your greatest challenge in fixing it; how often
does it happen; when was the last time; how much does this problem cost you; how much time do
you spend on it now; what were you doing yesterday; describe a time when this issue came up;
what were the downstream consequences when it went wrong. Use why, how, when, what. Avoid
leading questions (his example of what not to do: "don't you think J. Cole is the best
rapper out there").

Close with three questions every time:
1. Is there anything important I did not ask?
2. May I reach out if I have other questions? (also the way to capture contact details from
   someone met at a mall or event)
3. Do you know other people who would be willing to talk to me? This is the easiest and most
   organic route to more interviews.

Take notes, which are a course requirement and the raw material for pattern-finding. AI
transcribers or a simple recording plus later transcription are fine. With a teammate, run
some interviews in pairs so one asks and one takes notes.

### Ecosystem mapping
Beyond the user there is a cast of people who shape the purchase:
- Decision maker: makes the final call
- Economic buyer: signs the check
- Influencer: sways the decision without controlling it
- End user: actually uses the product
- Saboteur: hurt by your implementation, content with the status quo, resistant to change
- Early evangelist: early adopter and advocate, first in line for the beta
- Others: regulators, lawmakers, insurers, standards bodies

Saboteurs exist even with no competing product on the market; the competitor may simply be
"we are fine as we are."

Worked example, an ed-tech company (Scientific Learning) selling online phonics and
vocabulary software for elementary school:
- Student: end user
- Teacher: influencer if invested in student outcomes and test results; saboteur if content
  with the projector or flashcards and skeptical that software or AI teaches better
- Principal: economic buyer
- Superintendent: economic buyer at district scale
Parents who are invested in a student's success also act as influencers.

Second worked example, an invisible-aligner product. Product sits at the center. End user
Jenna wants straight teeth without metal in her mouth, exchanging money for that value. Her
fiancé wants a better smile for the wedding, an influence exchange rather than a money one.
Influencers include TV segments and dental hygienists on TikTok and Instagram, sponsored or
not. Orthodontists sat as potential saboteurs 15 years ago when they lacked the capability and
feared losing business, and now act more as subscribers and recommenders. Health and dental
insurers are also in the system.

Value flows on the map are labeled in three kinds: value, money, and influence.

Why it matters: it becomes your marketing plan, showing who your customer listens to and
where to spend. A team working on MRI technology, an I-Corps winner, did 100 interviews
believing radiologists sat at the center of all hospital decisions. The map changed
significantly: radiologists held a narrow role, and a different chain of command ran before
anything reached them. The person you think decides may have three people between them and
the actual decision.

### Closing exercise
Roughly 17 to 20 minutes to draft an ecosystem map on posters and sticky notes or in Google
Slides, followed by a few teams sharing. The recruiting team mapped CFO as economic buyer,
CPO or CHRO plus recruiting managers as decision makers, recruiters as end users with subsets
acting as early evangelists and saboteurs, HR consultants as influencers, and an HR officer
rather than the CEO as the standard setter, on the grounds that a CEO would not know enough
about recruiting to set one. Arrows were color-coded by value, money and influence.

## Writing and reading-skills guidance
No academic writing or close-reading instruction occurred. The nearest analogues, if you want
to carry anything across to R5A work, are three research habits the session does teach:
- Evidence before conclusion. Do not commit to a claim until you have enough data points to
  see the pattern, which is the Dalmatian image argument.
- Interview without leading. Open-ended, non-leading questions that start from "describe the
  last time" rather than "don't you think," plus the closing question "is there anything
  important I did not ask."
- Expect your hypothesis to fail and treat invalidation as progress, not failure.
