## Lecture notes

### The value proposition statement as an elevator pitch

Start plain. One sentence or less describing what you are working on and who you are working on it for. "If you're stuck in an elevator and someone asks you what you do" is the test. Nothing fancy, just a place to start.

Then enhance it through customer discovery. Get more specific on:

- the pain you are relieving
- the time and cost involved, with metrics on what the problem costs the customer now and what savings you can bring
- the customer segment, down to a persona rather than a category

Keep asking why. Aim for a statement the customer can invalidate or say no to. The end state is a quantified value proposition.

### Specifications are not value propositions

The SpongeBob example, from an episode the transcript renders as "All Backliggers" (title garbled, likely "Bummer Vacation" or similar; unintelligible on the recording). SpongeBob's job is flipping burgers at the Krusty Krab. His spatula breaks, he goes shopping, and he is sold a new one on the pitch that it is fancier, more advanced, and has more features.

The class agreed this is a bad value proposition. The reason given: those are technical features. They do not tell the customer what the thing does *for them*. The instructor noted students had already done a good job stripping specifications out of the value proposition box on their Business Model Canvas.

The diagnostic question for any feature: what is that helping the customer do? What does it enable them to do, feel, or take part in?

### Rebuilding the spatula value proposition

The class brainstormed what a better spatula would actually offer. Student answers and the instructor's push for the "so what" behind each:

- **More durable.** It does not break, so it lasts longer.
- **Efficiency.** Pressed on how to measure it: you can flip more patties per shift.
- **Self-turning.** Pressed on what that means: less work for the cook, which becomes an ergonomics claim.
- **Lighter weight.** Easier to use, less ergonomic strain.

The instructor then gave the versions these become after real interviews:

- easier to clean, because current spatulas are hard to clean
- lighter, so less ergonomic strain
- cooler to the touch, because current spatulas get too hot and cause burn accidents

Safety was the one carried onto the canvas, with the hypothesis stated as: our spatula is five times safer than a traditional spatula.

Honest caveat from the instructor: your first value propositions probably are not quantifiable yet. That is expected. But the program wants to see numbers developing over time, and specific detail about the customer's problem where numbers are not available.

### Narrowing the customer segment

"People who make burgers" is a legitimate starting point and a useless segment. It could mean someone cooking dinner in their apartment, someone barbecuing in a backyard, a family at a picnic in the park, a line cook at a fast food chain, or a chef at a high-end restaurant. All different people with different problems.

Customer discovery is how you niche down to who has the bigger problem. After roughly twenty interviews you should be finding that it is a particular type of person who cares enough to pay, and that it is probably not your first guess.

The referenced example: a team that narrowed from all food and beverage manufacturers to those already using alternative proteins, on the reasoning that companies already exploring sustainability and non-meat alternatives were the ones willing to pay.

**The pivot question.** When an interview tells you this is not a problem for them, ask: who do you think does have this problem? The instructor called this one of the best ways to find your next pivot. Cited example from Rhonda's session the previous day: a team working on fish oil started in the medical industry and pivoted mid-interview to fish food after what they heard.

### Case studies of teams that pivoted

**Cruso** (transcript spelling; a launch team and NSF I-Corps team). Started making surfboards from recycled shrimp shell waste, prototyping in garages around Santa Cruz. Customer discovery showed biodegradable boards already existed and this was not a sharp pain for surfers. They pivoted to food and beverage cooling, making packaging material for seafood and refrigerated food. Won significant grant funding and recruited board members including Leonardo DiCaprio and Ashton Kutcher, helped by the peak of interest in sustainability philanthropy.

**Super Spatial.** First cohort of the program. Started with VR/AR glasses targeting museums, on the hypothesis that museums worldwide wanted interactive exhibits. Visits revealed willingness to pay concentrated in more touristy areas, so they pivoted there. Flagged by the instructor as having an excellent storytelling strategy for the final pitch, saved for later in the lecture.

**A legal-tech team.** Started helping lawyers find documents and conversations buried in email, hypothesizing that lawyers wasted too long hunting PDFs and important threads. Interviews showed this was not a paid pain, so they stopped chasing it. The instructor's framing: invalidations matter as much as validations, because they move you toward the right problem.

**We Should Farm** (transcript spelling; vertical farming, went through the launch program in 2017). California has strawberry farms in the Central Valley and Salinas, so fresh strawberries are a couple hours' drive away. Much of the rest of the country is not. On the East Coast, fresh strawberries were hard to get and spoiled quickly after purchase. Customer discovery gave them the supply chain and logistics metrics to prove the case for vertical farming of strawberries so other regions could get access. The instructor recalled a Series B of roughly $150 million in 2024 and another round "a few months ago," putting total raised above $300 million. The figures and round letters in the transcript are garbled and should not be quoted. They had planned to go direct to retail and pivoted several times.

### Storytelling lecture: the photo exercise

Five minutes on the clock. The class described a black and white photograph.

Literal observations from students: four people, in front of or on a staircase, staggered so the leftmost is furthest back and they get progressively closer to the camera. Order left to right was guessed as man, woman, man. They appear to be carrying instrument cases, one medium sized, one slung. Background suggests a city and going underground.

Then the instructor asked people to go beyond the photo and supply a backstory. Answers: they are a band or an orchestra; they are heading to a train station, travelling between venues or back to a hotel; they look confident, so whatever they just played probably went well.

The point: the photo on its own is a flat black and white image. Everything that makes it interesting was supplied by the viewer. In your pitch, you have the same ability to make the audience see the problem the way you see it.

### Why storytelling works

- Stories are how humans engage and connect, and how we remember things over time.
- Raw data and information do not stick. Information tied to a story does.
- The instructor cited a figure of being "22 times more likely to remember facts" when they are tied to a story. The number is stated twice in the recording and once garbled as "two times," so treat the exact figure as approximate.
- Analogy to medicine: pre-med students and doctors memorize terminology through analogies and mnemonics that make no literal sense but attach information to a narrative hook.
- Steve Jobs was invoked as an example of storytelling as an unfair competitive advantage. Out of ten pitches, the two with a real story are the ones remembered.

### What the judges actually want

Explicitly **not** wanted:

- how amazing your product is
- how much revenue you will generate
- heavy technical focus
- slides dense with text

Wanted:

- the problem you are solving and how big it is
- what you learned from your customers
- your emotional journey through customer discovery: the early enthusiasm, the disappointment when nobody agreed the problem was real, the frustration of not landing interviews, the excitement when you finally found a validating customer
- why you personally care about this problem

The instructor's line: it is always less about the technology and more about the problem you are solving.

### The founder journey as story arc

Every story has a recognizable turn of events: a protagonist starts somewhere, goes through conflict, comes out changed. The instructor referenced the standard story shapes (the transcript garbles the author's name, likely Kurt Vonnegut's story shapes) and named examples: man in a hole (positive life, negative turn, recovery, with Hamilton and Black Panther given as examples), boy meets girl for rom-coms, from bad to worse.

Mapped to entrepreneurship:

1. Initial peak of enthusiasm before and at the start of the program.
2. Interviews begin. Customers say this is not a sharp pain for them. Disappointment, struggle, trouble even getting interviews booked. This is the bottom of the arc.
3. Experimenting, pivoting, niching down the customer segment.
4. Eventually product market fit, scale, exit, though the class was told they are all still at the beginning or middle.

You are not graded on reaching product market fit. You are graded on showing you used evidence from your interviews to drive your next step, even if that step is a different problem entirely. Not knowing your customer yet is an acceptable ending as long as you show how you got there.

Reference example: the "my shake" video Rhonda shared the previous day (name garbled in the transcript). A two-minute founder story about an app with a few hundred thousand users, learning in interviews that nobody had a problem big enough to pay for, continuing to interview until they found the right buyers, and eventually being acquired by Google. The details are mangled on the recording; the point is that the story held the room.

### Pro tips for the pitch

**Make it personal.** A past founder named in the transcript as Rishbira Lazada (spelling uncertain) opened every single presentation with a photo of her grandmother. She was building a medical device for COPD patients and her grandmother had the condition. The vulnerability made the audience connect and made the story memorable.

**Strong openers.** "If...", "Imagine...", "Take us back in time...". Or open on a single arresting metric: $17 million, 17,000, 20 seconds. The team Medinas opened on a number.

**Keep it simple.** Your judges are not subject matter experts in your problem space. The instructor showed a slide of real academic thesis titles and their plain-language translations. The example read aloud was unintelligible on the recording, something like "the role of [a gene] in pluripotent..."; the plain version was about muscle cells. The lesson: communicate the problem so everyone in the room gets it.

**Practice.** Run it at least five times. Time yourself. Presentations are cut off at four minutes.

**Delivery.** Speak slowly, enunciate, use pauses for effect, control your breathing, be excited, own the stage. People can tell when you have practiced.

**Appendix slides.** Anything you could not fit into four minutes, including interview detail, goes in an appendix you can jump to during Q&A.

**In Q&A, "I don't know, let me get back to you" is acceptable and better than making something up.** Getting caught fabricating looks far worse than admitting a gap.

### The ten-step pitch formula

Developed by Darren Clips (name uncertain in transcript), the program's CIO, who also teaches some of the classes. Steps one through five, plus the team slide, are the make-or-break parts for a four-minute pitch. Steps seven through ten are for later-stage teams on bigger stages (business model, IP, distribution).

1. **There's a problem.** What does it look like? Make the audience experience it in your first few sentences.
2. **There's a big problem.** How big is it for your customers? This is where the shark bite versus bee sting distinction applies.
3. **There's a solution.** How you address the problem.
4. **There's a great solution.** The sharp value proposition that directly addresses the pain.
5. **It's irresistible to a customer segment that will buy it.** Willingness to pay. Measure this in interviews by asking what they currently pay for to solve the problem, rather than asking willingness to pay directly. If they are already paying for something that is not working, that is your opening.
6. **Your team can do this.** What makes you passionate about this problem and what in your backgrounds makes you the right people.
7. **End with an ask.** Not a fundraising number. This is a lessons-learned pitch, so ask for what gets you to the next stage: connections to subject matter experts, help reaching a different customer segment, technical help building a prototype, guidance on distribution channels or revenue models. Do not ask for an arbitrary $100,000 or $100 million.

### The Medinas sample pitch

Played in class as a model of steps one through five. Founder named as Chloe, a repeat founder now involved in three startups. The company works on hospital capital equipment and surplus. Transcribed from the recording:

> "Hi everyone, we are Medinas, and we are an easy way for healthcare organizations to monetize their surplus medical supplies and equipment. In 2015, my late grandmother was diagnosed with terminal brain cancer, and we had to decide whether or not to bankrupt our family to pay for a surgery that might have only slightly prolonged her life. So you can imagine my shock when I learned about the $700 billion worth of waste the US healthcare system generates every year. 91% of the countries on Earth have a national GDP smaller than that number. If we want to solve this problem, we need to start with the billions of dollars in surplus medical supplies and equipment in perfect, usable condition that's thrown away every single year. For example, the San Francisco hospital's neurology department estimates that it generates $3 million a year in waste. If we can help them monetize that for just 50 cents on the dollar, that's an extra $1.5 million in one department in one hospital. Imagine the lives that can save."

Note: the speaker says "$700 billion" and then misspeaks as "$700 million." The billion figure is the one that matches the GDP comparison.

What the instructor pointed out in it: a clear problem, a personal hook, a metric establishing scale, and a quoted finding from an actual customer interview (the SF hospital neurology department). Medinas later used the same pitch at another competition and won a $1 million check.

## Writing and reading-skills guidance

No close reading, poetry explication, or academic writing instruction appeared. The communication guidance in this session was oral-presentation guidance for a startup pitch. Two pieces of it do transfer to any kind of writing and are worth keeping:

- **Translate jargon for a non-expert reader.** The thesis-title exercise is the same discipline as writing an abstract or an introduction a non-specialist can follow.
- **Lead with a concrete, specific opening rather than an abstract claim.** A metric or a scene earns attention; a category statement does not.
