## Summary
This lecture finished the Songtsen Gampo unit by looking at the Tibetan writing system, which tradition says Tonmi Sambhota created from Indian scripts in the seventh century. The instructor compared the Tibetan alphabet with Sanskrit. Tibetan looks like a simplified version of the Sanskrit system, with fewer vowels and fewer rows of consonants, but it keeps the same arrangement by where each sound is made in the mouth. The instructor then argued that tracing Tibetan only to Sanskrit is one-sided: one vowel per syllable and seven-syllable verse lines also match Chinese, and Tibetan tradition may have played down its debt to China while stressing its link to Buddhist India. The discussion widened to language and ethnic identity, sinicization, and Antonio Gramsci's remark that the "question of language" is always tied to politics and cultural hegemony. The last few minutes began a new unit on primary sources. They introduced Dunhuang as the central hub of the Silk Road, and the Library Cave will be covered next time.

## Lecture notes

### Two frameworks for reading Songtsen Gampo
- The instructor recapped the frames used for Songtsen Gampo so far: a narrow frame (Tibet by itself), a wider frame (Tibet in regional history), and Buddhist history as a separate category of history.
- The unit on Songtsen Gampo is now complete. It covered his life story, rise, military expansion, marriage alliances with neighbors (Nepal, China, and a third state the transcript garbles as "Xiao"), the internal marriage system, his relationship to Buddhism (how far and in what way he embraced it), and finally writing and language.

### Creation of the Tibetan script
- Tradition says Songtsen Gampo sent Tonmi Sambhota to India in the seventh century. Tonmi learned Indian writing there and designed a Tibetan writing system based on it.
- The instructor treated this as the traditional account. Van Schaik likewise calls Tonmi a figure "lost in the misty valleys of legend" but says the story "may contain more than a grain of truth."

### Tibetan alphabet compared with Sanskrit
- **Vowels.** Tibetan has four written vowel signs (i, u, e, o) plus the inherent "a" built into each consonant, so five vowels in all. Sanskrit has short and long pairs (a/ā, i/ī, u/ū) along with e, ai, o, au. Tibetan looks like a simplified version of the Sanskrit set.
- **Consonants by place of articulation.** Sanskrit arranges its consonants in five rows, each defined by where the sound is produced:
  1. Guttural (throat): ka, kha, ga, nga
  2. Palatal (tongue against the palate)
  3. Cerebral/retroflex (tongue curled to the roof of the mouth)
  4. Dental (tongue tip at the root of the teeth)
  5. Labial (lips): pa, pha, ba, ma
- Tibetan has fewer rows (the instructor counted four against Sanskrit's five) but keeps the same organizing principle.
- **Sibilants.** Sanskrit has three sibilants (śa, ṣa, sa). Tibetan has fewer, which is further evidence of simplification.
- Conclusion: Tibetan writing is clearly connected to Sanskrit and follows the same phonetic ordering.

### Why Sanskrit came to count as "holy" in Buddhism
- Many Tibetan Buddhists say Tibetan is a holy language because it comes from Sanskrit.
- The instructor called this ironic. In the Buddha's own time Buddhism avoided Sanskrit, because Sanskrit was the sacred language of Brahmanism and of the elite. Buddhism began as a grassroots movement and taught in vernacular languages (Prakrits) so that ordinary people could understand it.
- Things changed with the rise of Mahayana. Major Mahayana thinkers such as Nagarjuna and Asanga were educated in Sanskrit and used it to compose scriptures and to debate rival religious schools. Sanskrit became the common language of the Buddhist world and gained its "holy" status.
- This Sanskrit was Buddhist Sanskrit, which differs from classical Sanskrit.
- Tibetans stressed that their script came from India because of this Buddhist ideology.

### The Chinese side of the story
- **Syllable structure.** Sanskrit words usually have several vowels. The instructor's examples were *sangha* (two), *iti*, the Sanskrit quotation marker (two), and *sutra* (two). In Tibetan, a small dot (the *tsheg*) separates syllables, and each syllable has exactly one vowel. The instructor said this matches Chinese, where each character is one syllable.
- **Verse form.** A sample Tibetan verse had seven syllables per line. Chinese poetry uses lines of fixed syllable counts (the instructor named five, seven and nine), and the instructor said Tibetan verse is organized and divided in the same way.
- Claim: it is "not scientific" to say Tibetan borrowed only from Sanskrit. It probably also borrowed from Chinese.

### Open questions the instructor posed
1. **Why would Tibetans hide their connection to China?** A student's answer, which the instructor accepted: they identified more with India. The instructor added that they wanted both to build a link to India and to set a boundary against China, so they downplayed how much they had learned from Chinese culture. The instructor called this a big question across all of Tibetan history.
2. **Why did Songtsen Gampo create a new script instead of adopting Sanskrit or Chinese characters?** He supported Buddhism, and Tibetan nobles studied Chinese language, ritual and administration. He also married a Chinese princess. Korea, Japan and Vietnam adopted Chinese characters, and in the instructor's account they began replacing them with their own scripts only in the twentieth century, as nationalism and ethnic identity grew. This ties into scholarly debates on **sinicization**: taking on Chinese characters means part of one's identity is shaped by Chinese culture.

### Language, culture and identity (Gramsci)
- Antonio Gramsci was an Italian political theorist and founder of the Italian Communist Party, writing in prison. In class a student read aloud his passage saying that whenever the question of language comes up, a series of other problems come up with it: forming and enlarging the governing class, building closer ties between the governing groups and the national-popular masses, and reorganizing cultural hegemony.
- The instructor's reading: culture works like an "operating system." Law, culture, ethnic identity, state identity and nationality are built on top of it, and language is the most basic, direct expression of that culture.
- Example: a female state leader (the name is not captured in the transcript) who speaks fluent English and whose husband was a British scholar still chose to speak her own language at events like the Nobel ceremony. The instructor's point was that the choice asserts national and cultural identity.
- The instructor said these questions of ethnicity and identity will become more pressing in the world students are entering.

### New unit: primary sources and Dunhuang
- **Two layers of history.** There is the history that happened and the history later scholars built. We know the first through primary sources.
- Van Schaik's sources include the Tang Annals, the Dunhuang manuscripts, and native Tibetan writings such as the *Testament of Ba*. Part of this passage is garbled.
- Plan for the Dunhuang topic: (1) where Dunhuang is and what happened there; (2) the Library Cave: what came out of it, why and under what circumstances it was sealed, and how it was discovered.
- **The Silk Road on the map.**
  - It starts at Xi'an, the ancient Chang'an, which was the capital of many Chinese dynasties.
  - In the second century BCE a Chinese official (not named in class) traveled west, made contact with peoples of Central Asia, and on returning to Chang'an wrote up his journey in a travel record.
  - From China the route runs northwest to Dunhuang, where it splits into three branches: northern, middle and southern.
  - Merchants, pilgrims, monks and others used these routes. Everything gathered at Dunhuang before entering China proper, which makes Dunhuang the central hub.
- The name "Silk Road" was coined in the nineteenth century by a German geographer (not named in class). The instructor said the network actually stretched from Japan to Europe.

## Key people, places and terms
- **Songtsen Gampo**: seventh-century Tibetan emperor ("Songtsen the Wise"), credited with sponsoring the Tibetan script. (van Schaik ch. 1; Kapstein ch. 1)
- **Tonmi Sambhota (Thönmi/Tönmi Sambhota)**: legendary minister sent to India (Kashmir in Kapstein) to create the Tibetan alphabet; "Sambhota" means "The Good Tibetan." (van Schaik pp. 12 to 13; Kapstein ch. 1, p. 24)
- **Brahmi**: ancient Indian script, the ancestor of the North Indian scripts that Tibetan writing was based on. (Kapstein ch. 1, p. 24)
- **Sanskrit**: classical Indian language of Brahmanism, which later became the scriptural and debate language of Mahayana Buddhism. (Kapstein; Gethin)
- **Prakrit**: vernacular Indian languages used in early Buddhist teaching. (from class; not found by grep in readings-text)
- **Nagarjuna**: Indian Mahayana philosopher, founder of the Middle Way (Madhyamaka) and its teaching of emptiness. (van Schaik; Kapstein)
- **Asanga**: Indian Mahayana scholar named in class as a Sanskrit-educated author. (from class; check Gethin)
- **Mahayana**: the later "Great Vehicle" movement in Buddhism, whose scriptures were composed in Sanskrit. (Gethin; van Schaik)
- **iti**: Sanskrit particle that marks the end of a quotation. (from class)
- **tsheg**: dot that separates syllables in written Tibetan. (from class; Kapstein notes Tibetan is "written and read syllabically")
- **Sinicization**: absorbing Chinese culture, script or identity. (from class)
- **Antonio Gramsci**: Italian Marxist theorist and founder of the Italian Communist Party; linked language to cultural hegemony. (from class slide; not in readings-text)
- **Cultural hegemony**: Gramsci's term for dominance exercised through culture instead of force. (from class)
- **Dunhuang**: Silk Road city in Gansu, conquered by Tibet under Trisong Detsen after an eleven-year siege, and the site of the Library Cave. (van Schaik ch. 2, p. 28; Kapstein)
- **Library Cave (Mogao Cave No. 17)**: cave sealed with thousands of manuscripts, found in 1900, holding the earliest surviving Tibetan documents. (Rong Xinjiang article; van Schaik p. 28; Kapstein)
- **Chang'an (modern Xi'an)**: Tang capital and eastern start of the Silk Road. (Kapstein)
- **Silk Road / Silk Route**: network of trade routes linking China, Central Asia and Europe. (van Schaik; Kapstein)
- **Old Tibetan Annals / Tang Annals**: year-by-year Tibetan and Chinese court records, both primary sources for imperial Tibet. (van Schaik; Kapstein ch. 1, p. 27)
- **Testament of Ba (Dba' bzhed)**: early Tibetan narrative history of Buddhism's arrival; fragments survive from Dunhuang. (van Schaik; Kapstein)

## Connections to the readings
- **van Schaik, ch. 1 "Tibet Appears, 600–700," pp. 12 to 13**: the full Tonmi story. Tonmi offers half his gold to a Brahmin who knows twenty scripts, earns the name Sambhota, and returns to teach Songtsen, who shuts himself away to learn to read. Van Schaik notes the letters are "based so closely on the writing of Nepal and northern India." He presents writing as one sign of Tibetans "adopting and combining elements" from Nepal, India, China and Persia, which fits the instructor's argument about China. He also says Songtsen asked the Chinese emperor for "the secrets of paper and ink."
- **Kapstein, ch. 1, pp. 24 to 25**: here Tonmi is sent to Kashmir. The script derives from Brahmi-based North Indian or Central Asian scripts. Tibetan uses "thirty letters together with four signs showing vowel modifications," which matches the instructor's count of four vowel signs. Kapstein notes that Tibetan was open "above all" to Chinese and Sanskrit influence. He also says there is "no graphic device used to mark word boundaries," a useful detail next to the instructor's point that the tsheg marks syllables.
- **Kapstein, Songtsen Gampo legends section**: Grammatical texts attributed to Tönmi Sambhota are "no doubt apocryphal" but still memorized. The script took shape "close to the time of Songtsen Gampo."
- **Kapstein, ch. 1, pp. 27 to 28, and ch. 2**: Historical writing was "influenced to some extent by Chinese historiographical practices." The Dunhuang hoard contains the Old Tibetan Annals and Old Tibetan Chronicle, and the Library Cave held more than four thousand Tibetan manuscripts. This supports the lecture's primary-sources theme.
- **Kapstein on the Gansu Corridor**: describes it as the place "where trade routes converged before entering China proper," the same point the instructor made about Dunhuang as a hub.
- **van Schaik, ch. 2 "The Holy Buddhist Empire, 700–797," p. 28**: the eleven-year siege of Dunhuang. Under Tibetan rule, Tibetan became the language of government and Chinese inhabitants had to write in the Tibetan alphabet. The Tang Annals record them weeping as they put their Chinese clothes away. The manuscripts were sealed in a cave at the end of the tenth century. This passage is a direct case study of language and identity, which links the Gramsci discussion to the next topic.
- **Rong Xinjiang, "The Nature of the Dunhuang Library Cave and the Reasons for Its Sealing"**: covers the 1900 discovery of Mogao Cave No. 17, Stein's "waste repository" theory, Pelliot's theory that the cave was sealed in 1035 against the Xi Xia, and Rong's own explanation. This is the reading for the Library Cave part of the next lecture.
- **van Schaik, Acknowledgements**: names the Old Tibetan Annals and the Testament of Ba as his core Tibetan sources. This is a good place to start the "what primary sources does the author use" exercise.

## Writing and reading-skills guidance
- When you read academic work in the humanities, always check which primary sources the author uses. For van Schaik these are the Tang Annals, the Dunhuang manuscripts, and native Tibetan histories like the Testament of Ba.
- Separate the two layers of history: what happened and what later scholars or traditions built. The Tonmi story is a good example of the second layer.
- Watch for one-sided claims such as "Tibetan comes only from Sanskrit." Ask what evidence is missing, and ask why a tradition might emphasize one connection and hide another.
- Place a topic in more than one framework (Tibet alone, regional history, Buddhist history). Each one raises different questions.
