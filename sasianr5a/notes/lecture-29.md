# Lecture 29: Chan Doctrine and the Road to the Samye Debate

## Summary

This session finished the groundwork for the Samye debate by explaining who Moheyan was and what kind of Buddhism he brought to Tibet. Most of the hour was a lecture on Chinese Chan (Zen) doctrine: the flower sermon and the idea that ultimate truth cannot be put into words, the slogan "not establishing words and letters," the finger-and-moon metaphor, the constructed Indian-to-Chinese patriarchal lineage, Bodhidharma's two short teachings, and the split between Shenxiu's gradual path and Huineng's sudden path. The instructor then connected Moheyan to Shenxiu's branch, described his success among the Tibetan nobility, the jealousy and open conflict with the Indian monks, and the tsenpo's decision to settle the quarrel with a formal debate. The class closed with a student summary and a list of unresolved problems: who judged the debate, in what language it was conducted, and whether a debate between a scholastic and an anti-scholastic tradition is a fair contest at all. The details of the debate itself come next class.

## Lecture notes

### Opening discussion: why invite both Indian and Chinese teachers

The class continued a discussion from the previous session about Trisong Detsen's motives. Students suggested he invited Chinese monks alongside Indian ones, and brought in Chinese law and Chinese teachers for his family members, without showing a doctrinal favorite. The instructor agreed: at this stage the ruler had no preference for a particular flavor of Buddhism. What mattered was that a tradition be well established. His position was essentially political, and the result was an inclusive religious atmosphere. The instructor then flagged the turn the story takes: that inclusive atmosphere does not last, because the Chinese monk and the Indian scholars end up competing with each other.

### Who was Moheyan, and why detour into Chan

Moheyan was a Chinese Chan master. Because of that, the class has been working through Chan doctrine first: its self-understanding, its lineage claims, and its view of mind and enlightenment. Without that background, the instructor argued, the debate at Samye cannot be read properly.

### The flower sermon and the limits of language

The instructor reviewed the Chan origin story. At a gathering the Buddha silently held up a flower and said nothing. Nobody understood what he meant to deliver, except Mahakashyapa, who smiled. The instructor pressed the class on what the key point of the story is: not the flower, not the smile, but what sits behind both. The truth here is something inexpressible, something that cannot be revealed by words.

He pushed students for examples of things language cannot carry. One student offered love, and the instructor agreed: ask different people and you get different definitions, some tied to sexual attraction, others not, so even one word hides many layers.

He then ran a demonstration with a bottle of fragrance. He described it in words first, oolong tea notes, the sense of standing in a garden in spring, then passed the bottle around and asked students to smell it. The point: once you have smelled it you know exactly what the experience is, but your description still fails, because your listeners cannot map your words onto an experience they have not had. The problem of transmitting truth is the same problem. The capacity of the audience is part of the equation.

Two possibilities were distinguished for why someone stays silent: either they do not know the answer, or they know it and cannot articulate it. Chan takes the second position.

### "Not establishing words and letters"

The instructor laid out the standard Chan self-description: a special transmission outside the scriptures, not establishing words and letters, directly pointing to the person's mind, seeing one's own nature. The Buddha did not tell Mahakashyapa to go read a sutra.

He then corrected the common misunderstanding. "Not establishing words and letters" does not mean Chan produced no texts, nor that it was purely oral. The Chan tradition produced a large body of Buddhist literature and became one of the most widely read Buddhist genres. The claim is narrower: you cannot rely on words alone, and you cannot express your own enlightenment through words. Real experience is ranked above the letter.

Two metaphors carried this:

- Words are a boat that carries you to the other shore. Once you arrive, you discard the boat.
- Buddha nature is the moon in the night sky. Teachings and scriptures are the finger pointing at it. The mistake is looking at the finger, or taking the finger for the ultimate truth.

### The patriarchal lineage and its agenda

The instructor put up the Chan lineage: Mahakashyapa first, then Ananda (the Buddha's cousin, disciple, and the reciter who preserved the sutras), then Ashvaghosha, credited as author of the Buddhacarita, running down to Bodhidharma.

He told the class not to memorize it, because it is a fabrication constructed by Chinese monks. The useful question is why it was built. A student answered that it makes the tradition look more organized and more connected to India. The instructor confirmed and generalized the point: Chinese Chan traced its patriarchs back through India to the historical Buddha in order to claim authenticity. He noted the same move in Mahayana itself, which appears centuries after the Buddha's death and still traces its origin to the historical Buddha. Bodhidharma, by contrast, is a genuine historical figure, attested in Chinese historical documents.

### Bodhidharma's two statements

The instructor read two short teachings attributed to Bodhidharma and glossed them.

1. One must have profound faith in the fact that one and the same true nature is possessed by all sentient beings, both ordinary and enlightened, and that this true nature is only covered up and made imperceptible by false sense impressions.
2. If one discards the false and takes refuge in the true, one resides frozen in wall contemplation, in which self and other are one and the same; one resides fixedly without wavering, never again to be swayed by written teachings.

The first establishes that every sentient being already possesses Buddha nature, obscured by dust, meaning ignorance. That is why we remain ordinary beings even though we hold the seed of buddhahood. The second locates authority in contemplative experience rather than in written teachings. The instructor tied this back to a question he had asked the class earlier in the term about whether all sentient beings possess Buddha nature.

### The sixth patriarch splits in two: Shenxiu and Huineng

Bodhidharma's teaching passed through six generations in China, beginning with Huike. At the sixth generation there are two claimants rather than one: Shenxiu, the older of the two, and Huineng. The instructor stressed that the difference between them is not a matter of personal experience or teaching style but of doctrine, and that this split shapes the whole later tradition.

He compared their two famous verses line by line.

Shenxiu:
> The body is a bodhi tree, the mind a standing mirror bright. Time and again wipe it clean, let no dust alight.

Huineng:
> Bodhi fundamentally has no tree. The bright mirror is not a stand. From the beginning, not one thing exists. Where can the dust alight?

The comparison the instructor drew out:

| Shenxiu | Huineng |
| --- | --- |
| The body is a bodhi tree: an affirmation, an assertion | Bodhi fundamentally has no tree: negation |
| The mind is like a clear mirror | The bright mirror is not a stand |
| Wipe it clean: there is a task to perform | From the beginning not one thing exists: nothing to do |
| Let no dust alight: avoid contamination | Where can the dust alight: there is no dust |

He asked the class to notice the rhetorical strategy, not just the content. Shenxiu affirms; Huineng only negates and refuses to affirm anything. A student connected this to the Heart Sutra, which the instructor endorsed: no form, no sensation, no eye, no ear, no tongue, and then the double negation, no non-form, no non-eye. If you understand the negation you cross to the other shore.

### Gradual versus sudden

From the two verses the instructor derived two paths.

Shenxiu's position is the path of gradual cultivation. It treats mind and ignorance as two separate things, a realist and dualistic view in which pure mind and worldly defilement are locked in constant struggle. Something has to be discarded for the true face of the mind to appear. The corresponding practice is step by step: study, practice, progressive attainment of awareness.

Huineng's position is non-duality. Mind and dust are not two. Everything is empty at its core, emptiness is already the dharma, and inherent wisdom needs no accumulation. If nothing exists, there is nothing to purify and nothing to do. The instructor categorized this attitude with the Chinese term wuwei, non-action, a term Chan borrowed and one that circulated widely across the East Asian Buddhist world including Japan. Do not act, do not fabricate, do not chase, stay in place. The crucial twist: doing nothing is itself the practice, not an absence of practice. You are already a Buddha, already uncontaminated, so dwell in that understanding. Enlightenment comes in the moment one realizes this.

He offered a contemporary analogy for the attitude, the East Asian internet term "lying flat," used by young people in China, Japan, and Korea who feel they cannot climb socially but will not fall to the bottom either, so they stop striving. He was explicit that this is only a loose illustration of the structure, not an equation.

The instructor asked the class twice to remember the distinction between gradual and sudden, because it is later absorbed into Tibetan Buddhism and becomes central to its internal conflicts.

### Reading the supplementary article on the debate

The instructor acknowledged that the assigned scholarly article on the Samye debate is difficult. The author's name as spoken is not recoverable from the recording. His characterization: the article compiles and surveys the existing scholarship on the debate, uses a strongly textual and philological method, works closely with the documents rather than with what people believe about them, and piles up technical jargon by giving terms in Sanskrit, Tibetan, and English at once, which makes the same concept appear under several labels. His advice was to work out which labels refer to the same term and then follow the argument; if you can get through it, the article gives you the whole picture of how the debate came about. Students who had not finished it were told to finish it, and he promised to walk the class through it after completing this part of the lecture.

### Moheyan in Tibet and the breakdown

By the period of the sixth patriarchs, in the eighth century, Chan had separated into two branches: a southern branch represented by Huineng, and the gradual branch of Shenxiu. Moheyan belongs to Shenxiu's branch. The instructor said Moheyan was not a direct disciple of Shenxiu; if he remembered correctly, Moheyan's master was the direct disciple. He repeated for emphasis that Moheyan came from the gradual branch. (Note the friction here with the readings, flagged below.)

Arriving in Tibet, Moheyan transmitted Chan teachings to the nobility and won their hearts. The instructor pointed out what that success actually means in context: winning hearts among the nobility means winning power and money, since political power follows those followers.

According to Tibetan historical materials, the Indian monks grew jealous. The competition between the Chinese monks and the Indian scholars escalated into serious conflict, with some violence involved, including self-harm by some of Moheyan's disciples. Eventually the tsenpo intervened, stopped the fighting, and organized a formal debate so that the two sides could prove which doctrine and which tradition was superior. The instructor noted that formal debate is an ancient Indian tradition, and that under its logic the winner is taken to hold the higher and more correct doctrine.

### Why the debate was rigged from the start

The instructor asked the class whether a debate is a reasonable way to settle this. One student said the outcome is subjective. The instructor pushed further, setting the tsenpo aside and looking only at the two parties. Chan does not rely on scriptures and does not advocate doctrinal argument; it advocates practice. So how can a Chan master debate a scholastic trained in doctrinal argument and have the result mean anything? The format itself favors one side before anyone speaks.

He gave the outcome in advance: the tsenpo ruled for the Indian side, and Moheyan was forced to leave Tibet. The details come next class.

### Student summary and the list of tensions

A volunteer summarized the session, treating the lecture itself as a text to be summarized and outlined: Trisong Detsen invited Moheyan to spread Chan Buddhism in Tibet; Indian and Chinese monks began competing; a debate was held to decide which school was better; the Indian side won because it had the scriptures and Moheyan did not argue from scripture; Chan holds that truth is inexpressible and that scriptures are a vehicle toward enlightenment rather than enlightenment itself; and there are two models of enlightenment, gradual and sudden.

One correction to carry forward: in the summary the gradual and sudden labels were swapped. Shenxiu is gradual, continuous cultivation; Huineng is sudden, the mind already knows the truth and realizing it is enough.

The instructor then asked how many pairs of tension the class could count in the session:

- India versus China, both as sources of Buddhism and as political patrons.
- Scholastic study versus meditative practice.
- Gradual versus sudden, which also maps onto northern versus southern Chan.
- Monastic versus lay, raised by the identity of the judge.

### Unresolved problems to revisit

The instructor closed by adding complications rather than resolving them.

First, the debate was not between Moheyan and Shantarakshita. Shantarakshita had already died. His disciples needed a leading figure to succeed him, so they returned to India and invited Kamalashila to Tibet. The debate therefore takes place between Kamalashila and Moheyan.

Second, the language problem. Three parties with three languages: the Chinese master, the Tibetan court, and the Indian scholar, whose language may or may not have been Sanskrit. The instructor's working assumption is that the proceedings were in Tibetan, with interpreters, and he pointed to a passage in the van Schaik reading where an interpreter appears. He doubted Kamalashila's command of Tibetan, since he had only recently arrived from India and had little time to learn.

Third, the judge problem. The ultimate judge was the tsenpo, a layman, not a monastic. He had no experience of China and almost certainly did not understand Chinese. Is a lay ruler qualified to adjudicate a dispute between two monks over doctrine and practice? The instructor left all three questions open, and said the class would come back to them, possibly without solving them.

## Key people, places and terms

- **Moheyan** (Tibetan: hwa shang ma ha yan): Chinese Chan master, teaching at Dunhuang before being invited to central Tibet. Kapstein, *The Tibetans*, ch. 3, notes his following included members of the royal family.
- **Kamalashila**: Indian scholar, disciple of Shantarakshita, invited to Tibet after his master's death to argue the Indian side. Van Schaik p. 38.
- **Shantarakshita**: Indian abbot, dead before the debate, who had instructed the tsenpo to call on Kamalashila if disputes over practice arose.
- **Trisong Detsen**: the tsenpo who patronized both Indian and Chinese teachers, convened the debate, and awarded victory. Throughout the transcript the speech-to-text renders "tsenpo" as "temple" and "Zengbo."
- **Ba Selnang**: Tibetan nobleman sent to Nepal to invite Shantarakshita and also sent to seek Chinese teachers. Van Schaik pp. 33–34, 37.
- **Reverend Kim**: the Korean Chan teacher Selnang met in China, who taught him Zen meditation and advised him on which scriptures would persuade the tsenpo. Van Schaik p. 37.
- **Samye**: the monastic complex built under Trisong Detsen and the site of the debate, held in one of its smaller temples. Van Schaik pp. 35–38.
- **Dunhuang**: Silk Road city and center of Chinese Buddhism, conquered by Trisong Detsen's armies in the 780s; Moheyan had been teaching there.
- **Mahakashyapa**: the disciple who smiled at the Buddha's silent flower sermon; first patriarch in the Chan lineage.
- **Ananda**: the Buddha's cousin and disciple, reciter of the sutras; second in the lineage.
- **Ashvaghosha**: credited with the *Buddhacarita*; placed in the Chan lineage.
- **Bodhidharma**: historically attested figure who brought Chan to China; source of the two statements read in class.
- **Huike**: first of the six Chinese generations after Bodhidharma.
- **Shenxiu**: older claimant to the sixth patriarchate; gradual cultivation; northern branch; Moheyan's lineage.
- **Huineng**: the other sixth patriarch; sudden enlightenment; southern branch.
- **Buddha nature**: the true nature possessed by all sentient beings, covered over by the dust of ignorance.
- **Emptiness**: the absence of permanent, independent things, personal self and external phenomena alike existing only relatively. Van Schaik p. 38 quotes the Heart Sutra's "form is emptiness, and emptiness is form."
- **Heart Sutra**: invoked by a student as the model for Huineng's purely negative rhetoric.
- **Wuwei**: non-action; Chinese term borrowed into the Chan vocabulary for the sudden path's stance that there is nothing to do.
- **Lying flat**: contemporary East Asian term the instructor used as a loose structural analogy for wuwei, not as a historical equivalent.
- **Six Perfections**: giving, morality, patience, energy, meditation and wisdom. The Indian position held these necessary; the Zen teachers said they could be dispensed with once the nature of mind was recognized. Van Schaik pp. 37–38.

## Connections to the readings

**Van Schaik, *Tibet: A History*, "The Great Debate," pp. 36–39.** This is the spine of the session. Van Schaik gives the sequence the lecture followed: Trisong Detsen looking to both India and China, Ba Selnang's journey and his meeting with Reverend Kim, the invitation of Zen teachers including Moheyan, the Indian teachers' objection that the Chinese were not teaching genuine Buddhism, the extreme protest by the Chinese faction, and the tsenpo's decision to hold a formal debate. He describes the seating arrangement in detail, with the tsenpo enthroned in the middle, Moheyan on his right and Kamalashila on his left, disciples lined up behind in two rows.

Van Schaik also gives both debaters' arguments, which the lecture deferred to next class. Moheyan: only stopping ordinary thought can end samsara, and virtuous and sinful actions are both part of the problem, like black and white clouds that equally block the sun; the teachings on virtue were for duller disciples. Kamalashila: real wisdom comes through analytical insight, abandoning analysis cuts wisdom off at the root, and non-conceptualization without analysis produces a state like fainting, "a practice of stupidity."

Note one important friction between lecture and reading. The instructor placed Moheyan in Shenxiu's gradual branch and asked the class to remember that. Van Schaik (p. 39) and Kapstein (ch. 3) both present Moheyan in the debate as the advocate of the instantaneous or immediate approach, which the tsenpo then rejected in favor of gradual practice. This is worth raising in class or office hours. Either the instructor is distinguishing Moheyan's lineage affiliation from the position attributed to him by the Tibetan sources, or a word was lost in the recording. The first reading would be an interesting point: a teacher from the northern gradual lineage remembered by Tibetan historians as the champion of sudden enlightenment.

**Kapstein, *The Tibetans*, ch. 3 (pp. 80–81 of the extract).** Shorter but more skeptical, and useful against van Schaik. Kapstein states that the documents describing the two factions disagree about who won, that the debate "may well have been in effect a ritual performance that ended in polite accolades for both sides," and that although later historians insist Trisong Detsen banned Chan, the evidence suggests Chan continued to thrive in parts of the Tibetan empire, perhaps even with imperial support. Van Schaik makes a compatible point on p. 39: a Chinese version of the debate ends with the tsenpo blessing the Chinese teachers, and some modern scholars question whether a debate happened at all rather than a series of discussions and literary exchanges.

Put these next to the instructor's argument that the debate format was unfair to Chan from the outset, and you get a usable thesis: the "debate" may be less an event than a genre, a story told later to justify a preference already settled.

**Van Schaik, p. 39, on the aftermath.** The lasting consequence was not just the verdict but the direction of translation. India, not China, became the great source, and the translation project under Trisong Detsen eventually filled some three hundred volumes. The need for the graduated path and the preference for India as the source of true Buddhism came to characterize Tibetan Buddhism, and the debate story justified that position. This is the content behind the instructor's instruction to remember the gradual/sudden distinction because it is later absorbed into Tibetan Buddhism.

**The Dunhuang library cave article.** Not discussed directly, but relevant to the "ancient manuscripts" van Schaik cites as giving Moheyan a more nuanced view of meditation. Kapstein notes that the Tibetan occupation of Dunhuang lasted roughly three-quarters of a century and that the manuscripts discovered there in 1907 are the richest source for late-first-millennium Tibetan history. The article's one mention of Chan is a preface to the early Chan history *Lengjia shizi ji*.

**The supplementary article on the debate.** The instructor's description, a compilation of prior scholarship plus close philological work on the documents, matches the historiographical angle Kapstein takes rather than van Schaik's narrative one. Read it with Kapstein's paragraph in hand.

Lecture-only content with no counterpart in the readings: the flower sermon, the finger-and-moon metaphor, the patriarchal lineage, Bodhidharma's two statements, and the two verses of Shenxiu and Huineng. Neither van Schaik nor Kapstein covers these. If you cite them in an essay, cite them as lecture, and remember that the final and midterm essays must rely exclusively on assigned course materials and must not be summaries of lecture content.

## Writing and reading-skills guidance

**Summarizing a lecture as a text.** The volunteer exercise asked for the main argument plus an outline, using the class session itself as the text. That is the move UNC's "Summary: Using it Wisely" handout is built around: a summary states the central claim and its support rather than replaying everything that happened in order. Practice it on the readings too, since both essays are explicitly forbidden from being summaries, which means you have to be able to tell a summary from an argument in your own draft.

**Counting tensions as an analytical method.** The instructor's closing question, how many pairs of tension do you see, is a reusable close-reading tool. Asking what is in conflict in a passage, and at how many levels at once, is what gets you from description to argument. Here one episode yields at least four axes of conflict: geographic, methodological, doctrinal, and institutional. Any one of them could carry an essay; the geographic one alone would not.

**Attend to rhetorical strategy, not just content.** The instructor stopped the comparison of the two verses to ask about the language style and the strategy. Shenxiu affirms, Huineng only negates. The doctrinal difference is visible in the grammar before you reach the philosophy. That is close reading, and it is the skill the course names first in its learning objectives.

**Jargon in difficult secondary sources.** The method the instructor gave for the hard article is practical and worth generalizing: when a scholar gives the same concept in Sanskrit, Tibetan, and English, build yourself a correspondence list so the labels stop multiplying, then follow the argument. Related to UNC's "Reading to Write" handout.

**Be careful attributing positions to named figures.** The gradual/sudden mix-up in the student summary, and the possible mismatch between the lecture and the readings on Moheyan's branch, both show how fast an essay can go wrong once a label is pinned to the wrong name. Verify every attribution against the page before it goes into a draft, and cite the page.

**Distinguish the event from the account of the event.** Kapstein and van Schaik both separate what happened at Samye from what Tibetan historians later said happened. That distinction is the kind of analytical perspective the essay assignments reward, as opposed to retelling the debate narrative.
