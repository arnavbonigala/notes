# Lecture 10: Songtsen Gampo as "Dharma King": the Historical King and the Later Buddhist Image

## Summary
The class opened with the statue of Songtsen Gampo and his two princesses in Lhasa, and with the Jowo statue in the Jokhang temple, which pilgrims keep coating in gold. From there the instructor separated two "layers" of Songtsen Gampo. The first is the historical seventh-century king. The second is the Buddhist figure that scholars built from roughly the 10th to 12th centuries onward, who is a dharma king and an emanation of Avalokiteshvara, with his two queens as emanations of Tara. The instructor argued that the second layer should not be written off as "fake." It is a different kind of history, written through a Buddhist view of the world after Tibet became a Buddhist country, and it deserves to be studied on its own terms. The class then tested whether Buddhism was actually established under Songtsen by checking the Three Jewels (Buddha, Dharma, Sangha). There were statues and temples, but there was no reliable Tibetan translation of scriptures and no Tibetan monks. His funeral was also conducted by non-Buddhist priests. The conclusion was that Songtsen brought Buddhism into Tibet as a culture but did not establish it there.

## Lecture notes

### Opening: the statues in Lhasa
- The class looked at a famous statue group in Lhasa: Songtsen Gampo with his two queens, one from Nepal and one from China. The transcript is unclear about which building it is in. The instructor seemed to say it is not in the Potala but somewhere tied to Songtsen's own palace. Van Schaik places it "elsewhere in Lhasa" (p. 10), and Kapstein's Plate 9 shows a Songtsen statue in the Potala.
- Two Buddha statues were discussed:
  - **Ramoche**: the temple associated with Princess Wencheng.
  - **The Jowo** (Jowo Shakyamuni): the statue Wencheng is said to have carried to Lhasa. It now sits in the **Jokhang**. Van Schaik says her statue was first placed in Ramoche and later moved to the Jokhang (p. 10).
- **Why the Jowo looks "fat":** the instructor asked whether it still looks like a 12-year-old Buddha. It does not, because it keeps getting bigger. Devotees buy gold, melt it, and brush it onto the statue as an offering, so the layers build up over time.
- **Access today:** in the morning the shrine is open to pilgrims, who can go in and offer flowers and incense. In the afternoon it is tourist time, and the shrine is closed off so visitors can only look from outside. The instructor's advice for visitors is to go very early, around 5 or 6 a.m.
- **Living practice:** a friend of the instructor phoned from the statue and offered to carry the instructor's wishes and aspirations to it. The instructor used this as an example of how Buddhists interact with such images today.

### The dharma king and his divine family
- **Dharma king:** a king who protects and spreads the Buddha's teaching. Later Tibetans called Songtsen Gampo one.
- **Avalokiteshvara** (bodhisattva of compassion): Songtsen Gampo is regarded as his manifestation. The Dalai Lama is the best-known modern figure regarded the same way.
- **Potala:** the palace is named after Avalokiteshvara's pure land. The instructor linked the name to a lotus, which the transcript garbles. Van Schaik says the palace is "named after the visionary realm that is the home of Avalokiteshvara" and was built on the ruins of Songtsen Gampo's palace (pp. 123–124). The instructor also said it was rebuilt later, which matches the Fifth Dalai Lama's construction in the 17th century (Kapstein, Plate 18: White Palace begun 1645).
- **Manjushri:** the deity of wisdom. The instructor said Tibetans are especially attached to two qualities, wisdom (Manjushri) and compassion (Avalokiteshvara).
- **Tara:** the two princesses are regarded as manifestations of Tara, a female deity. The instructor described her as a protector and as the female expression of the same compassion. Kapstein only calls the brides "emanations of goddesses" (p. 63). Van Schaik mentions Green Tara as a female deity elsewhere, in a much later context.
- Together the king and two queens form what Kapstein calls "the royal trinity."

### Two layers of Songtsen Gampo
- The instructor asked the class who created the dharma-king image. Students guessed Songtsen himself, his ministers, or his descendants. The instructor corrected this: **no such idea existed in Songtsen's own time.** Nobody then thought of him as a manifestation of Avalokiteshvara.
- The idea took shape after about the 10th and 11th centuries, was developed in the 12th century, and was strengthened again in the 17th century with the Dalai Lamas and the Potala. One phrase about the context of the 10th-century development is unintelligible in the transcript.
- **Layer 1:** the historical figure.
- **Layer 2:** the figure as Buddhist scholars after the 10th and 11th centuries portrayed him, a dharma king and protector of the Dharma.
- **How to judge layer 2:** do not simply label it true or false, "fake" or "untrue." Ask why these scholars built this image and what agenda it served. It reflects the big turn in Tibetan history. Before the 10th century, Tibetans had contact with Buddhism but were not deeply shaped by it. Afterward Tibet became a Buddhist country and reread its whole past through Buddhist cosmology. It is "another kind of history," and the task is to understand it.

### Did Songtsen establish Buddhism? Testing the Three Jewels
- The class reviewed that Songtsen took up Buddhism as part of building his empire. Along with language and culture, it helped him deal with the surrounding states. The question now was what he actually did for Buddhism inside Tibet.
- The main evidence people point to is that he built temples, together with Wencheng and the Nepalese princess. But does a temple count as a real Buddhist monastery? A real one needs all **three jewels**:
  1. **Buddha**, represented by statues. **This was present:** the princesses' statues, and possibly others made locally.
  2. **Dharma**, represented by scriptures. In later imperial times emperors sponsored copies of the scriptures, which were kept in well-decorated rooms and recited every year, a tradition that continues. Some later sources say Wencheng and the Nepalese princess organized translation from Chinese and Sanskrit. The instructor doubted this. The writing system was brand new at the time, and it is not certain whether it came before or after the princesses arrived. A new script could not yet carry complex Buddhist philosophy. About a century later Tibetans were still working out standards for translating scripture. Some Chinese scriptures may have been physically present in Lhasa, but reliable Tibetan translation on a large scale in Songtsen's time is doubtful.
  3. **Sangha**, meaning "assembly" or "gathered group" in Sanskrit.
     - In early Buddhism it meant monastics. These renunciants do no productive work. Their jobs are to preserve and preach the Buddha's teaching and to internalize it through practice.
     - With Mahayana (the instructor dated it to about the 1st to 3rd centuries CE), the term expanded to include lay people who take refuge and vows and support the monastic community.
     - The narrow meaning is monastics. The wider meaning includes the lay community.
     - Under Songtsen there were probably foreign monks in Lhasa, from China, Central Asia, and perhaps India. But there was **no Tibetan monk.** The first real Tibetan monks appeared about a century later, and Songtsen never organized an ordination for Tibetans.
- **Conclusion:** Songtsen introduced Buddhism as culture and resource, but Buddhism did not take root in Tibetan soil in his time. Historically he played a key role in welcoming Buddhism, but he was not himself a committed Buddhist.

### Supporting evidence: Songtsen's death and funeral
- He died young. The instructor appears to say he was about 32. Van Schaik gives his death as 649. Kapstein cites a shorter chronology in which he was born c. 617, which would fit that age.
- His funeral was conducted by **Bön** (non-Buddhist) priests in the traditional way, not by Buddhist ritual.
- **Why funerals matter:** funeral rites mark a person's religious identity. The instructor's comparison was Japan from about the 16th to the 20th century. Families asked Buddhist temples to perform funerals whether or not the dead person was really a Buddhist. They paid well for the rites and for a posthumous Buddhist title. This is how religion works in society.
- The transcript ends before the instructor finishes this point.

## Key people, places and terms
- **Songtsen Gampo** ("Songtsen the Wise"): the founding tsenpo (emperor) of the Tibetan empire, died 649 (van Schaik ch. 1) or c. 605–650 (Kapstein ch. 3).
- **Princess Wencheng**: Tang Chinese princess who married into the Tibetan royal house and is said to have brought the Jowo statue (van Schaik p. 10; Kapstein pp. 61–63).
- **Bhrikuti / Tritsün**: Nepalese princess known only from later sources, whose existence some scholars question (Kapstein p. 62; van Schaik notes p. 271 n. 6).
- **Lhasa**: capital of the empire (van Schaik ch. 1).
- **Jokhang**: Lhasa's oldest Buddhist temple, built on a Nepalese model and home of the Jowo. It was also called Rasa Trulnang or Tsuglagkhang (van Schaik p. 6 and n. 6).
- **Ramoche**: Lhasa temple where Wencheng's statue was first placed (van Schaik p. 10; Kapstein p. 63).
- **Jowo** ("Lord") **Shakyamuni**: the Buddha statue credited to Wencheng and Tibet's greatest pilgrimage image (Kapstein pp. 61, 64).
- **Potala Palace**: palace named for Avalokiteshvara's realm, built on the ruins of Songtsen's palace (van Schaik pp. 123–124); White Palace begun by the Fifth Dalai Lama in 1645 (Kapstein Plate 18).
- **Dharma king**: a Buddhist ruler who protects and spreads the Dharma (van Schaik, "dharma kings," pp. 30–33).
- **Avalokiteshvara**: bodhisattva of compassion and patron of Tibet (Kapstein ch. 1 p. 2 and ch. 3 p. 63; van Schaik pp. 11, 123).
- **Manjushri**: bodhisattva of wisdom (van Schaik, later chapters).
- **Tara**: female Buddhist deity; the instructor said the two princesses are regarded as her manifestations (in-class claim; Kapstein says "goddesses").
- **Three Jewels**: Buddha, Dharma, Sangha (van Schaik p. 36, Samye pillar inscription).
- **Sangha**: from Sanskrit, "assembly." Narrowly the monastic community, and in Mahayana usage also the lay community (van Schaik p. 36: "the community of monks").
- **Mahayana**: the Buddhist movement that widened the meaning of sangha (in-class).
- **Bön / non-Buddhist priests**: ritual specialists who ran royal funerals (van Schaik pp. 14–16, 33).
- **Tonmi Sambhota**: the legendary creator of the Tibetan script (van Schaik pp. 12–13; Kapstein p. 64).

## Writing and reading-skills guidance
- **Separate the event from its later retelling.** When a source describes Songtsen as a Buddhist king, ask when it was written and by whom. Van Schaik and Kapstein both mark this line with phrases like "later tradition credits," "for later Tibetans," and "at the time, though." Watch for those signals when you close-read.
- **Don't judge later sources only as true or false.** Ask what agenda or worldview produced them and what they tell us about the people who wrote them. This kind of question can carry an analytical essay, and the syllabus asks for a new perspective rather than a summary.
- **Test a claim against a clear set of criteria.** The Three Jewels check is a model for building an argument: set out the criteria, check the evidence for each one, then reach a qualified conclusion ("introduced, but not established").
- **Use supporting evidence from a different angle.** The funeral argument backs up the Three Jewels argument with a separate kind of evidence.
