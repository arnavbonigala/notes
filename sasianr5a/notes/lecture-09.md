## Lecture notes

### Assignment 2 prompt: the Wencheng marriage
- The topic is Songtsen Gampo's marriage to Princess Wencheng of Tang China. Leave out his other marriages (Zhangzhung, Nepal).
- Build a clear argument about which effects of the marriage were most important to the development of the Tibetan state.
- The prompt lists possible areas, some covered in lecture and the readings and some not. You do not have to cover every one. Choose one, two or three that best support your main argument.
- This is an essay. Do not answer the listed points one by one as you would on an exam.
- Refer specifically to the readings and lectures.

### Grading philosophy
- Grades are not curved and not compared across students.
- The instructor compares your work to your own starting point. A student who earns lower scores early but improves a lot can still get a high final grade.
- If you are confused or unhappy about a score, or curious why it was high, email the instructor or come to office hours to go through the paper together.

### Two common problems in student writing
1. **Content accuracy**
   - *Historical accuracy:* only state what you are confident actually happened.
   - *Conceptual clarity:* when you use a concept, give its definition and its boundary.
2. **Organization**
   - Most students followed last week's model structure: an introduction with the main argument, body paragraphs (roughly the second through fourth) that each argue one supporting point, and a conclusion. Academic books use the same structure. Experienced writers can depart from it, but beginners should stick to it.
   - The weak spot is inside the body paragraphs. Each paragraph should mirror the essay: the first sentence states the sub-argument, and every following sentence supports it. In some papers the sentences within a paragraph did not work together to serve the sub-argument.

### Sentence clinic (examples from student papers)
**"Songtsen Gampo, the 33rd king of Tibet, married Princess [Bhrikuti] of Nepal to ally with Nepal."**
- Good: it names the specific princess and country and doesn't just say "a marriage."
- Fix: "Nepal" appears twice. Use "the Nepalese princess."
- Introducing historical figures: the first time a person, event or period appears, give the reader basic information. Assume a general reader with no background.
  - "33rd king" still leaves the reader asking why that number matters. Something like "founder of the Tibetan Empire" tells the reader both who he is and that imperial Tibet began with him.
  - Always give dates, in the form "Songtsen Gampo (617–649 CE)." Adding CE/BCE "means you are well educated."
  - Scholars also give the name in the original script and its transliteration. At your stage that is optional, but dates are required.

**"Unlike the emperors of China, Songtsen had to share his power among the clan leaders and ministers; marriage was often the only way to control the clans."**
- *Use the full name.* Write "Songtsen Gampo," never a shortened form. The instructor said Tibetan royals, like the Japanese royal family, have no family name, so the name can't be split into first name and surname the Western way. Shortening it confuses readers, just as students find Sanskrit names hard to keep straight.
- *Avoid absolute terms* such as "the only way," "the most," "always." Even when you are sure, scholars still debate many of these questions, and an absolute claim commits you to disproving every alternative. Soften it: "marriage was often regarded as an influential way to control the clans." Words like "believed" and "regarded as" present the claim as an interpretation, which gives you room to argue.
- *Be precise about "China."* Today "China" means the PRC. For this period write "Tang China" or "the Tang dynasty." (The instructor gave the Tang's dates only loosely, as running to about the 9th or 10th century.) The instructor said they won't push hard on this one.

**"For Songtsen, it seemed as though the best way to deal with his neighbors was through a political marriage."**
- Good hedging with "seemed." Still write the full name.

**"...royal marriage focused heavily, in fact solely, on the connection..."**
- "Solely" is too strong.

**The geography paragraph** ("Tibet has long been set apart from the surrounding empires... because of the nomadic and dispersed lifestyle... clashing with the sedentary lifestyle of neighboring regions")
- The class identified it as an introduction. It gives context before the topic, which is the right move.
- Opening from geography is fine if it connects to your main argument.
- Accuracy problem: the claim is too broad. The instructor's corrections:
  - Add examples after "neighboring regions" ("such as China").
  - Only China had a long history of centralized government. India and Nepal sought centralized rule too, and the peoples to the north (the instructor mentioned Uyghurs) were nomadic.

**Model introduction on marriage under Songtsen Gampo**
- It opens the topic and ends with a thesis: the marriage relationships helped Tibet build military power and become a more stable, connected kingdom.
- Strengths: logical connecting words between sentences, and every sentence has a clear job.

**Model introduction on legitimacy**
- It defines legitimacy as the right to govern, then argues that this definition is too simple because it leaves out the *sources* that justify a ruler's authority. It names three: divine right, recognition from religious authority, and acceptance by the people.
- Why defining matters: concepts have several layers, and readers won't automatically read them your way. A definition sets a boundary that writer and reader both work inside. A narrower boundary also makes the argument easier to defend.
- Anecdote: a professor (name unclear in the audio) got a review that seemed to misread his paper. A week later he reread the paper from the reviewer's point of view and saw that it really could be read that way. You can't control how readers interpret you, so be as clear and accurate as you can.

### Model student paper: royal legitimacy under Songtsen Gampo
- **Title:** always include one. A title can pose a question, give an explanation, or state the argument. The instructor's example was van Schaik's *Tibet: A History*: "a history" tells you the book is chronological.
- **Introduction:** early Tibet was not always unified. Powerful clan leaders formed alliances and competed with each other. Setting the scene in the 7th century gives the reader a specific time.
- **Handling a foreign term:** the student gave the English first ("the Tibetan ruler, known as the tsenpo") and then used "tsenpo" alone. Explain every foreign term the first time it appears.
- **Thesis:** royal authority was legitimized through the tsenpo's divine status, military success, and political institutions that strengthened royal control. The thesis previews the body paragraphs.
- **Body paragraph 1 (divine status):** first tsenpos came down from heaven and local chiefs bowed to them. The student kept only the details relevant to the argument, then added their own view: this does not mean everyone simply believed in divine rule. The student backed this up with van Schaik, who says the clan leaders most likely believed when it suited them and conspired against the tsenpo when that was more useful. The instructor praised this as close reading and "very political thinking": belief alone cannot guarantee legitimacy. That limitation leads into the next paragraph.
- **Body paragraph 2 (military success):** when Songtsen was thirteen his father was poisoned and parts of the kingdom rose up. Songtsen executed the killer, put down the insurgency and rode west against a hostile army. The context is brief but precise, and it follows logically from paragraph 1.
- The paper used only assigned readings, read closely. Citations support the argument and aren't there for their own sake. The instructor will upload the paper to bCourses Files.

### Citation
- **Direct citation (quotation):** copy a word, phrase or sentence exactly, put it in quotation marks, and give the author. The usual form is author, year, page. For this class, just the author's name (for example, van Schaik) is enough.
  - The year is normally required so readers can tell apart several works by the same author. Two works in the same year become 2011a and 2011b, both listed in the bibliography.
  - Class limit on direct quotation length: the number was cut off in the audio ("don't cite over 15..."), probably 15 words. Confirm before relying on it. Too much quotation makes the paper look copied.
- **Indirect citation (paraphrase):** use signal phrases such as "according to van Schaik" or "as van Schaik suggests." Too many of them interrupt the reader. The alternative is a footnote giving the book title and page. Footnotes are common in academic work. For these essays, "according to..." is fine.
- Citation shows you are not plagiarizing. From Assignment 2 on, every citation must be marked.
- **Reusing your own earlier writing:** copying from your own previous assignment without marking it counts as plagiarism. If you reuse it, cite it as your previous paper.
- **Bibliography:** not required yet, but you should know how. Use the Chicago Manual of Style. Easiest method: search the library catalog by title or author (the demo used *Tibet: A History*), click the cite option, choose Chicago, then copy and paste the entry into your bibliography. ## Key people, places and terms
- **Songtsen Gampo:** 7th-century tsenpo remembered as founder of the Tibetan Empire; name means "Songtsen the Wise" (van Schaik ch. 1; Kapstein ch. 3, which gives c. 605–650 and a reign from c. 617, noting an alternative birth date of c. 617; van Schaik gives his death as 649).
- **Tsenpo:** title of the Tibetan ruler, believed to embody the divine (van Schaik ch. 1, pp. 2–4; Kapstein ch. 3, p. 58).
- **Princess Wencheng:** Tang princess sent to Tibet around 641 as a bride, via the minister Gar Tongtsen. Kapstein says she was first meant for crown prince Gungsong Gungtsen. Credited with bringing the Jowo Buddha statue (van Schaik ch. 1, pp. 6–10; Kapstein ch. 3, pp. 61–62).
- **Nepalese princess (Tritsün / Bhrikuti):** Songtsen Gampo's Nepalese wife. Van Schaik calls her "perhaps legendary." Kapstein notes she appears only in much later accounts (van Schaik ch. 1, p. 10; Kapstein ch. 3, p. 62).
- **Tang dynasty:** the Chinese dynasty of this period; its emperor Taizong negotiated the marriage (van Schaik ch. 1; Kapstein ch. 3).
- **Clan leaders:** nomadic nobles bound to the tsenpo by oath who supported or conspired against him as it suited them (van Schaik ch. 1, p. 4).
- **Primus inter pares / zhanglön:** Kapstein's description of the tsenpo as "first among equals" and the title for in-law ministers from bride-giving clans (Kapstein ch. 3, pp. 58–59).
- **Zhangzhung:** western kingdom conquered after Songtsen's sister, married to its king, spied for him (van Schaik ch. 1, pp. 5–6; Kapstein ch. 3, p. 60).
- **Lhasa:** Songtsen Gampo's center, originally Rasa, "Walled City" (van Schaik ch. 1, p. 5).
- **Legitimacy:** the right to govern and the sources that justify it (defined in class using student work; the concept runs through van Schaik ch. 1).
- **Direct / indirect citation:** quotation versus attributed paraphrase (UNC "Quotations").
- **Chicago Manual of Style:** the citation style for this course (named in class; mentioned in UNC "Quotations").

## Connections to the readings
- **Van Schaik, ch. 1 "Tibet Appears, 600–700," pp. 2–4:** first tsenpos "came down from heaven via the local sacred mountain," "Local chiefs bowed down before him"; the clan leaders "believed when it suited them" and "conspired against him when that seemed more useful"; "When Songtsen was just thirteen, his father was poisoned." The model paper's first two body paragraphs rest on this passage.
- **Van Schaik, ch. 1, section "Power and the Princess," pp. 5–10:** the Zhangzhung marriage ("princesses were political pawns"), the Nepal alliance through Narendradeva, and the Wencheng negotiations with Taizong. He says the marriage "ushered in two decades of peace" and brought cultural exchange: students sent to Chang'an, paper and ink makers sent to Tibet, and the first Buddha statue. These are the main sources for Assignment 2.
- **Kapstein, *The Tibetans*, ch. 3 "The Tsenpo's Imperial Dominion," pp. 58–62:** the tsenpo as primus inter pares and marriage alliances as a source of loyalty and instability, which bears on the "marriage was the only way" sentence. It also has the Tang's first refusal, Songtsen's attack on the Azha, Gar Tongtsen's embassy in 640/641, Wencheng's intended husband Gungsong, and the reduced hostility with Tang afterwards. Useful for an argument about political or diplomatic effects.

## Writing and reading-skills guidance
- Form one main argument and choose evidence for it. Don't march through every prompt point.
- Put your main argument in the introduction and **highlight it in every assignment from now on** (new requirement). For each paragraph, ask how it relates to the main argument.
- Body paragraphs: the first sentence states the sub-argument and every following sentence supports it. Use logical connecting words.
- The first time a figure appears, give who they are, why they matter, and their dates with CE. Write for a reader who knows nothing.
- Always use full Tibetan names such as "Songtsen Gampo."
- Avoid absolute words ("only," "solely," "most"). Hedge with "often," "regarded as," "believed," "seemed."
- Be precise with place and state names: "Tang China," not "China."
- Define abstract concepts such as legitimacy, and set boundaries that make the argument manageable.
- Explain foreign terms (tsenpo) in English on first use.
- Give context before the thesis, and make sure the context is historically accurate.
- Close reading: use the author's own qualifications (van Schaik's doubt about the clans' belief) to support a critical point of your own. Include only details relevant to your argument.
- Always give your paper a title that signals the argument or approach.
