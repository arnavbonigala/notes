# Lecture 2: Course Warm-Up and the Etymology of "Tibet"

## Summary
This was a warm-up session. It opened with student introductions and the instructor's two class rules: lecture slides are not shared or photographed, and AI may only be used in limited ways. The main topic was the etymology of "Tibet," set beside the origins of "India" (from the Sindhu river, with Indians calling their country Bharat) and "China" (from the Qin state, which unified the Chinese mainland). The Tibetan name for Tibet is Bod (pronounced "bö"), and no scholar knows for certain where it comes from. The instructor gave two theories: Bod may come from the religion Bön, or it may have been the name of the winning clan or royal house, the way "Qin" became "China." The class ended with two terms the course will use constantly: "dharma king," the title of the three great Buddhist emperors, and "tsenpo," the title of the Tibetan emperor.

## Lecture notes

They suggested this is an open field of research for Indian students.

- **Ladakh:** It lies on the India–China border and is under Indian administration today. The course will return to it at the end of the semester, because its rulers descend from the Tibetan imperial royal line after the mid-9th-century collapse.

### Etymology: what it means
- Etymology is the history of a word: where it came from and in what social and historical setting it was coined.

### India
- "India" comes from **Sindhu**, meaning "river," and specifically the Indus River (the river, not the Indus Valley).
- Persians and Greeks could not pronounce the initial S, so it became "Hindu/Hind." Over time the H was dropped, which gave "Ind-/India."
- So "India" is a name given by outsiders. Indians call their country **Bharat**. At the 2023 G20 summit, the Indian president's title was written as "President of Bharat" instead of "President of India."

### China
- A common myth says "China" comes from porcelain ("china"). That is wrong.
- After a period in which six states fought each other (plus Qin), the state of **Qin** won through about a century of war and diplomacy and unified the Chinese mainland.
- Qin's influence reached Central and East Asia, so outsiders called the region "the land of Qin," which became "China."
- The instructor said China has had about 25 dynasties, each with its own name, from the Qin at the start to the Qing at the end.

### Two ways to name a country
1. After a geographic feature, as India is named for its river.
2. After a winning state, as China is named for Qin. The name of one small state becomes the name of the whole empire.

### Tibet
- Nobody knows where "Tibet" comes from. There are no written sources to settle it, and no mountain in the region is called "Tibet."
- The Tibetans' own name for Tibet is **Bod**, pronounced "bö" (the transcript renders it as "period").
- **Theory 1 (Bön):** Bön is the old, indigenous religion of Tibet. It existed before Buddhism arrived and was accepted, and it survived throughout Tibetan history. Bön communities remain today near the Chinese border, and there are Bön temples in Ladakh. The instructor said the final "n" and "d" can alternate, so Bön and Bod could be the same word, with the country named after its religion. There is no evidence to prove or disprove this.
- **Theory 2 (the instructor's preference; follows the China pattern):** Before the empire, many clans lived on the plateau. The bigger clans absorbed the smaller ones. One clan became the final winner, and the instructor said it was based in the Lhasa area. Its name was **Pu** (Pugyal, "king of Pu"). That name became the name of the whole country, as Qin did. It is unclear whether Pu was the royal family's name or the name of a place.

### Micro history and macro history
- **Macro history** covers emperors, kings, governments, ministers, wars and politics. **Micro history** covers the lives of ordinary people.
- This course will mostly be macro history. There are two reasons: students need the overall structure of Tibetan history first, and very little material about ordinary people survives. Asian record-keeping mostly preserved elite lives. Some sources on ordinary life do exist; the transcript's "Black World" is probably the Dunhuang library cave.

### The three dharma kings
- Three emperors of imperial Tibet are honored as dharma kings: **Songtsen Gampo**, **Trisong Detsen** and **Ralpachen**. The course will be built around them, starting with Songtsen Gampo next week.
- **How "dharma" changed meaning:**
  1. In ancient Indian thought, dharma is the proper principle or order by which the world runs (students also offered "duty" and "rule"). Demons try to break the dharma, and a dharma king is the ruler with the power and duty to defeat them and restore order.
  2. It then came to mean religion, as the proper way to keep that order.
  3. Once it spread to Central and East Asia, it meant Buddhism specifically. In Tibet, a dharma king is a king who protects and spreads the Buddhist dharma and is personally involved in Buddhism's growth.
- **Religion and politics:** A student said religion requires faith while politics can be critically examined. The instructor answered that this split is modern. In the ancient world religion and politics were hardly different, and even today religion is tied up with politics (the Middle East, Israel and Palestine, and the religious culture of the US).

### Tsenpo
- **Tsenpo** is the title of the Tibetan king or emperor. Students don't need to memorize it but will see it constantly in the readings and should use it in their assignments.
- *Tsen* means strong or powerful, mentally and physically. The *-po* suffix marks a male and *-mo* marks a female, so tsenpo means "the strong, powerful man."
- The instructor also gave a word for queen, which the transcript mangles as "Zenbo." Going by the -mo rule it would be "tsenmo," but that form does not appear in the readings.

## Key people, places and terms
- **Bod (pronounced bö):** the Tibetans' own name for Tibet, and in a narrower sense Central Tibet; "Greater Tibet" is Bod chen po. *van Schaik, Preface (p. xv)*
- **Bön:** Tibet's pre-Buddhist religion, which still exists; one theory links the word to "Bod." *Kapstein, ch. 2 (p. 49); van Schaik*
- **Pugyal / Pugyel:** old dynastic title of Tibet's kings, "king of Pu." *Kapstein, ch. 2*
- **Tsenpo:** title of the Tibetan emperor; van Schaik says the tsenpo was seen as "the embodiment of the divine in this world." *van Schaik, ch. 1; Kapstein, ch. 3 "The Tsenpo's Imperial Dominion"*
- **Dharma king:** a Buddhist ruler who protects and spreads the dharma; Tibet honors three of them. *van Schaik (index: dharma kings pp. 30–33, 120, 150, 204)*
- **Songtsen Gampo:** first dharma king and the "Divine Son" at the center of the new empire. *van Schaik, ch. 1 "Tibet Appears, 600–700"*
- **Trisong Detsen:** second dharma king; established Buddhism in Tibet and founded Samye monastery. *van Schaik*
- **Ralpachen (Tritsug Detsen):** third dharma king, "the long-haired." *van Schaik*
- **Lhasa:** capital of Tibet, originally called Rasa ("Walled City"); renamed Lhasa, "Divine City." *van Schaik, ch. 1*
- **Yarlung valley:** southern Tibet, where the royal line's kingdom began. *van Schaik, ch. 1*
- **Dharamsala:** Indian hill town and base of the 14th Dalai Lama and the government in exile after 1959. *van Schaik; Kapstein*
- **Ladakh:** Himalayan region now in India; its first dynasty descends from the Tibetan royal line. *van Schaik, Preface; Kapstein*
- **Karmapa:** head of the Karma Kagyu school, likely the garbled figure in the Dharamsala passage. *van Schaik*
- **Sindhu:** Sanskrit for "river," specifically the Indus; the root of "India." *(class only)*
- **Bharat:** Indians' own name for India. *(class only)*
- **Qin:** the state that unified the Chinese mainland; the root of "China." *(class only)*
- **Chittamatra ("mind only"):** Buddhist school with a layered model of consciousness. *(class only; not found in readings-text)*
- **Etymology:** the history and origin of a word. *(class only)*
- **Macro / micro history:** history of elites and big events versus history of ordinary lives. *(class only)*

## Connections to the readings
- **van Schaik, Preface (pp. xv–xvi):** Opens with "Where is Tibet?" It distinguishes "political Tibet" from "ethnographic Tibet" and gives the Tibetan terms Bod and Bod chen po. This is the best reading companion to today's etymology discussion. It also covers the 1959 flight of the Dalai Lama to India.
- **van Schaik, ch. 1 "Tibet Appears, 600–700," section "The Divine Destiny of Prince Songtsen" (pp. 2–4):** Covers the tsenpo as divine ruler, the legend of the sky cord and Drigum, and clans absorbed by Songtsen's father starting from the Yarlung valley. This supports the instructor's "winning clan" theory. Note that the reading places the dynasty's origins in **Yarlung, in southern Tibet, not in Lhasa**, and says Lhasa became the center of Songtsen's kingdom.
- **van Schaik, ch. 1 "Power and the Princess" (p. 5):** Rasa ("Walled City") renamed Lhasa ("Divine City").
- **Kapstein, *The Tibetans*, ch. 2 (around p. 49):** Explains that "pre-Buddhist Bön" should be kept separate from the organized Bön religion that took shape around the 10th century. It also records that "It has been proposed ... that bön is related to the word for Tibet itself, bö," and that some Dunhuang manuscripts "do use bön to name Tibet." This is the scholarly source for the instructor's first theory.
- **Kapstein, ch. 2 (the Nyaktri Tsenpo legend):** Gives "the old dynastic title of Tibet's monarchs, Pugyel, 'king of Pu,' that is, the southeastern district of Powo." Note that Kapstein ties Pu to Powo in the southeast, while the instructor tied the winning clan to Lhasa.
- **Kapstein, ch. 3 "The Tsenpo's Imperial Dominion":** Background on the tsenpo title and imperial rule.
- **Kapstein on Ladakh:** Pelgigön, a son of Kyidé Nyimagön, inherited Maryül (Ladakh) and "is considered as the founder of its first dynasty." This backs up the instructor's point about Ladakh.
- **van Schaik on the dharma kings:** Later Tibetan politics looked back to "the three ancestral dharma kings" as the model for uniting religion and government. That echoes the instructor's point that religion and politics were not separate.
- **Dunhuang library cave article:** Relevant to the aside on micro history. Some material on ordinary life survives only in sources like these.

## Writing and reading-skills guidance
- **Etymology as an analytical tool:** Ask who named a place (insiders or outsiders) and in what historical setting. This connects to the syllabus goal of recognizing "how a single name can carry multiple meanings."
- **Separate theory from evidence:** On the Bön/Bod theory, the instructor said it can't be proven right or wrong because there is no evidence either way. In your writing, label speculation as speculation.
- **Keep an objective, academic tone:** You may disagree with the instructor, but argue as a scholar, not from politics.
- **Use the course's terminology:** Learn "tsenpo" and similar terms. You will meet them in the readings and should use them in assignments.
- **Take your own notes:** Slides help you follow the lecture, but you are expected to take notes and write your own summaries to complete assignments.
