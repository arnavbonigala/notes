# Lecture 5: Marriage as Statecraft in Songtsen Gampo's Tibet

## Summary
This lecture looked at how Songtsen Gampo used marriage to build up his young state, both inside Tibet and with the kingdoms around it. The first case was his sister's marriage to the king of Zhangzhung. She was pushed to the edge of that court, ended up spying for her brother, and Zhangzhung was conquered and absorbed. Inside Tibet, the tsenpo took wives from different aristocratic clans in rotation. This kept the clans balanced, won him allies, and protected his heir. The instructor then covered the Nepal alliance, which is thinly documented, and the marriage to the Tang princess Wencheng. That marriage was explained from a Tibetan angle and from a Chinese angle, and the later Buddhist legends were set apart from the historical record. The lecture ended with a comparison of naming systems in Tibet, Japan and China, and with the point that Tibet had no writing system yet. There was also a writing lesson on knowing your own position and its "blind zone" when you argue a paper.

## Lecture notes

### Marriage as a political tool
- In ancient diplomacy, marriage was a common way to build alliances between states or regimes. It links two families, not just two people.
- The theory: marriage creates family-like bonds, and families share resources (military, economic, financial) and do not attack each other.
- The instructor stressed that this is the theory and reality is always different. Van Schaik makes the same point: "That at least was the theory."

### Digression: personal belief vs. public policy
- Example: when Xi Jinping took office, many Buddhists (Tibetan, Chinese and Western) hoped he would support Buddhism. They believed he was a Buddhist, and his wife is described as a follower of Tibetan Buddhism who made offerings at the Mahabodhi temple in Bodh Gaya, where the Buddha attained enlightenment.
- About five years later these Buddhists were disappointed. A leader's private religion does not decide the policy of a large state, which has many dimensions to weigh.
- Lessons drawn: (1) politics is complicated, so don't be an idealist; (2) the Buddhists judged only from their own position and ignored everyone else's. This led into the writing advice below.

### The first royal marriage: Songtsen's sister and Zhangzhung
- Zhangzhung was a kingdom to the west of Songtsen's realm, near the Himalayan border (the instructor used a map showing India, Nepal/Kathmandu and Tibet).
- Songtsen married one of his sisters to the king of Zhangzhung. The readings name her Sekarma (van Schaik) or Semakar (Kapstein) and the king Ligmigya (Kapstein).
- She was unhappy. The cause is unknown, but the king already had a wife or wives, an established family and heirs. Although she was queen in name, she was an outsider and a bride from a hostile state, so she was kept out of court politics.
- Songtsen sent envoys telling her to act like a queen, meaning she should involve herself in the political life of the court. She did not or could not.
- She turned against her husband and sent Songtsen intelligence, which helped him defeat the Zhangzhung king. Zhangzhung became part of Tibet and is still a large part of the Tibet Autonomous Region today.
- The instructor added that, later on, one branch of the Tibetan royal family moved to this western region and stayed there.
- From class Q&A:
  - Did the Zhangzhung court know she was spying? The instructor thinks they probably did. They could not kill her, because killing the queen meant open war with her home state. The only thing they could do was keep her out of politics. That Zhangzhung lost suggests its power was limited.
  - Did she have full queenly rights, or was the marriage understood as purely political? Both. She was queen in name, but how much power she could actually use is questionable and depended on the situation.

### Marriage inside Tibet: rotating queens among the clans
- Students usually focus on the Chinese and Nepalese marriages and forget that Songtsen already had Tibetan wives.
- Tibet was not a centralized state. The tsenpo sat at the top of a hierarchy of big, middle and small clans. He owned only part of the land, and the rest belonged to clan heads (roughly, "ministers"). The clans competed and fought to expand.
- The tsenpos did not take wives from just one or two clans. They rotated among the big clans, so that each major clan got a chance to marry into the royal family and share its power.
- Three benefits (the instructor's recap):
  1. **Balance and fairness among the clans.** Every major clan has a stake in the royal family, which lowers their urge to compete with it.
  2. **Allies for the tsenpo.** The queen's clan (and his mother's clan) becomes his strong support against the other clans.
  3. **Protection of the heir.** The young tsenpo's mother comes from one clan and his wife from another. A maternal uncle might want to kill a young tsenpo and put a different boy from his own clan on the throne. The wife's clan would protect the young tsenpo, because if he died they would lose their link to royal power. The two clans check each other.
- Summary line from class: the queens of Tibet often came from powerful aristocratic clans in turn, and these marriages reinforced alliances with the kingdom.
- **Tibetan heirs from Tibetan mothers.** The instructor noted that the tsenpos' heirs were born to Tibetan women, not foreign queens. Scholars think the royal family may have deliberately avoided heirs by foreign queens. The exception people cite is Tri Songdetsen, whom some sources call the son of a Chinese princess. There is no firm proof, and he was officially claimed as the son of a Tibetan queen. Either way, the royal family seems to have been careful to keep a Tibetan-blood heir. To be discussed later.

### The Nepal alliance
- Tibetan sources don't record this. The instructor said it isn't in the Old Tibetan materials either, only in the Chinese Tang Annals, which briefly mention two things:
  1. King Narendradeva of Nepal was overthrown by his subjects and fled to Songtsen in Lhasa. He stayed a long time and brought builders and craftsmen, which is why Tibetan architecture was strongly shaped by Nepalese style. Songtsen later sent a force that defeated the usurper and put Narendradeva back on the throne. According to the Chinese sources, Nepal then became a vassal of Tibet and owed it tribute.
  2. In one sentence: it is said that Songtsen married a Nepalese princess. There is no name, no date and no details.
- Eleventh-century Tibetan histories, written about four centuries after the events by Buddhist scholars, supply a name and details (Bhrikuti or Tritsün in the readings) and attach religious roles to her. We don't know their sources, and much may be invented.
- Conclusion: the marriage probably happened, but its nature is unknown. Since Nepal was a vassal and lower in the hierarchy, the princess's role is also unclear.

### The Tang marriage: Princess Wencheng
**Tibetan perspective (the version in van Schaik)**
- The envoy was Gar Tongtsen, prime minister, from the Gar clan. He was sent to the Tang court and turned down at first.
- The instructor said Gar went three times. A student said he was refused twice.
- A rival request came from the Azha (Chinese: Tuyuhun), and the Tang promised the princess to them. Songtsen's army then defeated the Azha, and the Tang recognized Tibet as the stronger candidate.
- Why the early refusals: Tibet was a newly formed state that hadn't yet appeared on the world political stage. The Tang, a recentralized and powerful empire, treated a princess as a resource to be "invested" in strong partners.
- Songtsen attacked part of the Tang borderland and won. This surprised the emperor and convinced him that a strong new power had risen. The place name is unintelligible in the transcript.
- Speculation the instructor raised: the marriage was first proposed for Songtsen's son, not for Songtsen himself.
  - A tsenpo normally took the throne at 13. Songtsen handed the throne to his son, but the son died soon after, so Songtsen became tsenpo again.
  - With negotiations already under way, he took the bride himself.
- On Songtsen's age: later Tibetan scholars say he lived into his 80s. The instructor said historical and archaeological evidence points strongly to death in his 30s (the transcript is garbled; "32 or 39" was said).
- Why all the small neighbors wanted Tang brides: the Tang was "a big lion." A tie to China protected you from being attacked by China and could be used against your own neighbors.

**The riddle and test stories are legend**
- A student mentioned the story of picking out the princess from a line-up after getting a description from a helper. The instructor called this drama, invented later to entertain.
- The Tang court followed formal ritual. Decisions were long debated and then formally announced, so a marriage would never be decided by a guessing game. "It's good, but it's not the history."

**Chinese perspective (instructor's addition, not in van Schaik)**
- Emperor Taizong married princesses to several frontier states at around this time, including the Tuyuhun/Azha and Tibet, not just Tibet. The aim was stability on the western frontier.
- At this moment he was putting his armies and resources into a war to conquer Korea. He could not commit more troops in the west, so he used marriage to keep the western border quiet. That is what changed his decision.
- Aside: the instructor said Korea remained a vassal of China until China lost the war with Japan in the 19th century.

**How the marriage was later retold**
- Tibetan accounts elaborate the princess's journey from Chang'an to Lhasa (said to take years) and where Songtsen met her. These come from oral tradition and texts based on it.
- Chinese records give only about three sentences: the year she went, who escorted her, and that's all. She probably arrived within months, not years.
- The later Buddhist framing:
  - Songtsen married the Nepalese and Chinese princesses in order to bring Buddhism to Tibet, since both were Buddhists.
  - The Nepalese princess brought a Buddha statue. The Chinese princess brought another statue (installed in Ramoche, later moved to the Jokhang, where it still is), plus Buddhist texts, translators and scholars. The transcript is garbled on which statue went where; the reading-based version is given here.
- The instructor's view: this is late Tibetan Buddhist imagination, or expectation, which places Tibetan history inside a Buddhist framework. At this stage Songtsen was not a Buddhist and had no clear understanding of Buddhism. Why he later turned to Buddhism is the topic of the next class.

### No written language yet
- The Tibetans were strong militarily but not yet culturally. At this time they had spoken language only, no writing system.
- Gar Tongtsen was a nobleman from a powerful clan, yet he could not read. The reason was not lack of education: there was no Tibetan script to read. More detail next class.

### Naming systems: Tibet, Japan, China
- **Imperial Tibet**
  - Names like "Gar Tongtsen" are clan name + personal name. The instructor wrote a dot between them on the board.
  - Only noble families had clan names. Commoners had none.
  - Tibetan society was organized by clan, not by family.
- **Japan**
  - Similar to Tibet: commoners had no family names, and only nobles did.
  - Example: a member of the Fujiwara house, the key noble house of the Heian period, written "Fujiwara no [given name]". The transcript gives the given name as "Michikala/Michikara," most likely Fujiwara no Michinaga.
  - The Japanese government required everyone to take a surname only around the 1870s, so most modern Japanese surnames are recent and don't connect to old houses.
- **China**
  - For thousands of years the basic unit of society was the family. Every family had a surname, often tied to a family village, and family relationships came before all others.
- Why this matters: the Chinese read the royal marriage through family logic. By marrying in, Tibet took a place in the Tang "state family." This framing shaped later Chinese policy toward Tibet and the treaties the two signed. To be discussed later.

## Key people, places and terms
- **Songtsen Gampo**: 7th-century tsenpo (d. 649) who built the Tibetan empire; "Songtsen the Wise." van Schaik ch. 1; Kapstein ch. 3.
- **Tsenpo**: title of the Tibetan emperor. van Schaik ch. 1.
- **Zhangzhung**: ancient kingdom west of Songtsen's realm, conquered and absorbed as Western Tibet. van Schaik pp. 5–6; Kapstein ch. 3.
- **Sekarma / Semakar**: Songtsen's sister, married to the Zhangzhung king, later his spy. van Schaik p. 6 and n. 5; Kapstein ch. 3.
- **Ligmigya**: king of Zhangzhung killed in Songtsen's attack. Kapstein ch. 3.
- **Khyunglung (Silver Castle)**: Zhangzhung royal castle in the princess's song. van Schaik p. 6.
- **Narendradeva**: Nepalese king exiled in Lhasa in the 630s, restored by a Tibetan army as a vassal. van Schaik p. 6.
- **Bhrikuti / Tritsün**: Nepalese princess known only from later sources; her existence is questioned. van Schaik n. 6; Kapstein ch. 3.
- **Gar Tongtsen**: Songtsen's prime minister from the Gar clan, envoy to the Tang, later regent; "illiterate" per the Tang historians. van Schaik ch. 1; Kapstein ch. 3.
- **Taizong (Li Shimin)**: Tang emperor (r. 626–49) who gave the princess to Tibet. van Schaik pp. 7–9 and n. 7.
- **Princess Wencheng**: Tang princess married into the Tibetan royal house in 641. Kapstein ch. 3 (unnamed in van Schaik's main text).
- **Gungsong Gungtsen**: Songtsen's son, the intended groom of Wencheng; died in 646. Kapstein ch. 3; van Schaik n. 11.
- **Azha (Tuyuhun)**: tribal confederacy between Tibet and China and rival suitor for the Tang princess; defeated by Tibet in 638. van Schaik p. 7; Kapstein ch. 3.
- **Chang'an**: Tang capital. van Schaik p. 8.
- **Tang Annals**: Chinese dynastic history and the source for the Nepal episode. van Schaik n. 6.
- **Old Tibetan Annals / Old Tibetan Chronicle**: Tibetan imperial records found at Dunhuang. van Schaik plates and notes; Kapstein ch. 3; Dunhuang library cave article.
- **Jokhang**: Lhasa's oldest Buddhist temple, built on a Nepalese model; also called Rasa Trulnang / Tsuglagkhang. van Schaik p. 6 and n. 6.
- **Ramoche**: temple where Wencheng's Buddha statue was first placed. van Schaik p. 10.
- **Jowo Shakyamuni**: the Buddha statue credited to Wencheng. Kapstein ch. 3.
- **Tri Songdetsen**: later emperor (742–c. 797); later histories call him the son of a Chinese princess (Jincheng), which Kapstein calls "a pious legend." Kapstein ch. 3.
- **Bodh Gaya (Mahabodhi temple)**: site of the Buddha's enlightenment. Kapstein ch. 3.

## Writing and reading-skills guidance
- **Know your position.** A paper expresses your ideas from one starting point. You need a position to reach a firm conclusion, and you must not shift positions within the paper.
- **Know your blind zone.** Standing at the front, the instructor can see the class but not the screen behind him. Every position has a blind zone. To deal with it:
  - move your position a little to see more, or
  - ask others to look at your blind zone for you.
- **State your position.** In a longer paper, tell the reader your position. In a short one, at least be clear about it in your own mind.
- **Get readers for drafts.** Have other people read your draft so they can raise questions from your blind zone. The instructor called this a long-term writing skill and will return to it in the composition sessions. It connects to the syllabus's Diagnostic Essay (peer review) and the UNC "Getting Feedback" handout.
- **Don't judge only from your own perspective.** The Buddhists' hopes about Xi Jinping failed because they ignored other stakeholders' expectations. Apply the same check to your own arguments.
- **Criticize your sources.** Separate early evidence (Tang Annals, Old Tibetan Annals) from much later Tibetan histories, which may invent or fabricate details. Ask when a source was written, what its purpose was, and what it could actually know. Compare the Tibetan and Chinese sources on the same event.
