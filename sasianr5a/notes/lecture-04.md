### Legitimacy is necessary but not sufficient
- Review from last class: in the legitimacy story, the **tsenpo** (emperor) *receives* legitimacy, the priest or religious figure *confers* it, and the common people *follow*.
- A student suggested that people also need to be fed. The instructor extended the point: a regime needs **power** as well as legitimacy, meaning military force and economic resources (money, gold). The analogy was the U.S. government, whose legitimacy comes from the people but which still depends on power to govern.
- **Songtsen Gampo** started from military power. His father had legitimacy but was murdered, which left the throne contested. Songtsen's first step was to gather military force and secure the throne. (van Schaik: the father was poisoned when Songtsen was thirteen. Songtsen executed the killer and put down the insurgency, ch. 1, p. 4. Kapstein dates the assassination of the father, Namri Löntsen, to about 629, ch. 3.)
- The instructor cited a scholar's argument that great empires tend to be founded right after a major crisis, because a leader who survives one looks strong, tough-minded and blessed by divine power. *(The scholar's name is garbled in the transcript as "Professor De customer" and could not be matched to any course reading.)*
- At this stage Songtsen's state was small and new, far from a vast empire. Growth had **internal** drivers (putting down rebels, consolidating rule) and **external** ones (conquering neighbors).

### A state needs a center: the capital
- After consolidation, the next need is a **base**, a capital city. It is the "White House" of the state.
- This ties back to the **mandala model** from earlier lectures: the ruler sits at the center, and that center is a real physical place, not just a metaphor. For Songtsen Gampo it was Lhasa.
- Songtsen is credited with building the first palace on the site of today's **Potala**. The instructor said it was then called something like the "Red Palace." *(Caution: in van Schaik, "Red Palace" is the name of a later part of the Potala, finished under Desi Sangye Gyatso in the 1690s, p. 130. van Schaik only says the Potala "was built on the ruins of Songtsen Gampo's palace," p. 123.)*

### Three meanings of the name Lhasa
1. **Rasa, the early city.** *Sa* means soil or earth, so "place." *Ra* has two readings:
   - An enclosure or wall, so "place enclosed by walls." This is van Schaik's reading: "the town of Rasa. The name meant 'Walled City'" (ch. 1, p. 5).
   - "Goat," the more common folk explanation. The city was built on a lake, and goats carried earth to fill it in, so Rasa is the place built by goats.
   - The instructor's own speculation: goats and sheep symbolize auspiciousness in both Tibetan and Chinese culture, possibly through Chinese influence, though no firm connection can be claimed. In later Buddhist literature a key figure is said to have been born in a sheepfold, which marks the birth as auspicious. Some scholars study the role of animals in religion and history.
2. **Lhasa, the city of the tsenpo.** Pronunciation shifted from *ra* to *lha* (the instructor described a move from a retroflex to a dental sound). *Lha* has three meanings: (a) god or divine; (b) the royal family, meaning the tsenpo, who descends from the gods; (c) in Buddhism, a deity. So Lhasa is the divine place, the home of the divine tsenpo and his family, and it became a poetic way of naming the heart of the Tibetan state. (van Schaik, p. 5: "Rasa acquired a new, more dignified name: Lhasa, the 'Divine City'.")
3. **Lhasa, the city of the Dalai Lama (from the 17th century).**
   - After the empire collapsed in the 9th century, Tibet broke into small regimes and Lhasa stopped functioning as a capital.
   - The **Fifth Dalai Lama** (17th century), backed by the Manchu Qing emperor, reunified rule and rebuilt on the palace site. The first stage was the **White Palace**. The whole complex was later named the **Potala**.
   - "Potala" comes from the name of the realm of **Avalokiteshvara** (the Sanskrit form is Potalaka), the bodhisattva of **compassion**. The Dalai Lama is regarded as his manifestation. In India and Tibet Avalokiteshvara is male. In China the figure is female (Guanyin). The instructor said gender does not matter in the Buddhist symbolism.
   - The paired bodhisattva of **wisdom** is Manjushri (the name is garbled in the transcript). Compassion and wisdom are the two key elements of Mahayana, which will be covered later.
   - So *lha* here means a Buddhist deity, and Lhasa is the deity's city.
- **Main point:** a place name shifts with history: walled or goat city, then the city of the divine tsenpo, then the city of the Dalai Lama as Buddhist deity.

### Lhasa today and the 1959 question
- Lhasa is the capital of the Tibet Autonomous Region. The instructor said Tibet is "no longer an independent state" and has been part of China since 1959.
- Three landmarks were shown: the **Potala Palace**, which has been empty since the 14th Dalai Lama left, and two Buddhist temples from Songtsen Gampo's time, the **Jokhang** and **Ramoche**. Both will come up in class soon.
- A student asked why the Dalai Lama left. The instructor's answer, with a pointer to van Schaik chs. 9 and 10:
  - The 14th Dalai Lama signed an agreement with the Chinese central government (the Seventeen-Point Agreement of 1951 in van Schaik ch. 9) and later decided to break with it. An agreement is hard to undo from one side.
  - After the imperial period, Tibetan regimes had legitimacy but lacked their own military and economic base, so they relied on outside powers. The Sakya relied on the Mongols, the Pagmodru on Ming China, and the Gelug (Ganden Phodrang) government on the Manchu Qing.
  - Seeing no hope of resisting, he left in 1959 for India, where he has stayed since.

### The mobile court: sedentary, nomadic, or in between?
- The Chinese economy rested on agriculture, and China was a **sedentary** society with fixed capitals such as Chang'an (later Beijing). Occasional moves between capitals did not change that. The instructor compared this to the Delhi and Calcutta capitals of British India.
- The **Mongols** were **nomads**, moving constantly with huge tents, some holding a thousand people.
- Tibetans, and the tsenpo in particular, were **semi-nomadic**, somewhere in between. The Tibetan annals show that the tsenpo had a fixed capital in Lhasa but also kept several other residences and moved his great tented camp with soldiers, guards, counselors and servants, usually once a year and sometimes twice. He sometimes traveled toward Nepal and possibly India. (van Schaik p. 5: the court, "true to nomadic tradition, moved around Central Tibet in great tented encampments." See also p. 43: "a mobile residence, a vast tented encampment, with the tsenpo's tent in the very middle.")
- **Why keep moving?** The class came up with several reasons, and the instructor stressed that the answer has more than one dimension:
  - **Political:** show his charisma and win people's hearts. Most land belonged to his ministers and clan leaders, so he had to appear in their territories.
  - **Security:** a ruler on the move is harder to assassinate.
  - **Economic:** make sure taxes, which the ministers collected, were properly gathered and that he received his share.

### Map: Tibet and its neighbors in the 7th century
- The map combined 7th-century Tang-era borders with modern place names. It is not historically exact but helps orientation. The spelling "Lhasa" follows standard romanization of Tibetan *lha*. The *h* is silent for English speakers.
- In the 7th century Tibet was only a small part of the gray area the map labels "Tibet."
- **China (Tang dynasty):** the instructor listed the great periods of Chinese history as Qin (which gave China its name), Han, Tang and Qing. The Tang was founded slightly before Songtsen's state.
- **Azha** (Chinese name Tuyuhun): a small state between Tibet and China, founded by non-Han people and strongly influenced by China.
- A second nomadic or semi-nomadic regime to the north, similar to the Azha, which later disappeared. *The name is unintelligible in the transcript. It is probably the Sumpa, whom Kapstein describes as "a nomadic or seminomadic people living to the northeast of Tibet" (ch. 3, p. 60), but this is not certain.*
- **South and west:** Nepal, India, Kashmir, Ladakh, and the site of later Bhutan (the region existed but the state did not yet). **Zhangzhung** lay between Nepal and Tibet to the west, a large and strong kingdom that the instructor said may have had cultural ties to Persia, citing van Schaik. Mongolian peoples lay to the north.
- **Strategy:** Songtsen was ambitious. He tried to build good relations with the large powers and to conquer the small kingdoms around him. Besides force, his most useful tool was **marriage**.

## Key people, places and terms

- **Tsenpo**: title of the Tibetan emperor, understood as descended from the gods (van Schaik ch. 1).
- **Songtsen Gampo**: 7th-century tsenpo who consolidated the Tibetan state and made Lhasa its center (van Schaik ch. 1; Kapstein ch. 3).
- **Namri Löntsen**: Songtsen's father, assassinated around 629 (Kapstein ch. 3; unnamed in van Schaik p. 4, where he is poisoned).
- **Rasa**: early name of Lhasa, "Walled City" (van Schaik p. 5; Kapstein glossary *ra sa*).
- **Lhasa**: "Divine City," the new name Rasa took as the tsenpo's power grew (van Schaik p. 5).
- **Lha**: god or divine, the royal line, or a Buddhist deity (class; van Schaik p. 5 for "Divine City").
- **Potala Palace**: palace of the Dalai Lamas built on the ruins of Songtsen's palace and named after Avalokiteshvara's realm (van Schaik pp. 123–124).
- **White Palace**: first part of the Potala, built under the Fifth Dalai Lama (van Schaik p. 124; Kapstein Plate 18).
- **Red Palace**: in van Schaik, the later part of the Potala, finished in the 1690s (p. 130). The instructor used the name for Songtsen's original palace.
- **Avalokiteshvara**: bodhisattva of compassion and patron of Tibet, of whom the Dalai Lama is an emanation (van Schaik p. 123).
- **Guanyin**: the Chinese, female form of the bodhisattva of compassion (van Schaik index, p. 191 area; ch. 9).
- **Manjushri**: bodhisattva of wisdom (van Schaik, e.g. ch. 5).
- **Fifth Dalai Lama**: 17th-century ruler who reunified Tibet with Qing backing and built the Potala (van Schaik ch. 6).
- **Jokhang**: Lhasa's oldest Buddhist temple, built on a Nepalese model in the 630s and originally called the Rasa Trulnang (van Schaik p. 6 and ch. 1 n. 6; Kapstein ch. 3).
- **Ramoche**: early Lhasa temple built to house a Buddha statue later moved to the Jokhang (van Schaik ch. 1; Kapstein ch. 3).
- **Zhangzhung**: kingdom west of Tibet with "a distinct Persian flavour," conquered by Songtsen (van Schaik pp. 5–6; Kapstein ch. 3).
- **Azha (Tuyuhun)**: confederacy of tribes between Tibet and Tang China (van Schaik p. 5; Kapstein ch. 3, pp. 60–61).
- **Sumpa**: nomadic or semi-nomadic people to the northeast of Tibet (Kapstein ch. 3, p. 60).
- **Tang dynasty**: Chinese dynasty that was just rising in Songtsen's time (van Schaik p. 5).
- **Semakar**: Songtsen's sister, married to the Zhangzhung king, who later spied for Songtsen (Kapstein ch. 3; unnamed in van Schaik p. 6).
- **Tritsün / Bhrikuti**: Nepalese princess said to have married Songtsen, known only from later accounts (Kapstein ch. 3, p. 62).
- **Princess of Wencheng**: Tang princess sent in 641, first intended for Songtsen's son (Kapstein ch. 3, p. 61).
- **Semi-nomadic**: in class, the tsenpo's pattern of a fixed capital combined with a moving tented court (van Schaik pp. 5 and 43).
- **Mandala model**: the ruler at the physical and symbolic center of the realm (earlier lectures; class).
- **Seventeen-Point Agreement (1951)**: the agreement the instructor referred to in explaining the 1959 departure (van Schaik ch. 9).
- **Pagmodru / Sakya / Gelug**: post-imperial ruling groups that the instructor said depended on Ming, Mongol and Qing backing respectively (van Schaik chs. 4–6).

## Writing and reading-skills guidance

- **Define your key terms.** The humanities work with concepts, and many debates and confusions come from writers never defining their terms. Draw the boundary: what is inside the concept and what is outside it.
- **When reading academic papers,** find the key concepts and ask what the author means by each one.
- **When writing,** say exactly what you mean by your central terms.
- **Example: "China."** Does it mean a regime, a region, a place, or a people? Who counts as "Chinese"? The instructor contrasted the U.S. model of ethnic groups with clear boundaries and distinct identities with the Chinese model, a Han majority plus 55 other groups under a vaguer umbrella idea of China. The instructor argued that Western and Chinese scholars often talk past each other because they use "China" differently.
- **Place names are historical.** Track how a name like Lhasa changes meaning across periods, and do not assume one fixed meaning.
- **Look for more than one cause.** The reasons the court kept moving were political, security-related and economic all at once. The instructor said historical explanations are "not always aligned with one dimension."
