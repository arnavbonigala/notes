# Lecture 19: From the Buddha's Death to the Four Noble Truths: Councils, Canon, and the Indian Vocabulary Tibet Inherited

## Summary

This session covered the Indian Buddhist background that the course needs before it returns to Tibetan material. The instructor moved through four blocks: how the Buddha's teachings were gathered after his death at the three councils, the Three Jewels as the formal definition of being a Buddhist, four pan-Indian concepts that Buddhism borrowed rather than invented (karma, samsara, moksha, dharma), and the Four Noble Truths with an extended analysis of dukkha. Two threads tie back to Tibet. First, King Ashoka as the model of the Buddhist dharma king, which later Tibetan scholars used to shape the image of Songtsen Gampo. Second, the distinction between the gradual path and the instantaneous path, which the instructor flagged as the live issue for chapter 2 and Trisong Detsen's reign.

A note on sources: most of this lecture's content is Indian Buddhist background that does not appear in the extracted course readings. The Gethin *Foundations of Buddhism* file in readings-text/ is empty, so the council names, the Four Noble Truths and the three types of dukkha could not be checked against an assigned text. Where I could verify a term in van Schaik or Kapstein I have cited it below. Spellings of the Indian terms are my reconstruction of what the transcriber mangled, and several passages were unintelligible and are marked as such.

## Lecture notes

### Finishing the Buddha's biography

The opening picked up mid-story. Austerities did not bring the Buddha to enlightenment, so he abandoned them and went to Bodh Gaya, sat under the tree, and made a vow not to rise until he attained his goal. (Bodh Gaya appears in Kapstein, p. 4111 of the extracted text, as "the site of the Buddha's enlightenment.") After enlightenment he travelled across a large territory in India teaching people how to reach enlightenment themselves. One clause here is garbled in the transcript and I cannot recover it: "before the conquered the Chinese eventually he gets invited."

### Compassion is not a Mahayana invention

The instructor pushed back hard on a common misreading. Because Mahayana Buddhism is described through two core elements, compassion and wisdom, students often conclude that early Buddhism, Theravada or mainstream Buddhism, lacked compassion. He called this "completely wrong." Compassion, or altruism, is present from the Buddha's own time and is a foundation of Buddhism as such.

His evidence was biographical. When the Buddha left home he was criticized heavily, because Indian society cherished social relationships, position, obligations and duties, and he had abandoned his roles as son, husband, father and future king. The Buddha's answer was that many people could take up those responsibilities, but he wanted to pursue full enlightenment so that afterwards he could help not only his family, his wife, his son and his beloved servants, but everybody. The altruistic orientation is therefore built into the founding act, not added later.

### The three councils

The Buddha's discourses were not written down during his lifetime. Only after his death did his disciples gather to preserve them. The instructor numbered three councils.

**First council.** A few days after the Buddha's death, at Rajagriha. He noted that the site is still a place of worship today. The work was the recitation of the canon by Upali and Ananda, plus discussion of major and minor rules.

He stressed that these two names matter because each is tied to a specific part of the canon. Recall the "three baskets," the Tripitaka: sutra, vinaya and abhidharma. (Kapstein gives Tripitaka as the Buddhist canon at p. 6823 of the extracted text, and lists Vinaya as the monastic code and Abhidharma as the "meta-doctrine" at p. 14633.)

- **Upali** was the expert in Vinaya, the monastic precepts, so he recited the Vinaya texts.
- **Ananda** was the Buddha's cousin and became his closest attendant. Because he accompanied the Buddha for a long period and heard enormous numbers of teachings in person, he is a witness. He was also famous for an exceptional memory, able to recall things after hearing them once or twice. He therefore took charge of the sutras. (The transcript garbles the praise of his memory: "Fokke is a good memory. He can recall all things. He has an idea.")

**The Ananda connection to the Heart Sutra assignment.** The instructor turned to the assigned Heart Sutra and worked on its opening lines. The first line in the version students read is a homage to the Perfection of Wisdom, the Blessed Mother. He pointed out that this homage is **not** in the original Sanskrit or the Chinese text; it is a later insertion, and he said the class will return to why.

The line that matters structurally is the next one, which he had the class read aloud: "Thus have I heard at one time," rendered in older translations as "Thus have I heard." He then ran a short Socratic sequence on it. The "I" is not the Buddha; it is a disciple, specifically Ananda. From this he drew three points:

1. It identifies the reporter. Ananda heard the Buddha, and it is his account.
2. It functions as a **disclaimer**. The formula says "this is how I heard it," not "this is what I correctly understood." The reporter cannot guarantee his memory or his comprehension.
3. It is a genre marker. "Thus have I heard at one time" signals a sutra, as distinct from a shastra, a commentary, or a Vinaya text. He estimated the formula opens roughly seventy percent of Buddhist sutras, but warned that not all sutras have it.

He illustrated the unreliability of oral transmission with the childhood telephone game: a sentence passed person to person, and by the last person it is badly altered. This is why oral transmission changes and why it is unreliable.

**Second council.** About one hundred years after the Buddha's death, at Vaishali. He explicitly told the class to take notes here. The substance was a dispute, not a compilation. After a century, people had come to understand and interpret the Buddhist precepts and vows differently, and disagreed about how to observe them properly. He noted that even today, visiting Thailand or elsewhere, you find different Buddhist branches, and the difference is fundamentally about observance. The actual disputed points, as he described them, were things like whether monks could accept gold or silver and other details of the monastic code. He declined to go deeper, saying such issues "sound ridiculous" to a non-practitioner and are hard to evaluate from outside. He added a research warning, noted below under writing guidance.

**Third council.** Sponsored and pushed by King Ashoka. The aim was to resolve disputes between schools, in particular the **Mahasamghika**, "the great assembly," and the **Sthavira**, "the elders."

**Comparison across the three.** He asked the class to compare them beyond the obvious differences of date, place and sponsor. A student gave the answer he wanted: the first was about getting the material written down at all, while the later two debated specifics of the texts. He confirmed and sharpened it. The first council gathered and fixed the content, sutras included. The second and third were disputes over interpretation, and specifically over Vinaya rather than sutra. The sutra corpus stayed comparatively stable and was not the object of serious dispute at these gatherings.

### Ashoka, the dharma king, and Songtsen Gampo

He paused on Ashoka and asked a student to summarize him in three sentences. The student's answer: Ashoka conquered a large part of what is now India plus parts of Pakistan and a little of eastern Asia, and set up pillars at the borders to mark his territory. The instructor accepted this and added that Ashoka was the first great emperor to unite the subcontinent into something empire-scale, and that the empire was not colonial in structure but worked on a mandala-like model, with the ruler at the center surrounded by differing peripheries. Ashoka sponsored and supported Buddhism and converted to it before his death, which is why the third council is sponsored by him. (Kapstein, p. 2034 of the extracted text, dates Ashoka to the third century BCE and notes his edicts were inscribed in early Brahmi, the script family from which Tibetan writing ultimately derives.)

He then asked which Tibetan figure Ashoka should remind them of. A student answered **Songtsen Gampo**, correctly. His clarification was careful and worth getting right: he is *not* claiming Songtsen Gampo deliberately imitated Ashoka. He is claiming that the **image** of Songtsen Gampo, as created or shaped by later Tibetan Buddhist scholars, was modelled on Ashoka. This is exactly van Schaik's position on p. 13: it is "unlikely that Songtsen really did create a new Buddhist system of law," but "the image of the great empire-building king who was also a compassionate Buddhist proved a powerful one."

Why Ashoka as the template? Because he is regarded as the first **dharmaraja**, dharma king. The duties of a dharma king, as the instructor listed them: protect Buddhism, sponsor and spread Buddhism, and involve himself in the affairs of the sangha, including helping to resolve the sangha's disputes and conflicts and settling questions of Vinaya. That last duty is precisely what the third council was.

He told the class to remember two paired terms, **dharmaraja** and **chakravartin**, and said chakravartin comes up in chapter 2. He called the dharma king a key term in Buddhist political history across the whole tradition, with great royal sponsors in China and Japan (the Japanese *tennō*) shaping themselves on the model. The specific Chinese emperor he named is unintelligible in the transcript ("the most the greatest one the 10"), as is an earlier figure he gestured at on the slide ("a fellow Zarek Eshwala"), which from context is another title or epithet of Ashoka.

### The Three Jewels

The three refuges or Three Jewels: Buddha, Dharma, Sangha. (Kapstein, p. 14284 of the extracted text, gives this as "the Three Precious Jewels (Sanskrit *triratna*, Tib. *könchok-sum*): the Buddha, his teaching (Sanskrit *dharma*, Tib. *chö*), and the religious community (Sanskrit *sangha*, Tib. *gendün*)," and notes that in Tibet the lama is often added as a fourth refuge.)

**Buddha.** Reviewing an earlier distinction, he separated:
- the historical Buddha, Shakyamuni;
- capital-B Buddha, meaning other beings who have reached enlightenment, such as the Medicine Buddha, Manjushri, and Maitreya as the future Buddha;
- the bodhisattva, who aspires to become a Buddha but is not one yet.

He added that this is a Mahayana account. In the Theravada framing there is only one Buddha, Shakyamuni, and the pre-enlightenment Shakyamuni is the bodhisattva.

**Dharma.** He said dharma has many senses in Buddhist usage but can be reduced to three categories. Outside the Buddhist context, in Indian philosophy generally, dharma means the principle or rule of the world. Within Buddhism:

1. Dharma as the **teaching** of the Buddha.
2. Dharma as **truth**, the law of the universe. He stressed that this means the Buddhist interpretation is *the* truth, as against truths proposed by other traditions.
3. Dharma as the indivisible, irreducible **constituents of reality**, the basic units out of which material things are composed. This third sense was new to the class.

He worked sense three out through a question: what is the defining feature of a basic unit? He used "atom" as his analogy. A student supplied that it cannot be broken down, and he confirmed: indivisible, inseparable. He then pressed, since atoms can in fact be divided; a student offered **quarks**, and when asked whether quarks are the smallest, answered that no method of dividing them has been found so people think they cannot be. He accepted this and remarked he might replace "atom" with a better example next time. The feature to remember: indivisible and self-contained. He flagged that this will matter when the class discusses impermanence.

**Sangha.** He listed four groups, two monastic and two lay:
- monastic: bhikshu (monk), bhikshuni (nun);
- lay: upasaka (male lay disciple), upasika (female lay disciple).

**How one becomes a Buddhist.** Taking refuge, which means making a vow before the Three Jewels: a statue, image or picture of the Buddha representing the Buddha; a Buddhist scripture representing the Dharma; and members of the sangha, in practice the monastics, representing the Sangha. The vow is that from now on one is a Buddhist and accepts Buddhist doctrine. (Compare Kapstein, p. 14284: "One must turn from worldly activities to religion by taking refuge in the Three Precious Jewels.")

### Four pan-Indian concepts: karma, samsara, moksha, dharma

He framed these as the Indian cultural inheritance. Buddhism grew up in India and out of Indian culture, so it rests on an existing philosophical foundation. These four terms are universal in the Indian cultural sphere, not exclusive to Buddhism, but Buddhism adopted all four.

**Karma.** Etymologically from the Sanskrit verbal root *kṛ*, "to do, to make." He noted this root is the standard way Sanskrit builds verbs out of nouns: noun plus "do" equals verb. As a noun it becomes *karma*, meaning action.

He then asked what counts as action for a single person, bracketing interaction with others, and built the standard threefold scheme:

1. **Body.** Bodily movement: shaking a limb, stretching the legs, shaking hands.
2. **Speech.** Talking, reciting.
3. **Mind.** Thought. Invisible and inaudible, but still action, and the most subtle of the three.

The ordering runs from gross to subtle, and he stressed that all Buddhist practice is practice of body, speech and mind. His example of bodily discipline was **tapas**, austerity: fasting, restricted diet, never lying down to sleep. He noted this last is still practiced in the Himalayan region, where practitioners sit in a wooden box too small to lie down in.

The second layer of karma is **cause and effect**. His demonstration: if I shake your hand and smile, you receive a kind attitude, feel happy, and reciprocate. If I hit or insult you, the same mechanism runs with the opposite content. As long as there is a cause there is a result. He was emphatic on the point students most often get wrong: karma does **not** mean life is doomed or predestined. It means that where there is a cause there is a result, and that *you* are the one who manipulates the cause, which is to say your body, speech and mind.

**Samsara.** The result then becomes the cause of the next result, and so on without end. This is a causal chain, and that chain is samsara. He corrected the popular usage directly: samsara is not simply "this world." Our world is a kind of samsara, but the term means the causal chain that keeps functioning, and as long as you lack the power to stop it you are conditioned by it, roaming inside it. (Kapstein, p. 14270 of the extracted text, gives samsara as the "perpetual round of rebirth," Tib. *khorwa*, with one's condition in any lifetime determined by past karma, Tib. *lé*. Van Schaik, p. 37, describes the aim of Mahayana Buddhists as being "to transcend samsara, the vicious cycle of suffering.")

**Moksha.** Breaking the chain. To liberate, to separate, to transcend, to go beyond conditioned existence. He noted that **nirvana** is likewise not originally a Buddhist term but a pre-existing Indian one, and started to name another tradition's equivalent term for liberation, but the word is unintelligible in the transcript ("hivarana").

**Dharma, and how the four fit together.** He asked the class to state the logical relation among the four in one sentence. One student proposed that from the repeating cycle you reach nirvana, which is enlightenment, and that dharma lets you see the truth. Another proposed that dharma comes after liberation. A third framing, which he drew out, was dharma as an instrument or method by which you attain liberation. He told the students they were all correct and then assembled the sequence:

> We have karma. Because we have karma, we experience samsara. In order to break samsara, we need dharma. The result is moksha.

But, he added, dharma is not only the instrument of enlightenment; dharma also means truth. And this is where the traditions split:

- **Theravada**: dharma is an instrument and a method. You practice dharma and only afterwards get enlightened. This is the **gradual path**. He noted the traditional framing that one practices for three incalculable aeons before attaining the goal.
- **Mahayana**: enlightenment *is* seeing the truth, and dharma *is* the truth, so dharma is not a means toward enlightenment but is itself enlightenment. He called this the result path or **instant path**.

He explicitly flagged this as the setup for chapter 2 and Trisong Detsen's reign, saying "we will encounter soon in our chapter two," and promised a fuller discussion then. This is the Samye debate (see Connections, below).

### The Four Noble Truths and the three types of dukkha

The first thing the Buddha taught, and an assigned reading. The four:

1. The truth of **dukkha**, suffering.
2. The truth of the **origin** of dukkha.
3. The truth of the **cessation** of dukkha.
4. The truth of the **path**, *marga*, leading to the cessation of dukkha.

He identified the key term: everything is centered on dukkha. So the class worked on dukkha itself. English synonyms the class produced: suffering, pain, unpleasantness, "not something happy."

He then sorted examples using the body/speech/mind scheme and arrived at three distinct kinds of dukkha.

**First type: dukkha as ordinary pain.** The obvious kind. Physical pain, being beaten or tortured, whether human or animal. Sickness. For mind, mental suffering: people cry because they are suffering. His examples were the loss of family and bereavement, and he spoke about still missing someone who has died. Also thwarted expectation: wanting a good job and not getting it, applying to Harvard, being rejected, and "having no choice" but to come to UC Berkeley. He also described the anxiety of foreign scholars and scientists applying for US visas, worrying about additional screening. Everyone shares this kind, and it is immediately recognizable.

**Second type: dukkha as change.** Not pain, and much subtler. His thirst example: you are very thirsty, you drink a bottle of water, you feel refreshed and happy, the thirst is solved. But if he forces you to drink three bottles you become sick and annoyed. Refreshment has turned into suffering. The point is the change itself, from the good state to its opposite. Other examples: being healthy feels good, then you catch a cold. The good state cannot be maintained forever. His sharpest example was social media influencers, who must constantly check follower counts; rising numbers feel good, declining attention produces real anxiety, and the suffering lies in the shift. He asked whether students notice this kind of suffering in themselves, and expected that many do not, because it is subtle.

He connected this to the basic unavoidable change: **aging**. Medical technology and money can slow the process, but cannot halt it.

**Third type: dukkha as conditioning, and pervasive.** He asked what *causes* the change. A student suggested things just change naturally. He reframed: it means you have lost control of it. You can slow aging but not stop it. Everyone dies, and despite thousands of years of people spending fortunes seeking immortality, no one has succeeded. No control means we are **conditioned**, subject to conditions.

His immediate example was the class itself. You must come on time, follow the syllabus, engage, do weekly assignments. Every Thursday evening the announcement arrives that your assignment has been graded; you open it and read the score, and that moment is dukkha. Some students see a good score and are happy, which is itself subject to change. Others see a bad one, which is dukkha of the first type. Either way, you belong to a set of conditions. As long as we are conditioned by something, we suffer.

He closed on the pervasiveness claim: dukkha is pervasive, everywhere. The objection "but I am happy, I have no suffering" does not defeat it, because you are still subject to change and still subject to conditioning. Using all three types to view the world is what makes the first Noble Truth intelligible.

The session ended mid-topic, with the remaining three truths deferred.

## Connections to the readings

**The gradual path and the instant path are the setup for the Samye debate, van Schaik pp. 37 to 38.** This is the most important link in the lecture. The instructor's Theravada-versus-Mahayana framing of dharma as means versus dharma as truth is the abstract form of the concrete historical conflict in chapter 2. Van Schaik: both Moheyan (Chinese Chan or Zen) and Kamalashila (Indian) "were good Buddhists of the Greater Vehicle" and agreed that samsara "was a product of deluded thoughts and emotions." What they disagreed about was "how that insight was to be realised." Moheyan argued that only stopping ordinary thought ends samsara, and that teachings on the practice of virtue were "for his duller disciples." Kamalashila argued that "real wisdom came about through analytical insight" and that abandoning analysis "would be to cut off wisdom at its root," dismissing Moheyan's method as "a practice of stupidity." Victory went to the Indian side, and "the tsenpo would now support only those who taught the path of gradual practice, and would reject the instantaneous approach of the Zen teachers."

Two things van Schaik adds that the lecture did not: the consequence that "Buddhist scriptures from India would be preferred over those from China," and the historiographical caveat that "this, anyway, is the Tibetan version of the story," since Chinese versions have the tsenpo blessing the Chinese side and modern scholars question whether a single formal debate happened at all.

**The Heart Sutra assignment reappears in van Schaik, p. 37.** Van Schaik uses the Heart Sutra to define emptiness: "form is emptiness, and emptiness is form." Pair this with the instructor's reading of the sutra's opening formula. You now have two angles on the same assigned text, its philosophical content and its textual construction.

**Songtsen Gampo and Ashoka, van Schaik p. 13.** The instructor's claim about constructed images is van Schaik's claim. Van Schaik: "For later Tibetans, who came to see Songtsen as the first of the great Buddhist kings, his lawmaking went hand in hand with Buddhist ethics. Though it seems unlikely that Songtsen really did create a new Buddhist system of law, the image of the great empire-building king who was also a compassionate Buddhist proved a powerful one." Van Schaik's index points to "dharma kings 30 to 33, 120, 150, 204" for the fuller treatment.

**Chakravartin, Kapstein p. 7749.** The term the instructor told you to pair with dharmaraja. Kapstein's instance is much later than Ashoka: Pakpa "granted tantric initiation on the emperor, thereby symbolically annointing him as a Chakravartin, a 'wheel-turner,' or universal monarch," which "served as an important precedent for the later ceremonial relations of Tibetan hierarchs with China's rulers." This is useful evidence for the instructor's larger claim that the dharma-king idiom runs across the whole history, not just the imperial period.

**Karma, samsara and the Three Jewels in Tibetan practice, Kapstein p. 14270 and following.** Kapstein gives the Tibetan terms for everything the lecture covered in Sanskrit: samsara / *khorwa*, karma / *lé*, nirvana / *nyangdé*, triratna / *könchok-sum*, dharma / *chö*, sangha / *gendün*. He also shows the practical form these take in Tibet, merit gained through donations, lamps and incense, recitation, prostration and circumambulation, and ransoming animals from the butcher. Note the one addition the lecture did not mention: in Tibet the lama is often added "as a fourth refuge."

**Ashoka's edicts and the Tibetan script, Kapstein p. 2034.** A second, indirect Ashoka connection to Tibet. The Tibetan writing system derives from North Indian or Central Asian scripts descended from Brahmi, and early Brahmi "had been used to inscribe many of the edicts of the famous Indian monarch of the third century B.C.E., Ashoka."

**The Tripitaka in Tibetan monastic education, Kapstein pp. 6823 and 14633.** Kapstein shows the three baskets as a live curriculum: Vinaya (*dülwa*), Abhidharma (*ngönpa*), Prajnaparamita (*pharchin*) and Madhyamaka (*uma*), beginning with rote memorization of key texts, "of which an accomplished scholar might learn thousands of pages by heart." This is a useful counterweight to the lecture's telephone-game point about unreliable transmission.

**Nothing in the readings covers the three councils or the Four Noble Truths.** I grepped all of readings-text/ for the council sites, Upali, Ananda, Mahasamghika, Sthavira, "Four Noble Truths" and dukkha, and found nothing. The Gethin *Foundations of Buddhism* file is empty. If you need a citable source for this material in an essay, you will need the actual Gethin text or another assigned source. Do not cite the lecture.

## Writing and reading-skills guidance

This was a content-heavy session with no formal writing instruction. What there was came embedded in the historical material, and it is worth extracting because it is methodological.

**Oral transmission is unreliable, and good texts say so about themselves.** The reading of "Thus have I heard at one time" as a disclaimer is a close-reading move you can reuse. The formula marks the boundary of what the reporter can vouch for: he transmits what he heard, not what he correctly understood. The telephone-game analogy makes the same point about chains of transmission generally. Applied to your own writing, this is the habit UNC's Evaluating Print Sources handout is after: ask who is reporting, at what remove, and what they are and are not claiming to know. Van Schaik models the same scruple at p. 38 with "this, anyway, is the Tibetan version of the story."

**Formulaic openings are genre markers, so use them as evidence.** The instructor's third point about the sutra formula was that it tells you what kind of text you are holding: sutra, not shastra, not Vinaya. He also qualified it immediately, roughly seventy percent, and not all sutras have it. That combination, a pattern plus its honest limits, is the model for how to deploy textual evidence in a paragraph. See UNC Writing, Evidence.

**Define key terms before arguing with them.** Almost the whole lecture was definitional work, and the instructor repeatedly rejected loose popular usage: samsara is not just "this world," karma is not fate, compassion is not a Mahayana innovation. Each correction took the form of naming the common misreading and then showing what the term actually does. That is a usable paragraph structure.

**A warning about research topics.** When the class asked about the Vinaya disputes at the second council, he said: "You are welcome to do this research, but I need to warn you guys, precepts are a difficult topic, because unless you are an insider it is very difficult for you to understand." Take this as an advisory on midterm topic selection. The monastic code is a genuinely hard subject to write on from outside, and the course readings give you very little purchase on it. Pick something the readings actually support.

**Note-taking cues.** He was explicit twice. At the second council he said "I want you to take notes" on the nature of the dispute. On the three features of the basic unit of matter, indivisible and self-contained, he said "remember this," because it returns in the discussion of impermanence.
