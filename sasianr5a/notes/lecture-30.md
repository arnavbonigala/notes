# Lecture 30: Who Wrote the Samye Debate? Testing the Testament of Ba as Historical Evidence

## Summary

This session closed out the Samye debate unit by turning from the content of the debate to the reliability of the one source that transmits it. The instructor first finished the Padmasambhava story (he exits the narrative without a recorded death, and is reframed as a living culture hero whose tantric tradition is pragmatic and ritual-centered rather than scholastic). He then asked the class a source-critical question: where does the story of the Samye debate actually come from? The answer is the *Testament of Ba* (Tibetan *dBa' bzhed* / *sBa bzhed*), attributed to Ba Salnang, the nobleman who invited Shantarakshita from Nepal and who travelled to China. The lecture then dismantled that attribution. The text covers events decades after Ba Salnang's lifetime, survives in four recensions of different lengths, and has a Dunhuang fragment that van Schaik dated to around the eleventh century. The conclusion was deliberately negative: the evidence is real but contaminated, so we cannot recover what was said at Samye. The methodological payoff was the point of the class, and it maps directly onto the course's three-dimension framework. Dimension one (what happened) is closed; dimension two (how different traditions narrate it) starts next class; dimension three (your own interpretation) is the essay.

## Lecture notes

### Finishing Padmasambhava: a figure with no death date

The instructor picked up mid-story from the previous session. Padmasambhava is pushed out of Tibet, and the court sends archers after him, but he cannot be killed or poisoned and the pursuers cannot find him. Where he went is not recorded. The instructor noted that when he introduced Padmasambhava he gave neither a birth date nor a death date in brackets, because there is no record of his death in the tradition. For Tibetan, Himalayan and other Buddhist followers he has not died: he is still present in the world and manifests to practitioners. The instructor's framing was that Padmasambhava functions as a culture hero rather than as a datable historical individual.

(van Schaik gives the court's rumor campaign, the archers frozen "like paintings," Padmasambhava's angry parting speech, and his later elevation to Guru Rinpoche, the Precious Teacher.)

### Tantric practice as pragmatic religion

By the middle of the eighth century, the instructor said, tantric practice already exists in Tibet and is becoming a tradition or school in its own right. He drew a comparison with Chinese practice: the emphasis is not on intellectual engagement or scholastic exercise but on ritual, power, purification, and acting on karma and cause and effect. His word for it was pragmatic and practical.

He illustrated this with living practice in Tibetan and Himalayan communities. People consult monks and lamas as diviners. The instructor named the practice *mo*, a dice divination: the lama throws the dice, reads the result, and gives a prophecy, then prescribes a ritual to perform in response. Kapstein's *The Tibetans* confirms the term, captioning a photograph of "a diviner (mopa) on a Lhasa sidewalk" consulting an astrological almanac with a hand-drum above his stand.

### Not solving the problem: closing dimension one

The instructor returned to the course plan and made the structure explicit. We have accumulated problems around the Samye debate and we are not going to solve them. The options he put to the class were, first, keep trying to find the truth of what happened, and second, do something else. The second route he described as asking to what extent this debate is really history, and what really happened.

He laid out two research approaches for the first route. One is archival collection, finding documents distributed across different places and countries and pulling them together. The other is cross-regional and trans-regional engagement, which is what you do if you refuse to confine the question to Tibet. But before either, he insisted on a prior question.

### Where does the story come from?

The instructor pressed the class repeatedly: who tells the story of the Samye debate, and from what kind of text? Student guesses included Samye itself, a royal text, and scripture. He rejected each. There is no royal text tradition in Tibet of the kind being imagined, and the source is not a Buddhist scripture in the canonical sense; it is a historical text.

The question that unlocked it was: which person went to India to invite Shantarakshita? The answer is Ba Salnang, and the text is the *Testament of Ba*. The instructor gave the Tibetan title as *bazhed* / *bashe*, noted that it appears in variant forms (the class heard both *dBa' bzhed* and *sBa bzhed* pronunciations), and said the standard English rendering is the *Testament of Ba* or the *Testament of Wa*.

Note one discrepancy worth checking against the reading. The instructor said repeatedly that Ba Salnang went to India to invite Shantarakshita. van Schaik writes that Ba Selnang "travelled to Nepal, where he met an eminent Buddhist abbot called Shantarakshita, and invited him to Tibet," and that Shantarakshita was later sent back to Nepal when disasters struck. Kapstein's glossary gives both titles: *Testament of Ba* = *sba bzhed*, *Testament of Wa* = *dba' bzhed*.

### Ba Salnang: who the attributed author was

The instructor built a profile of the attributed author, because the profile is what makes the attribution look plausible at first.

- He is the man sent to invite Shantarakshita.
- He also went to China to invite a Chinese Buddhist monk. There he encountered a Korean Chan monk who persuaded him of the value of Chan teachings and convinced him that Chinese Chan was a good tradition. (van Schaik names this monk Reverend Kim, "a famous Zen teacher," who advised Salnang on which scriptures to show the tsenpo and taught him Zen meditation.)
- The instructor flagged a caution here: it is historically established that Ba Salnang went to China to invite a Chinese monk, but the claim that he invited Moheyan specifically does not hold. Moheyan was brought to Tibet under compulsion. The transcript is garbled at this point, but the instructor's words were "forced or maybe kidnapped," and his conclusion was that we cannot sustain the claim that Ba Salnang invited Moheyan. (van Schaik, more mildly, says one result of the journey was that Zen teachers were invited to Tibet, and that Moheyan had been teaching in Dunhuang, the Silk Route city conquered by Trisong Detsen in the 780s.)
- He carries a clan name, Ba or Wa, which indicates his identity and social class. The instructor asked the class and they answered: high class. The Ba clan was a clan that supplied queens and consorts to Tibetan rulers, so Ba Salnang was a statesman by birth.
- He was one of the ministers.
- He was among the first seven Tibetans ordained as monks at Samye, the event that made Samye an authentic monastery. The instructor stressed that these first seven monks all came from noble families.
- He served as one of the earliest and most important monastic officials of the Tibetan Empire, meaning he held a monastic identity and a political office at the same time.

The instructor's summary: his identity is complicated. He is a patron or promoter of Buddhism, close to the political center, later a monk, and still an imperial official. On this basis the text would deserve attention, because its author would be a witness and a participant in the events it describes, possibly even an eyewitness.

### What is actually in the Testament

The instructor summarized the contents for the class, since nobody reads classical Tibetan:

1. The spread of Buddhism in Tibet from the reign of Tri Detsuktsen (the father of Trisong Detsen) onward. This covers the early temples and the arrival of Chinese Buddhist materials via the Chinese princess. (Kapstein: the princess of Jincheng, married to the future Tri Detsuktsen in 710, arrived with an entire court including scholars; the *Testament of Ba* credits her with introducing the Buddhist funeral service of seven weeks, and translations of Chinese Buddhist scriptures and works on divination and medicine may date to this period.)
2. Trisong Detsen's reign in the eighth century: sending an emissary to China to bring Buddhist scriptures, inviting Indian masters, and founding Tibet's first complete monastery.
3. The conflict between Buddhism and Bön. The instructor made the point that the *Testament* is not only about the Samye debate.
4. The doctrinal debate itself, between the Indian Mahayana side and the Chinese side.
5. Supplementary material: a brief note on Tri Desongtsen's reign and his promotion of Buddhism in the early ninth century, and a note on the conflict between Buddhism and Bön under Mune Tsenpo's reign at the end of the eighth century.

Kapstein corroborates both supplements. On Tri Desongtsen (d. 815, r. 804 to 815), he quotes the Karchung temple inscription in which the tsenpo lists the temples built by his ancestors and himself. On Mune Tsenpo, Kapstein writes that "in the Testament of Wa, a work that may date to the eleventh century and that certainly incorporates earlier materials, we read an interesting account of a debate between Buddhist and Bönpo priests as to who among them should perform the last rites for Tri Songdetsen. It is Muné Tsenpo who presides over the dispute." The instructor also mentioned a treaty in this supplementary layer; the transcript renders it as "the Treaty of the Ten," which is most likely the treaty of 821/822 preserved in the bilingual uncle-nephew pillar inscription in Lhasa, but the audio is not clear enough to confirm.

### The anachronism: the author outlives himself

The instructor asked the class to find what is abnormal in that list of contents. One student pointed to the Moheyan connection. The instructor accepted that and pushed for more, then supplied the decisive problem himself.

Ba Salnang is a mid-eighth-century figure. Even granting a long life, he died at the end of the eighth century or the very start of the ninth. But the text's later sections describe events in the ninth century. A student answered the question correctly: someone else wrote the later layers. The instructor agreed. Ba Salnang may well be the original or first-hand author of the core text, but other people took part in the process and left their own material in it. So the attribution cannot be left standing as it is.

This is where he stated the general lesson. When you handle any material (a text, an inscription, a drama) you have to ask who the producer is, whether there is one author or several, and how the material matured into its present form. Only after evaluating the reliability and the history of the material can you decide how to deal with it. This maps almost exactly onto the UNC "Evaluating Print Sources" handout, which tells you to analyze a text according to the author, the publisher, and the date.

### Four recensions and why length varies

The instructor explained that the *Testament* was originally transmitted as a manuscript in multiple versions, that a collecting effort went into the regions to gather copies, and that the surviving material sorts into four published versions: an abridged or short version, a medium version, an extended version, and a supplemented version. (The reading list points to the modern edition behind this: van Schaik's notes cite Pasang and Diemberger 2000 for the *dBa' bzhed*.)

He then ran a classroom exercise on why length differs across versions under one title. A student guessed additions from Chinese. The instructor redirected to the students' own writing process: you have a draft and a polished version of your midterm paper. Which is longer? A student said the polished one. He pushed back: not necessarily; sometimes his own polished version is shorter than his draft. The differences he identified are:

- Language edited and revised.
- New information added, which lengthens the text.
- The same content elaborated with specific details and explanations, which expands the text.

All three can change the length. He applied this straight back to the *Testament*: we should expect new information to have entered the longer recensions.

On the supplemented version specifically, he said the supplement works like an epilogue attached to the main text, and that it can be dated confidently because its content never appears in versions before the fourteenth century. The transcript wobbles between thirteenth and fourteenth century here, so verify the date before using it in writing. His conclusion: that layer is a later composition by someone else and is irrelevant to the inherent value of the core text.

He noted that the proper job here would be to compare all four versions and track the shifts and changes, but that the class does not have time for it. So he worked with the shortest and earliest witness instead.

### The Dunhuang fragment

The earliest witness is a fragment from the Dunhuang cave, and the instructor credited van Schaik with the work on it. (The bibliography confirms: van Schaik and Kazushi Iwao, "Fragments of the Testament of Ba from Dunhuang," *Journal of the American Oriental Society* 128.3, 2008, 477 to 488. van Schaik's endnotes also say the Shantarakshita arrival story appears in the *Testament of Ba* with "an earlier version" in the Dunhuang fragments.)

The instructor walked through the physical reasoning on the fragment, and this part of the transcript is the roughest in the session. The reconstructable argument: the fragment is a partial page, and van Schaik determined its orientation by noting that the other side carries Chinese text written horizontally. That fixes which side is the front and therefore which corner is missing, and it shows the Tibetan was written on the back of a reused Chinese document.

The consequence matters more than the mechanics. Because the Tibetan is on the verso of scrap paper and in handwriting, it is not a formal or official copy. The instructor was explicit: we do not know in what context someone copied it, but it is not a state-sponsored copy.

He then listed the dating evidence:

- The orthography of the words is archaic, not fourteenth-century.
- The language in use is old. His analogy for non-linguists: medieval English differs from ours in ways you can feel even without training.
- He dated the fragment to around the eleventh century, which matches Kapstein's estimate that the *Testament of Wa* "may date to the eleventh century and that certainly incorporates earlier materials."

He also relayed, with evident amusement, that van Schaik was obsessed enough with the handwriting question to contact Scotland Yard and ask for a handwriting expert, then wrote an academic paper on the basis of that analysis. Treat this as a class anecdote; it is not in the assigned chapter.

### Cross-checking against inscriptions

The next move was corroboration. The instructor said inscriptions are the most reliable class of evidence, that the class discussed inscriptions in the previous session, and that it is a big topic. van Schaik compared the content of the fragment with inscriptional evidence and found that the two match.

The instructor spelled out the logic of why that matters. The inscription is independently dated. It does not copy the *Testament*, and the *Testament* does not claim to copy the inscription. They are two independent pieces of evidence saying the same thing, so what they agree on can be treated as historical fact. (The Samye pillar text quoted in van Schaik is a good example of the kind of inscription in play: Trisong Detsen's oath that support for the Three Jewels at Rasa, Dragmar and elsewhere "must never be abandoned or destroyed," witnessed by worldly and transworldly gods.)

Two results followed:

1. The existence of an original version of the *Testament of Ba* is confirmed, and Ba Salnang can plausibly be accepted as the first author of that original core.
2. The text was being copied and circulating at Dunhuang during the Tibetan imperial period. Since the Tibetans took Dunhuang, the text was already in circulation there and local people copied it. (The Dunhuang library cave article dates Tibetan rule of Dunhuang to 786 to 848.)

### The negative conclusion

Then the instructor closed the loop. Most surviving *Testament of Ba* manuscripts were rewritten in later centuries, after the twelfth. Those are not Ba Salnang's work: other hands edited, adapted, added content, and elaborated. And supplementation started early, as far back as the end of imperial Tibet, with the Mune Tsenpo and treaty material. His characterization: the *Testament of Ba* is not a closed, sealed text. It is dynamic, open, continually adapted and reproduced, with omissions and additions layered over an original framework.

The consequence is that it is nearly impossible to know what happened at Samye. We cannot establish whether the Indian and Chinese monks were really there, whether Moheyan and Kamalashila really debated, or what they argued about. His term for the situation: the evidence is contaminated. It contains original historical material, but that material is mixed with later accretion, and absent further evidence we cannot say to what extent any part is true. This is the problem of a single, isolated piece of evidence.

He added one more caution, carefully hedged. Ba Salnang was a disciple of Shantarakshita. The instructor explicitly refused to conclude from this that Ba Salnang distorted the history to favor his teacher or to present Shantarakshita's side as the winner. He said only that the possibility exists because of the discipleship. Note the distinction: he modeled flagging a motive without asserting it.

This lines up with van Schaik's own framing of the debate: "This, anyway, is the Tibetan version of the story. Some ancient manuscripts indicate that Moheyan had a more nuanced view of meditation, and a Chinese version of the debate concludes with the tsenpo giving his blessing to the Chinese teachers. Modern scholars have even questioned whether a debate ever really took place, or whether there was instead a series of discussions and literary exchanges." van Schaik's endnote identifies the Chinese document as Pelliot chinois 4646 from Dunhuang.

### The method, stated as method

Asked what the day's topic was, a student answered: the accuracy of historical sources. The instructor accepted that and asked what else he had taught. A student said: methodology. He agreed, conceded that the material is dry, and insisted it is the technique you need if you do academic work and have to evaluate materials. The steps he listed:

1. Collect the materials.
2. Examine them.
3. Compare the different versions against each other.
4. Check the handwriting.
5. Compare language styles and orthography.
6. Compare against other classes of historical material, especially inscriptions.

All of this, he said, belongs to the first of the three dimensions.

### What comes next

The instructor confirmed that dimension one is closed with a negative verdict: he cannot tell us about the actual debate because there is not sufficient historical material. Next comes dimension two, which asks how historical people of different identities and positions talked about the debate. He named three perspectives:

1. The traditional Tibetan perspective.
2. The modern Western scholarly perspective. He said he has already sent the class a paper by this scholar and told them to read it. The name in the transcript is garbled as "Ranks"; verify it on bCourses before citing.
3. The modern Chinese scholarly perspective, represented by a scholar trained in Germany as a philologist and working in the philological tradition. The name is not audible in the recording.

Dimension three is your own interpretation, which is the assignment.

### Student summary

The instructor had a student summarize the class in the last two minutes. The student's version, with the instructor's one correction, covered: the Samye debate and the split between scholastic and practice-oriented Buddhism; Padmasambhava's Indian background; Ba Salnang's mission to India to invite Shantarakshita (the instructor cut in to say no details, main argument only); Ba Salnang as the author of the *Testament of Ba* and the text's importance as the channel through which the information reached us; the accuracy problem, namely that the *Testament* mentions events past the ninth century while Ba Salnang lived only in the eighth; the accuracy of historical sources in general; and the Dunhuang fragment, which matters because it confirms the *Testament* existed and that copies of it circulated in the Tibetan empire.

## Connections to the readings

**van Schaik, chapter 2, "The Holy Buddhist Empire, 700 to 797."** This is the chapter the instructor kept telling students to go back to. The Ba Selnang conversion story (the half-red pearl placed in the dead children's mouths, the child born with a half-red pearl on his tooth) is where van Schaik introduces him. The "Great Debate" section gives Ba Selnang's China journey, Reverend Kim, Moheyan's arrival from Dunhuang, the seating arrangement with the tsenpo enthroned between Moheyan on his right and Kamalashila on his left, and the substance of the disagreement: both sides agreed on emptiness, and disagreed about how the insight is realized. Moheyan's image is that virtuous and sinful actions are like black and white clouds, both blocking out the sun. Kamalashila's answer is that abandoning analysis cuts off wisdom at the root, and that non-conceptualization without analysis produces a state like fainting, "a practice of stupidity." Victory went to the Indian side.

**van Schaik's endnotes to chapter 2.** This is where the instructor's whole source-critical argument is anchored, and it is worth reading in full. Note 22 says the Shantarakshita arrival story is in the *Testament of Ba (Dba' bzhed)* with an earlier version in the Dunhuang fragments, citing van Schaik and Iwao 2008. Note 23 says the Padmasambhava account is based on the earliest sources, the Dunhuang manuscripts and the *Testament of Ba*, and that his role becomes more prominent in later literature. Note 25 explains the Chan / Zen terminology from Sanskrit *dhyāna*, Tibetan *samten*. Note 26 says the dispute and debate are related in the *Testament of Ba* and in Pelliot chinois 4646. The bibliography entry for van Schaik and Iwao 2008 is the paper the instructor's Scotland Yard anecdote attaches to.

**van Schaik's acknowledgements.** Relevant to the methodological point: van Schaik names "anonymous works like the Old Tibetan Annals and the Testament of Ba" alongside "religious histories by scholars like Go Lotsawa Zhonupal and Pawo Tsuglag Trengwa." Note that van Schaik calls the *Testament of Ba* anonymous. That is a quieter version of exactly the conclusion the lecture reached by argument.

**Kapstein, *The Tibetans*, chapter 3.** Gives the princess of Jincheng material behind the *Testament*'s opening section, the dating statement that the *Testament of Wa* "may date to the eleventh century and that certainly incorporates earlier materials," the Mune Tsenpo last-rites dispute, Tri Desongtsen's reign and the Karchung inscription, and the warning that "no evidence of these tales is to be found in pre-twelfth century documents." Kapstein also supplies the Samye provisioning passage quoted from the *Testament of Ba* and the *mopa* diviner photograph. His glossary distinguishes *sba bzhed* from *dba' bzhed*, which is the variation the instructor mentioned.

**"The Nature of the Dunhuang Library Cave and the Reasons for Its Sealing."** Supplies the dates for Tibetan rule at Dunhuang, 786 to 848, which is the window in which the *Testament* fragment was copied. It also describes how incomplete monastery libraries at Dunhuang were from the Tibetan period onward, which is useful background for why a text would be copied onto the back of a reused Chinese document rather than in a formal state-sponsored copy.

**UNC Writing Center, "Evaluating Print Sources."** The closest handout match to the lecture's method. It tells you to evaluate a text on objectivity, authority, and applicability, and to analyze it according to three criteria: the author, the publisher, and the date. It also makes the point the instructor made about Ba Salnang's discipleship: finding bias "does not mean you should discard it. Every interpretation is influenced by the author's context. Find out where the author is coming from and use the evidence accordingly."

**UNC Writing Center, "Evidence."** Relevant to how you will have to handle the *Testament* in an essay, since you are using a source that your own lecture established is unreliable. Say what it can and cannot support.

## Writing and reading-skills guidance

**Interrogate your sources before you use them.** The instructor's core instruction: when you have a material, whether text, inscription, or drama, ask who produced it, who the author is, whether there is one author or several, and how the thing reached its present form. Only after evaluating reliability and transmission history can you decide how to use it.

**Draft versus polished is not about length.** The instructor used the class's own midterm process to make the point. A polished version is not necessarily longer than a draft. His own polished versions are sometimes shorter. Revision means edited and revised language, genuinely new information, and elaboration with specific details and explanations, in some combination. Length changes as a side effect, in either direction.

**A negative conclusion is a legitimate result.** The lecture's whole payoff was that we cannot know what happened at Samye. That is a finding, not a failure, and it was arrived at by argument rather than assumed. Model this in your writing: state what the evidence cannot bear.

**Name a possible bias without over-claiming it.** The instructor explicitly refused to conclude that Ba Salnang's discipleship under Shantarakshita distorted his account, and said only that the possibility exists. Copy that register.

**Endorsement is not verification.** The instructor used a sharp analogy. When people hear you read at Berkeley, they infer you are intelligent without knowing your major or your work; that is the university lending you an endorsement. Likewise, the fact that the *Blue Annals* and the *Feast of the Wise* cite the *Testament* shows that major Tibetan scholars trusted it. It does not independently establish that the *Testament* is true.

**Independent corroboration is what actually raises confidence.** The instructor was precise about why the inscription comparison works. The inscription is independently dated, the *Testament* does not copy it, and it does not copy the *Testament*. Two independent witnesses agreeing on the same content gives you a historical fact. One isolated source does not.

**Summarize at the level of argument, not detail.** When the student's recap started listing narrative details, the instructor cut in: no details, only the main argument. Apply this to essay introductions and to your final presentation.
