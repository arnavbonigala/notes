# Lecture 14: Reading for the Argument, Writing the Overview Essay

## Summary

A workshop session, almost entirely about method rather than new Tibetan history. Three parts. First, the instructor unpacked this week's reading response prompt on what strengthened Songtsen Gampo's rule, and argued it differs from the previous two prompts: instead of one thematic angle, it asks for the interconnections among the strategies. Second, he taught summary as the foundation of humanities writing and demonstrated it on a passage from a book about colonial Korea, chosen precisely because nobody in the room had background knowledge of it. Third, he read aloud and line-edited the opening paragraph of an anonymous student paper on the Songtsen Gampo and Wencheng marriage, using it to teach transitions, historical accuracy, register, and the danger of overstating early Tibet's power against Tang China.

## Lecture notes

### Reading the prompt before writing: agent, action, object

Do not jump to a conclusion or start drafting immediately. Give yourself time to reflect on what the prompt actually asks: what the key evidence is, what the purpose of the assignment is, what the instructor is looking for.

The key term in this week's prompt is "strengthen." Break any prompt into three elements:

- Agent: Songtsen Gampo, who in this case stands for the early Tibetan state. The instructor said we can treat the ruler and the early state as roughly equivalent here.
- Action: strengthen.
- Object: his rule.

Having those three elements is not yet an essay. You still have to choose which point you will emphasize and elaborate.

### How this prompt differs from the previous two

A student answered that the first two essays focused on one specific part of what helped, while this one asks about everything that strengthened his rule. The instructor accepted that framing: the earlier prompts were thematic, asking for one perspective. Another student objected that you cannot cover everything in 700 words. The instructor agreed, and confirmed the limit is 700 words at most.

So "cover all of them" does not mean a list, one strategy after another in parallel. And it does not mean picking military instead of marriage, because that would just be another thematic essay. One student proposed combining marriage and military and writing about how the two together strengthened his rule; the instructor called that very close.

The actual task: we have just finished the first chapter, so he wants an overview. Detect the connections among the thematic strands. Do they support each other, contradict each other, assist each other, or stay separate? Why did Songtsen Gampo choose these strategies, and how do the strategies relate to one another and to the strengthening of his rule and of early Tibet's influence?

Note: several students had already written about the marriage twice, so repeating it alone is not useful.

### Summary is the foundation of the paper

The prompt says the essay should present the basic features of early Tibetan society but should not be only a summary. It should also analyze, and the analysis must be based on the summary of the readings and the lectures.

The instructor's larger claim about academia, especially humanities at Berkeley: however elaborately a professor phrases a prompt, the first and primary requirement is summary of the readings, the book, the paper. On top of a good summary you build analysis (a reflection paper) or research. If you can summarize well, that is about 90 percent of a good paper. Do not be frightened or distracted by fancy prompt language.

What summarizing a reading actually means, assembled from student answers and his corrections:

1. The main argument. This is the most important element. An article, an academic book, a chapter, even a single paragraph as the basic unit, each has a main argument. Find it first.
2. The author's background: academic training, experience, what they work on, what they tend to publish. Important, but secondary to the argument.
3. The evidence, which you never simply list.
4. How the main argument is established. Not just what the materials are (statistical data, online archives, records, video) but how those materials are organized and mobilized to prove the argument.

On the problem of detail, which he said answers a question a student asked last time: there are too many details to remember. Do not be distracted by them. Find the author's main logic and note where you can find the detail again when you need it. Do not sign your name to the author's points, directly or indirectly; a summary represents the author, not you.

### Modeling the method on an unfamiliar book

He handed out a chapter from *Offspring of Empire*, published by the University of Washington Press, subtitled (as he read it) on the Koch'ang Kims and the colonial origins of Korean capitalism, 1876 to 1945. The author's name is garbled in the recording; the instructor said the author is a professor at Harvard and praised the book both for content on Korean history and as a model of academic composition. This book is not among the course readings in readings-text, so nothing here is confirmable against them.

He chose it deliberately because it is completely irrelevant to Tibet, so students have no background knowledge and must work from structure alone.

What the title alone gives you: a time period (1876 to 1945, roughly the early modern and colonial era), a place (Korea), and a topic (capitalism, and specifically its colonial origins).

Reading the passage, subtitled in his description as a barrier to Korean capitalism:

- Finding the main argument. Students first proposed that the point was that Korean industrialization proceeded at a slow pace. The instructor pushed back: the argument is that the widely held belief in the scope of Korean industrialization in this period is exaggerated. The author denies the received view. He also noted the general rule: the main argument usually sits at the end of the introduction or at the beginning of the body, and here it is the first sentence of the section, stated straightforwardly.
- Finding the structure. The author supports the argument from two administrative perspectives, one representing the Japanese side and one the Korean colonial government. First, what Japanese policy wanted from Korean industrialization; second, what the Korean side wanted to see.
- The Japanese section. Japan wanted to use Korea for its imperial ambitions and incorporated it into its imperialist economy, shifting away from the earlier agriculture-first policy. The empire was a hierarchy: Japan proper on top, Korea in second place, so industrialization was pushed but not on Japan's own terms. The section's conclusion, that the new policy was only a tentative first step toward an industrial economy, ties directly back to the main argument: a tentative first step means not well developed.
- The Korean section. Industrialization came late and secondary. The instructor's technique for unfamiliar names: if you do not know what a term means, label it A and B and work out the logic between them. Here the terms were Korea and Manchukuo, the Japanese-controlled north of China, which the Japanese prized and developed ahead of Korea. He also noted that the passage ties Korean industrialization strongly to the Sino-Japanese War of 1937, decades after the period the main argument concerns.
- The conclusion. The author closes by citing Bruce Cumings, with a footnote, rather than paraphrasing, because the quotation supports the point.

His verdict: nothing redundant, everything serves the argument. Main argument, evidence organized into two perspectives, proof, conclusion, citation. If you can write one paragraph like that, the rest of the essay is just expansion; the same skeleton scales to 20 pages by adding detail.

Reading works the same way in reverse. Do not sit too close to the words. Step back and ask what the author is trying to say, what the main argument is, what materials they use, and how they establish the argument.

Feedback on submissions this week: several students supplied a lot of detail from the readings. Good, because it shows engagement with class and readings. Not good when the details are not directly relevant to the argument or are merely listed. Details must be employed, not placed.

### Line-editing a student's opening paragraph

Formatting: US academic convention is no indent on the first paragraph, indent on subsequent paragraphs. The sample followed this correctly.

Title: he declined to judge it as good or bad, but said it runs long and should be condensed to carry the key points of the main argument. Putting your key point in the title is a good strategy.

Word choice and precision:

- "Emperor" for the Tibetan ruler invites confusion, because king and emperor carry different hierarchies in different political contexts. Use "ruler," which is neutral and general.
- Giving Songtsen Gampo's lifetime in brackets is good practice and many students omitted it. It shows training, and it tells the reader where the person sits in the historical framework. Same for the Tang princess Wencheng: every historical person should be identified the first time they appear.
- "Tibetan empire" at this stage is too strong. Better: the nascent Tibetan state.
- "Bodhisattva" can be made more specific if you know more, for instance naming Tara.
- When you write about the wife of Songtsen Gampo, say so, or readers will not know the relationship.

Transitions: the student clearly understood that transition words carry logic, but used "however," "however," "nevertheless" in three consecutive sentences. The second "however" makes the logical map unreadable: the reader cannot tell how the third sentence relates to the first. Every sentence should know where it is on the logical map. Distinguish the functions: "therefore" for consequence, contrastive markers for contradiction or denial, "additionally" for a further step, "specifically" for more detailed information belonging to the previous sentence.

The problem sentence, roughly "during the reign of Songtsen Gampo and his marriage to Princess Wencheng, most of his government were not Buddhist despite the ..." drew several corrections:

- "And" implies the reign and the marriage are parallel, but they are not. The marriage happened within the reign, so subordinate it: put the marriage first because it is the point of the sentence, and let the reign supply when and where.
- The missing information is that the marriage was deliberately, intentionally arranged. Say so.
- "His government" is an abstract entity. He means the ministers. Say ministers.
- Historical accuracy. There is no evidence that Wencheng wanted to convert Tibet into a Buddhist state. What we have is that she brought a statue and scriptures. You may speculate that she had such an intention, but you cannot state it as fact.
- The sentence gives a conclusion without saying where it comes from. Cite the specific material: according to van Schaik's *Tibet: A History*, or according to the Tang annals, or whatever the source is. That tells the reader you are not just asserting.
- In an introduction, you mention the evidence but do not develop it. Detailed treatment belongs in the body paragraphs.

His pattern for an opening paragraph: context and topic, then the key point of the topic, then your own position (support, agree, disagree) and what kind of materials you use.

The student's thesis, roughly that the marriage was not a success in spreading Buddhism into Tibet as a religious conversion, and that it mainly provided peaceful relations and cultural exchange between early Tibet and Tang China, he praised, because the writer highlighted the main argument as instructed on the last assignment. He said about 90 percent of the class remembered to highlight; 10 percent forgot.

Register and hedging:

- "So" is colloquial. In academic writing use therefore, thereby, thus, in brief, in short, consequently.
- "In my opinion" is usable but reads high school. Avoid opening with "I think." Yes, you may use "I think," but do not overuse it, because it flags the claim as merely subjective. Use it once at most; the reader can tell which claims are yours.
- "Not a success" carries a value judgment of good or bad. Prefer the neutral formulation: it did not largely spread Buddhism in Tibet. Before a very extreme claim, find the softer, more neutral phrasing. This is not withholding your position; it is humility plus an honest acknowledgment that you do not have enough knowledge to be 100 percent sure.
- "Religious conversion" is better phrased as converting Tibet into a Buddhist state, which probably overstates what happened.

### Historical accuracy: early Tibet was not Tang China's equal

Some students wrote as if the Tibetan empire could compete with Tang China. The instructor said that is not true at this stage. In the seventh century and up to the mid eighth century, before the An Lushan Rebellion, Tang China was probably the most influential and most powerful state in the world, with influence extending into Central Asia. Tibet at this point was small. It did not yet have a systematic, well established ideology or, in his phrase, the operating system. China already had more than a thousand years of history and development behind it.

From the Tibetan side, the honest claim is that they could organize and mobilize forces to harass China. They could not really attack or occupy China. And the reason Emperor Taizong wanted a peace agreement was that his focus was Korea: he wanted to move military forces northeast to conquer Korea, so he used minimum resources to keep the border with Tibet quiet.

His closing formula: two things in any paper. Structure and main argument for the form; accuracy and precision for the content.

## Key people, places and terms

- **Songtsen Gampo**: the Tibetan ruler at the center of the assignment. Kapstein gives c. 605 to 650, reigning from c. 617; van Schaik notes he died in 649, the same year as Taizong.
- **Princess Wencheng**: the Tang princess sent to Tibet. Kapstein notes she was in fact intended for the crown prince Gungsong Gungtsen, in whose favor Songtsen abdicated in 641; after Gungsong predeceased his father in 646 she became her father-in-law's queen, and Tibetan tradition remembers her primarily as the Chinese wife of Songtsen Gampo.
- **Taizong**: the Tang emperor, co-founder of the Tang on the ruins of the Sui, remembered in Chinese history as one of its greatest rulers.
- **Gar Tongtsen**: Songtsen's prime minister, sent to the Tang court to request the princess.
- **Tsenpo**: the Tibetan ruler's title, used by van Schaik.
- **An Lushan Rebellion**: the mid eighth century turning point the instructor used to date the height of Tang power.
- **Jokhang and Ramoche**: the two early Lhasa temples. Van Schaik says the statue Wencheng brought was placed in Ramoche and later moved to the Jokhang.
- **Bodhisattva, Tara, Avalokiteshvara**: Kapstein records the tradition that Wencheng and the Nepalese princess Tritsün were emanations of goddesses, and that the monkey and ogress of the origin myth were emanations of Avalokiteshvara and Tara.
- **Jowo**: Kapstein's name for the image said to have been brought by Wencheng, meaning "Lord," housed in the Jokhang.
- **Manchukuo**: the Japanese-controlled territory in the north of China, from the Korea example.
- **Bruce Cumings**: the historian quoted in the *Offspring of Empire* passage's concluding citation.

## Connections to the readings

- **Van Schaik on the marriage negotiations**: Songtsen sent an ambassador in 634 with little effect, then four years later sent Gar Tongtsen to ask for a princess. Tibet was still "an obscure little kingdom in the barbarian south" as far as the Chinese court was concerned; the princess was promised to the Azha instead and Gar was sent home empty-handed. Songtsen then moved his army to China's border and demanded the princess. This is direct support for the instructor's point about the power differential and about accuracy.
- **Van Schaik on what the marriage produced**: two decades of peace, cultural exchange, young Tibetan aristocrats travelling to Chang'an to study, paper and ink and technology such as silkworms and millstones going to Tibet. This is the evidentiary base for the student thesis he praised, that the marriage mainly produced peace and cultural exchange.
- **Van Schaik on the two historiographies**: Chinese historians say the princess set to work civilising the Tibetans, persuading the nobility into Chinese silk and away from painting their faces red; Tibetan historians say her greatest contribution was Buddhist, the first Buddha statue in Tibet. This split is exactly why the instructor insisted you cite which source a claim comes from.
- **Van Schaik on the Jokhang's origins**: built during Narendradeva of Nepal's exile in Lhasa in the 630s, on a Nepalese model with details carved by Nepalese craftsmen. Relevant because the student paper attributed Buddhism's arrival to Wencheng alone.
- **Van Schaik on the Zhangzhung marriage**: a royal princess married to the king of Zhangzhung to seal an alliance, who then worked as a spy for Songtsen until he ambushed and killed the king. This is the clearest reading-based route into the prompt's real task, connecting marriage strategy to military strategy rather than treating them separately.
- **Van Schaik on Taizong's character**: practical, humble for an emperor, willing to take advice, close affinity with the semi-nomadic peoples west of China through his mother's Turkish clan, but slipping into decadence by the 640s. Useful for the instructor's point that Taizong's Korea ambitions, not Tibetan strength, shaped the peace.
- **UNC "Summary: Using it Wisely"**: this is the handout behind the session's central tension. It argues the opposite emphasis from the instructor's, that when the assignment requires a thesis and evidence you should limit summary to a few sentences of background and spend most of the paper on argument, and it gives diagnostic questions (are you following the author's order, are you just describing what happens). Worth reading alongside his claim that a good summary is 90 percent of a good paper: he means summary as the necessary foundation, not as the body of the essay.
- **UNC "Paragraphs"**: covers the problem he diagnosed in the student paragraph, that transitions are needed within a paragraph and not just between them, and that you must explain the relationship of an example to the point rather than leaving it adjacent.

## Writing and reading-skills guidance

1. Break the prompt into agent, action, object before you draft, then choose what to emphasize.
2. For an overview prompt, write the connections among themes, not a list of themes.
3. Find the main argument first. Look at the end of the introduction or the start of the body.
4. Then reconstruct the structure: how many perspectives, in what order, with what materials.
5. Evidence is never listed. Every detail must be shown to serve the main argument.
6. Unknown terms: label them A and B and work out the logic between them rather than stalling.
7. Identify every historical person the first time they appear, with dates in brackets.
8. Use "ruler" rather than "emperor" or "king" when the political hierarchy is unclear. Use "nascent Tibetan state" rather than "Tibetan empire" for the seventh century.
9. Cite the material behind any conclusion: according to van Schaik, according to the Tang annals.
10. One transition per logical move, and check the logical map every sentence. Never three contrastive transitions in a row.
11. Avoid "so" in academic prose. Limit "I think" and "in my opinion" to once, or drop them.
12. Prefer neutral phrasing over value judgments like "success" or "failure."
13. Introductions state context, topic, key point, your position, and your materials. They do not develop the evidence.
14. Highlight your main argument in the submission, both for the instructor and as a check on yourself: every body paragraph should be tested against it.
15. Revise in two passes. First structure: does every paragraph and piece of evidence support the argument. Second, only if you have time, language, meaning precision and clarity rather than English correctness. He is not requiring the second pass.
16. Read the way you write: step back from the words to the argument, the materials, and the method.
