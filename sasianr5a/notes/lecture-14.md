# Lecture 14 Notes: Writing the Third Reading Response

SASIAN R5A, Imperial Tibet: The Birth of Tibet

This was almost entirely a writing-methods session. No new Tibetan history was introduced. The class covered (1) how to read the third reading-response prompt, (2) what "summary" means in a humanities paper, and (3) a live exercise on a book from a completely unrelated field.

## 1. Unpacking the prompt: agent, action, object

The prompt asks what strengthened Songtsen Gampo's rule. The instructor's method for any prompt you do not know how to start:

- **Agent:** Songtsen Gampo, who can be treated as standing in for the early Tibetan state. The instructor said the two are roughly equal for this assignment's purposes.
- **Action:** "strengthen." This is the key term of the prompt.
- **Object:** his rule.

Find these three first. Then, before drafting, choose which of them you will actually emphasize. Having the elements is not yet having a paper.

Repeated instruction: do not jump to a conclusion on first reading. Read the prompt, sit with it, then ask what the evidence is and what the purpose of the assignment is.

## 2. How this prompt differs from the first two

Student answer accepted in class: the first two prompts each asked about one specific angle. This one asks about everything that strengthened his rule.

The instructor's correction on what "everything" means:

- It is **not** a list, one factor after another in parallel.
- A second student pointed out you cannot cover everything in 700 words. The instructor agreed, and used that as the pivot: the word limit is a signal that listing is the wrong shape.
- Picking only one factor (for example, military expansion alone) would just make this into another narrow prompt, which is what the first two already were.
- Do not write about the marriage alliances again on their own. Several people have already written on marriage twice.

**What he actually wants:** find the **connections between** the factors. The guiding questions he gave:

- Do these strategies support each other, undercut each other, assist each other, or run separately?
- Why did Songtsen Gampo choose this combination of strategies?
- Does each strategy actually strengthen his rule and the influence of early Tibet, or only appear to?

A student proposed combining marriage and military and was told this is very close, provided "combine" means tracing the interconnection rather than covering both in sequence.

This is achievable because the class has now finished the first chapter of van Schaik, so you have the overview needed to see across the strategies.

### Where the connections actually are in van Schaik, chapter 1 ("Tibet Appears, 600-700")

The reading gives several places where one strategy directly feeds another, which is the kind of link the prompt is after:

- **Marriage feeding military conquest.** A Tibetan princess was married to the king of Zhangzhung to seal an alliance, apparently continuing his father's policy. A few years later she was sending Songtsen reports on the king's troop movements; the army ambushed the king while he was away from his castle, and Zhangzhung, all of what became Western Tibet, was absorbed (pp. 5-6). Van Schaik raises the possibility that the marriage was designed from the start to bring the kingdom down from within. Marriage here is an instrument of conquest, not an alternative to it.
- **Hospitality feeding vassalage.** The exiled Nepalese king Narendradeva sheltered in Lhasa for most of the 630s. He returned to Nepal at the head of a Tibetan army in the early 640s and regained his throne essentially as a vassal (p. 6). The same stay brought Nepalese architectural knowledge: the Jokhang, Tibet's oldest Buddhist temple, was built on a Nepalese model by Nepalese craftsmen.
- **Conquest feeding diplomacy.** Gar Tongtsen was sent to the Tang court in 638 to ask for a princess and was sent home empty-handed because Tibet was still seen as an obscure kingdom in the barbarian south; the princess went to the Azha instead. Songtsen then sent his army, now reinforced with Zhangzhung troops, against the Azha, absorbed the northeast (today's Amdo), and with troops on China's border stopped requesting and started demanding (p. 6). Defeat of the Tang punitive army forced the emperor to revise his view. The marriage he eventually obtained was a product of military leverage.
- **Marriage feeding technology and writing.** The Chinese marriage brought two decades of peace, Tibetan aristocrats studying at Chang'an, and Chinese craftsmen skilled in paper and ink sent to Tibet along with silkworms and millstones (p. 10). That connects directly to the Tonmi Sambhota story and the creation of the Tibetan alphabet (pp. 11-12), and then to administration: the alphabet was immediately put to work governing the empire, which was divided into five *ru* or "horns," each with ten thousand-districts of a thousand households, used to extract taxes and conscripts (p. 13).
- **Administration against the clans.** Van Schaik notes that if the new districts split the clans across administrative units, "so much the better," because clan power still threatened Songtsen's lineage (p. 13). The ranked ministerial hierarchy with insignia of turquoise, gold, white gold, silver, brass and copper served the same function. Administrative reform is therefore also an internal-consolidation strategy, not a neutral one.
- **A strategy that did not strengthen the succession.** Songtsen stepped down when his son turned thirteen, the son died, and he resumed rule; he died in 649 leaving only an infant grandson as crown prince, and Prime Minister Gar stepped into the vacuum (pp. 14-15). Van Schaik's line is that Songtsen achieved everything the founder of an empire could wish for except a viable heir. If you want an example of strategies that do not reinforce each other, this is it.

One caution from the reading itself for anyone writing about Buddhism as a source of strength: van Schaik says the image of Songtsen as lawgiver reconciling government and religion is what later Tibetans made of him, that it is unlikely he actually created a Buddhist legal system, and that at the time Buddhism was only one of several cultural imports. His heir came from one of his Tibetan wives, not from the Chinese or (perhaps legendary) Nepalese princess (pp. 10, 14).

## 3. The requirement: summary, then analysis

The stated requirement is that the response should cover the basic features of early Tibetan society but should not be **only** a summary. The analysis is built on the summary, of both the readings and the lectures.

The instructor's broader claim: in humanities at Berkeley and in academia generally, however elaborately a professor words a prompt, the primary demand is summary. Summary is the foundation. His figure was that if you can summarize well, roughly 90 percent of the work of a good paper is done. Do not be frightened or distracted by fancy prompt language.

**What summarizing a text actually requires**, from the class discussion:

1. **The main argument.** This is the most important element. Every article, every academic book, every chapter, and even a single paragraph as the smallest unit has a main argument. Find it first.
2. **The author's background:** their academic training, experience, what they work on, where they publish. Useful but secondary to the argument.
3. **The evidence,** which you do not simply list.
4. **How the argument is built.** Not just which materials were used but how they are organized and mobilized to prove the argument. Ask what kind of material it is (statistical data, archives, records, and so on) and then ask how it is arranged.

On the problem of too many details, raised by a student in an earlier session: you cannot remember all of them, and you are not supposed to. Do not be distracted by details. Find the author's main logic and know where to go back and find the detail when you need it.

Practical placement tip: the main argument is usually at the end of the introduction or at the beginning of the body.

There is a live tension here worth managing. The UNC handout on summary warns against summary-heavy papers and says that when you do summarize, keep it brief and tied to your thesis. The syllabus says the midterm and final essays must not be summaries of lecture content or class discussion. The instructor's point is about summary as the **foundation** of your own analysis, meaning accurate command of the author's argument and its architecture, not as the content of your paper. For a 700-word reading response, summary should be compressed and in service of the connections you are arguing for.

## 4. In-class exercise: reading a book cold

The text used was *Offspring of Empire*, on the colonial origins of Korean capitalism, 1876 to 1945, published by the University of Washington Press, by an author the instructor identified as a Harvard professor. The transcript garbles the author's name and the full subtitle, and this book is not in the course readings folder, so verify the citation yourself before using it. The instructor chose it precisely because it is irrelevant to Tibet: you have no background knowledge, so it tests the method rather than your memory.

**Step one, mine the title page.** From the title and subtitle alone you can extract the period (late nineteenth century into the mid twentieth), the place (colonial Korea), and the topic (capitalism, and its origins under colonial rule). That already tells you the book asks what happened to capitalism in Korea under Japanese colonial rule.

He also flagged the book as a model of academic writing and composition, not only as content.

**Step two, find the main argument of the assigned passage** (subtitled, in the transcript, something like "a barrier to Korean capitalism"). Several student guesses were rejected as either too broad or as restating a fact rather than the argument:

- "industrialization developed over a long period"
- "they overtook the agricultural side"
- "the scope of Korean industrialization was not that fast"

The instructor's answer: the argument is in the **first sentence** of the passage, and it is a refusal. The received belief is that Korean industrialization in this period was already well developed. The author denies this and calls that view **exaggerated**. The argument is stated as a rejection of an existing understanding, and it is stated straightforwardly, up front, telling the reader what the author is about to do.

**Step three, ask how the argument is supported.** "Historical facts" and "a historical perspective" were rejected as too vague. Push to specificity: which perspectives, and how many. The passage works through **two administrations**, one representing the Japanese side and one the Korean side. The transcript is unintelligible at the point where the instructor names these two bodies, so the exact terms need to be recovered from the reading. The structure is: first what Japan wanted from Korean industrialization, then what the Korean side wanted.

**Step four, follow one branch.** On the Japanese side: Japan wanted to use Korea for its imperial ambitions and incorporated it into an imperialist economy, shifting away from the earlier agriculture-first policy for the colony. The relationship is hierarchical, with Japan proper on top and Korea in second place, and Korea is nonetheless required to keep pace with Japanese economic policy. So Japan did push industrialization in Korea, but from second position.

**Step five, check the conclusion against the main argument.** The passage closes by calling the new policy a **tentative first step** toward an industrial economy. Because it is tentative and early-stage, it is by definition not well developed, which is exactly the claim the opening argument made. The conclusion loops back to the thesis. That closure is what the instructor wanted the class to notice: main argument, then two organized perspectives, then an outcome that returns to the argument.

The recording ends mid-sentence as he restates that structure.

## 5. Action items

- Read the prompt for the third response and label agent, action, object before drafting.
- Do not organize the response as a list of factors, and do not make marriage alliances the sole subject.
- Choose two or three strategies from chapter 1 and argue how they connect: which enabled which, which pulled against which.
- Compress the summary. Use it as grounding, then spend the words on the connections.
- State your own main argument early and in one sentence, at the end of the introduction, the way the exercise passage did.
- Make the last sentence return to that argument.
- Format per syllabus: Word .doc or .docx on bCourses, 12-point, 1.5 spacing, standard margins. Late submissions receive zero credit.
