# Lecture 26: Active Reading, Note-Taking, and Setting Up the Diagnostic Essay Peer Review

## Summary

This session had two halves. The first was a practical talk on how to read scholarly books so that you still remember them a week later: read for the main argument, the sub-arguments, and the structure, take marginal notes as you go, and start with the preface and introduction rather than page one of chapter one. The instructor used van Schaik's preface as the worked example, showing that *Tibet: A History* is organized as ten historical periods with an argument in each chapter rather than one thesis stated up front, and that the book's governing concern is the contested, layered identity of Tibet.

The second half set up the Diagnostic Essay. Students paired off for peer review, and the instructor laid out what a peer reviewer is and is not (not an examiner, not a passive reader, not a journal editor), the difference between marginal and end comments, what "critique" means in an academic register, and the grading criteria: quality of feedback plus quantity of substantive comments, with no fixed number. He also announced that feedback on the midterm draft will be withheld until next week.

A long digression on Buddhist ethics (karma as cause and condition, benefiting others, the "poison medicine" story, the middle way) was used to frame peer review as mutual aid rather than self-sacrifice or attack.

## Lecture notes

### Why reading and comprehension come apart

The instructor opened with his own experience: he can read a 200 to 300 page book and feel confident about it, then find a week or two later that he has forgotten the main argument and the structure. He asked whether students have the same problem. The gap is between reading and comprehension or retention, and there is no single fix. Each student has to develop their own strategy. What follows is his strategy, offered as one model.

### His method: digital books and marginal notes

He says he rarely reads physical books unless he has no choice, and prefers digital for three reasons:

1. It is easy to make notes directly in the file.
2. You can carry the book and all your notes everywhere.
3. It is easy to search the PDF and to copy a passage or paragraph into your own writing.

He showed a book he is currently reading on screen with his own marginal comments beside the text. Next time he opens the book, he reads his notes instead of rereading the book.

Make notes in whichever language you prefer. He uses Chinese, not out of native-language loyalty but because it is compact and does not take up as much margin space as English. The language does not matter.

### What to make notes on

Not everything. The targets are:

- The main argument
- The sub-arguments
- The structure
- A small number of specific details

A book or article rarely has only one argument. There is usually a big argument plus several more specific ones. His own example of a specific main argument concerned Mindroling, the Nyingma monastery: that it functioned as a center for educating laypeople as well as an institution for monastic establishment. (The audio is rough here; the shape of the claim is clear but the exact wording is not.) The point is that a specific argument of that kind is legible, whereas in a broad history book you may read the argument and still not know what the book is "about."

### Authors who signal and authors who do not

Some scholars mark their claims explicitly: "I contend," "I argue," "I think," "I believe." Others dislike that register and are not overtly argumentative, so nothing on the page announces itself as the thesis. With those authors you have to summarize the argument yourself, in your own words, from the whole text. Train yourself to be alert to the question "what is this author's argument?" at all times while reading.

### Why details still matter

Details are not random. In academic writing, specific details exist to support a main argument or a sub-argument. He framed this through graduate study: a qualifying exam or reading list means working through many books, and you cannot retain everything. What you retain is the argument, the structure, the methodology, the primary sources, and a few specific details. That combination is what convinces an examiner that you actually understood the book.

If you have that much in your notes, he said, you already have a reflection paper in your head.

### Start with the preface and introduction

He asked the class where the argument of van Schaik's history is: chapter one? chapters two and three? The answer is that there is no single stated main argument in the body, and that there is something to read before chapter one. A student correctly identified the preface.

The preface and introduction are where the author tells you the main argument, the structure, and often the theoretical frame. Later in an introduction the author usually explains how the work is organized. If a book has no introduction, the instructor's fallback order is: introduction first, then contents to get the structure, then chapter one, then chapter two, then the conclusion. He strongly recommends reading everything, especially early in your academic career, but this is the triage order when time is short.

He also noted that history books are organized differently from thesis-driven monographs. Chapters are not always ranked by importance; often each chapter owns a period, so you have to sort out the chronology to navigate the book.

### Van Schaik's preface as the worked example

He walked through what question van Schaik raises. Van Schaik does not open with Buddhism or with the Dalai Lama. He opens with the bookshop problem: booksellers do not know which shelf books on Tibet belong on, so some go under "China," some under "Asia, General," and some under "Buddhism," which lets Tibet float free of politics and place entirely. (Van Schaik also describes a university library in London where the shelf sign is repeatedly scratched out and relabeled, "China" to "Tibet" and back again.) The instructor read this as a tension between different people's understandings of Tibet, and specifically between Tibetan and Chinese framings.

His reading of the book's project: it is not only about Tibet's history and not only about China. Van Schaik uses historical material, including sources like the Old Tibetan Annals, to open up the question of Tibetan identity, and the answer is that there is no single coherent identity. There are different layers of identity in different historical periods. That is why the book is ten chapters covering ten periods. If you read only chapter one you will not find a main argument; what you will find is the argument of each chapter.

### Writing a reflection paper from your notes

The checklist he gave for a reflection paper:

- Who wrote it, and when it was published. The publication date tells you which historical moment of scholarship the work belongs to. Academic fields have trends too, and historiography tracks how those trends shift.
- What the author's argument is.
- What materials and methods the author uses.
- What intervention the author is making, and what question or concern they are responding to. Do they agree with prior scholarship, oppose it, or add something new? Note the scholars the author names.
- A summary of the main argument of each chapter you read.
- Your own critique, which can also address the author's handling of primary sources.

A reflection paper is not a mere response to the author's content. It evaluates and critiques how the author organized and used their materials.

### Aside: disciplinary conventions differ

He recounted being trained in Buddhist studies, which he characterized as focused on chronology and on logic, where everything should have cause and effect, and then being surprised by how literature scholars read: they read everything, and they read subjectively. Different fields and disciplines have different conventions and criteria. Try to keep a balance. (Some names and departmental details in this passage are unintelligible on the recording.)

The closing frame on this half: notes are the tool that turns passive reading into active collection, and they are the bridge from reading to writing.

### Pairing up for the Diagnostic Essay

With 17 students in the class, pairing left one group of three. The instructor organized the exchange so that comments circulate in a loop rather than only in pairs, and told students to exchange email contacts.

### What a peer reviewer is not

He worked through this by elimination, with student input:

- Not an examiner. You are not grading or judging, and you are not in a position of authority over the writer. What you produce is not a verdict. You raise questions and concerns.
- Not an ordinary reader. When you read van Schaik you are not reviewing him; you largely accept what he says. Peer review requires the opposite posture.
- Not a press editor. A publisher's editor works for the press and handles everything: structure, arguments, ethics, primary sources, the whole apparatus. (He added that receiving a full editorial markup on your own paper is unpleasant enough that you do not want to reread your own work afterward.) Your peer is not trying to publish the paper.

### Peer review framed through Buddhist ethics

He used this to brainwash the class a little, in his words. The argument:

- Karma means cause and condition. Different causes produce different outcomes, and motivation is the root, like the root of a tree or plant.
- Calling Buddhism an altruistic tradition means acting so that others benefit.
- "Benefiting others" means giving people what they actually need, not what you happen to have. He told the classical poison-medicine story: for some people you should offer delicious food, because that is what they need; for someone who is ill, even a poison can be the right medicine. So first understand what the person needs, then give accordingly.
- It does not mean extreme self-sacrifice. Buddhism does not propose that. Peer review is mutual aid: when you help someone else you also learn something and gain something yourself.

He also raised, as a caution, the way asymmetry of knowledge and authority in academia can be abused, and told an anecdote about a scholar whose advisor or committee member used a veto to fail a PhD student after many years of work, so the student could not obtain the degree. He deliberately did not name the people involved. Parts of this passage are garbled, including the country and institution. His takeaway: whether you are a student now or a committee member or director later, remember what that power does.

### What you actually gain from reviewing a peer

Student answers, which he endorsed and extended:

- Inspiration and strategies you can apply in your own writing.
- You can learn from people more advanced than you and from people with more experience.
- You can learn from people at the same level, because they approach the same problem from different angles.
- You can even learn from people less far along, because answering their questions forces you to explain.
- You recognize common problems and your own blind spots, and the mechanics of writing become visible.
- You learn from both strengths and weaknesses.

He also described the carryover to self-editing: when you draft, you are the author; on the second pass you come back as a reader and editor of your own essay. Treat it as if it were someone else's paper. That is how you learn to edit.

### Format: marginal comments and end comments

The exchange happens on Google Docs. Two places to comment:

**Marginal comments** are specific and local. The unit to think with is the sentence. Questions to raise:
- What does this sentence mean? Does it make sense on its own?
- Is the meaning precisely delivered, or is it vague?
- Are there terms used vaguely? He singled out words like "interesting" and "significance" as the kind of vague term he does not want to see.
- Then read sentence to sentence: are these two or three sentences logically and coherently connected, or are they just A and B and C in sequence?
- Then paragraph to paragraph: are the passages well organized, is there a logic to the order, temporal or otherwise?

**End comments** are the overall view: the main argument, the structure, the big problems. This is where you summarize your marginal comments and name the central issue.

### What "critique" means

He asked the class directly: does critique mean to criticize? No. It is not negation, and it is not just agreement or praise. A professional critique serves improvement. Everything you write in a comment should serve the improvement of the writing.

So point out what does not contribute to the main argument, and also point out what does defend the main argument well. He reminded the class of the Friday sessions where he showed both bad examples and good examples, including a well-developed introduction paragraph. Flagging a strong move tells the writer "this is a strategy I can reuse."

Encouragement is part of the job and is a matter of diplomacy. He then distanced himself from one failure mode: insular, conservative scholarly circles whose members open with "maybe I'm wrong, but" immediately before attacking someone. He said that when he hears that formula he knows an attack is coming. You can play the politeness games of US academic settings if you want, but the thing that actually matters is sincerity and real respect for people.

The flip side: when you receive a comment that stings, remember that written language cannot control how it is read, and that how a comment lands depends on conditions. Do not take it personally. This is practice and exercise, and he asked students not to come to him with complaints about a peer's comments.

### How many comments

He pressed the class on this, since nobody asked. There is no standard number. It depends on:

- The paper itself. A well-developed paper leaves less to comment on; a less developed one leaves more.
- Your own level of reading and writing, and your own available time.

The stated criteria he read out:

- **Quality of feedback:** is it sincere, reasonable, and constructive?
- **Quantity of substantive comments:** no fixed minimum or maximum. Too few comments may affect your grade. Too many may overwhelm your peer and make revision harder. Aim for a balanced number, and prioritize quality over quantity while maintaining sufficient engagement.

How he grades the assignment: first, your attitude, meaning whether you genuinely engaged with your peer's paper in order to help them improve, and whether you learned from it; second, the quality of your comments. He called out two failure modes: comments that are effectively the same thing copied in different words, and comments so meticulous about grammar that they miss the point. Fix grammar only where it makes a sentence unreadable. The most important targets are always the main argument, the structure, and the logic. Keep to a middle way.

(One stretch about a number of points per paragraph, something like six or seven, is too garbled to reconstruct. Treat it as unverified.)

If you are unsure whether your comments are enough, bring them to him before you submit and he will look at whether you need to add or cut.

He closed by stressing that this assignment is not only on-paper work. Talk to your partner offline, communicate, and check what they actually think about the feedback.

## Key people, places and terms

- **Sam van Schaik**, *Tibet: A History* (Yale University Press, 2011). The course's primary text. Ten chapters, each covering a period, from "Tibet Appears, 600 to 700" to "Two Tibets, 1959 to the Present."
- **Preface (pp. xv to xviii)**. The bookshop problem, the London library shelf sign, political versus ethnographic Tibet, the clash of ideologies, and van Schaik's statement of method.
- **Political Tibet / ethnographic Tibet**. Van Schaik's distinction: the Tibet Autonomous Region versus the wider territory sharing Tibetan language and culture, overlapping Qinghai, Sichuan, Gansu and Yunnan and the Himalayan kingdoms. The Tibetan version of the distinction is **Bod** (pronounced bö), central Tibet, versus **Bod chen po**, Greater Tibet.
- **Old Tibetan Annals**. Anonymous early Tibetan historical source, named in van Schaik's acknowledgements alongside the *Testament of Ba*, and surviving in Dunhuang manuscripts including Pelliot tibétain 1288 and IOL Tib J 750. Kapstein also refers to it. The transcript's garbled "annals" reference is to this.
- **Mindroling**. Nyingma monastery in Central Tibet, founded by Terdag Lingpa and associated with the Fifth Dalai Lama; Lochen Dharmashri was one of its abbots. Appears in van Schaik at pp. 128, 139, 140, 165. The instructor used it as an example from his own research, not from van Schaik.
- **Tendrel**. Buddhist term van Schaik glosses as "interdependent origination": every event is suspended in a fragile network of causes and conditions. He calls it an apt lesson for anyone writing history.
- **Karma**, in the instructor's gloss: cause and condition, with motivation as the root.
- **Marginal comment / end comment**. The two comment types for the Diagnostic Essay.
- **Historiography**. The study of how scholarly trends shift over time, which is why a work's publication date matters.

## Connections to the readings

- **Van Schaik, preface.** Everything the instructor said about the preface is in the text. The bookshop example, the London shelf sign, the "war of wildly differing visions," and the observation that even talking about "Tibet" is to simplify and distort, since Khampas and Amdowas have long identified themselves regionally rather than as Tibetans. The instructor's claim that Tibetan identity is layered rather than coherent tracks van Schaik's own line about "the stories that, layer upon layer, have contributed to the cultural identities of Tibetans today."
- **Van Schaik on his own method.** The preface states explicitly that the book is a narrative, "necessarily partial and incomplete," following the individuals most influential in the making of Tibet, beginning in the seventh century and ending with what it means to be Tibetan in the twenty-first. This is the passage to cite if you need evidence for the instructor's point that the author tells you the structure in the introduction.
- **Van Schaik, table of contents (p. vii).** Confirms ten chronologically bounded chapters, which is the structural fact behind "ten different historical periods, one argument per chapter."
- **UNC, "Reading to Write."** Directly parallel to the first half of class: "Read (or at least skim) all parts of the reading. Sometimes the cover, title, preface, introduction, illustrations, appendices, epilogue, footnotes and 'about the author' sections can provide you with valuable information." It also tells you to consider the author, the genre, the intended audience, and the sources and methods used, which is the instructor's reflection-paper checklist in other words. It adds two things he did not mention: think about what is missing from the reading, and read with the assignment question in mind.
- **UNC, "Getting Feedback."** Covers the half of peer review the instructor touched on only in passing: how to ask for feedback rather than how to give it. Useful point for your own essay: readers unfamiliar with your topic are good at spotting unclear argument and organization, so ask them to read your paper and then summarize its main points back to you. Also: if you do not understand a comment you received, ask the person who made it.
- **UNC, "Revising Drafts."** Reinforces the instructor's priority ordering and sharpens it. Proofreading is not revision; it is "putting a band-aid on a bullet wound" if the thesis is weak and the organization is a mess. Editing is a final polishing step. This is the handout to cite for why you should comment on argument and structure before grammar.
- **UNC, "Evaluating Print Sources."** Has a section on date of publication, which supports the instructor's point that publication date situates a work within a moment in the field.
- **Dunhuang library cave article.** Relevant background for the manuscript sources van Schaik relies on. The article is largely about the competing accounts of how and why the cave was sealed, including Pelliot's conclusion that it was sealed in 1035 as a storage room. The instructor mentioned source material only in passing in this session.

## Writing and reading-skills guidance

**Reading**
1. Read the preface and introduction first. If time is short: introduction, then contents for the structure, then chapter one, then chapter two, then the conclusion.
2. Look in the later part of the introduction for the author's statement of how the work is organized.
3. In a history book, expect period-based chapters rather than ranked ones. Sort out the chronology first.
4. Hunt the argument actively, especially with authors who never write "I argue."
5. Annotate as you go. Use a format that lets you annotate, which is also a syllabus requirement.
6. Note the main argument, the sub-arguments, the structure, and a few supporting details.

**Reflection papers**
Author and publication date, argument, materials and methods, intervention and the conversation being joined, per-chapter arguments, then your own critique including the handling of primary sources.

**Giving peer feedback**
- Marginal comments for sentence-level meaning, vague terms, and the logic between sentences and between paragraphs.
- End comments for the overall argument and structure.
- Everything serves improvement. Name what works, not just what does not.
- Avoid vague words in your own writing, especially "interesting" and "significance."
- Do not pile on grammar notes unless a sentence is unreadable.
- Be sincere rather than diplomatically formulaic.

**Self-editing**
Draft as the author, then come back on a second pass as a reader and editor of your own work, as if it were a stranger's paper.
