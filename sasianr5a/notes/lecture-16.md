# Lecture 16: The Buddha's Life Story, Collective Memory, and the Craft of Summary

## Summary

A review and discussion session built around one question: how do we talk and write about a figure whose life reaches us only through narrative? The first half was a vocabulary and etymology lesson on the words we use for the Buddha, plus the historical-evidence problem behind them. The instructor argued that the near-total absence of archaeological evidence for Siddhartha Gautama means the life story is a product of collective memory, and he drew an explicit parallel to how Tibetan tradition piles achievements onto Songtsen Gampo that were actually the work of others, such as his minister Gar Tongtsen.

The second half was a writing exercise disguised as a presentation exercise. The class split into four groups, each took one episode of the Buddha's life, and each had a few minutes to build and deliver a summary. The point was not the content. It was the skill of finding anchors in a mass of narrative detail and organizing a summary around them rather than reciting everything you remember.

## Lecture notes

### "Attributed to": diction as an argument

The class opened on a single word. Saying the Buddha is "attributed as" the founder of Buddhism is a deliberately careful construction. It reports what a tradition claims without the writer personally vouching for it. The instructor's point for essay writing: the verb you pick carries a claim about how much you are committing to. Choose it on purpose.

### Buddha: etymology and capitalization

"Buddha" comes from the Sanskrit root meaning to wake. The form is a past participle, so the word means "one who has awakened." The instructor pushed the class on the obvious follow-up question: awakened from what? Not from sleep. From samsara, and metaphorically from confusion or delusion.

He then made a distinction students will meet constantly in the readings:

- Capital B, singular, with "the": **the Buddha** means Siddhartha Gautama, Shakyamuni, the historical Buddha.
- Capital B, plural, no article: **Buddhas** signals a Mahayana or tantric frame, where there are many buddhas, such as the Medicine Buddha and Amitabha.
- Lowercase b is the generalized sense, a buddha as a type rather than a specific person.

The contrast he drew was between traditions that recognize only one Buddha for our era, Shakyamuni, and Mahayana and tantric traditions that recognize many. The name he gave for the single-Buddha tradition is not intelligible on the recording.

### Shakyamuni

*Muni* means sage. *Shakya* is the name of his father's clan or people. So Shakyamuni is "sage of the Shakya clan." The instructor treated this as an epithet rather than a name, something conferred from outside rather than given at birth.

### Siddhartha: the most interesting name

He spent the longest here, and this was the richest part of the session.

*Siddha* comes from the root meaning to accomplish. *Artha* is the key term. It does not mean "heart" in the physical sense. It means essence, meaning, purpose, and crucially **benefit, interest, worldly gain**. So Siddhartha is "one who has accomplished his aims," specifically worldly aims.

He noted that *artha* has established equivalents in the Buddhist translation languages: a standard Tibetan term and a standard Chinese term, both chosen to carry this range of meaning. The specific syllables he gave are garbled on the recording.

He then connected *artha* to the **Arthashastra**, the classical Indian treatise on statecraft and political economy, composed in the Mauryan period and written as a minister's instruction to a king, comparable to a mirror-for-princes text. The minister's name he gave is not audible. The takeaway: *artha* in that tradition means worldly gain and political advantage, which is exactly what a king wants and exactly what a prince named Siddhartha was supposed to achieve. It is, as he put it, a very good name for a royal son, and its irony is the whole story.

He added that we do not actually know which of the three names was his. If forced to choose, he would pick Siddhartha, because Gautama and especially Shakyamuni look like honorifics assigned later, whereas Siddhartha sounds like something a parent would name a child. A student offered a supporting reading: the father named him Siddhartha in defiance of the prophecy about what he was destined to become.

### Is the Buddha a historical person?

A student asked whether there is archaeological evidence, and compared the situation to Jesus: evidence of a figure doing such things, but not proof that all the stories attach to one person. The instructor agreed and gave what he considers the only historical evidence: the pillar erected by **King Ashoka** during his tour of his empire, installed at the Buddha's reported birthplace near the modern Nepal-India border, inscribed to say that this is where the Buddha Shakyamuni was born. (The transcript does not name the site; the pillar is conventionally identified with Lumbini. Ashoka appears in Kapstein only as the third-century BCE monarch whose edicts were inscribed in early Brahmi, the script family behind Tibetan writing.)

Everything before Ashoka's inscription, he said, is silence.

### If not evidence, then what? Buddhist narrative

So where do the stories come from? Buddhist scriptures. And the scriptures were not written down during the Buddha's lifetime. The first recension came after his death, the second some decades later, and the corpus kept growing, changing, and absorbing new material for centuries. Episodes were elaborated, designed, and in his word fabricated. We cannot separate historical kernel from later accretion.

Scholarly dating puts the Buddha, if he existed, around the fifth century BCE. The instructor said not to memorize the competing chronologies.

He then summarized a recent exchange between two scholars, neither named in class:

- One argues the historical Buddha must have existed, because a religion needs a founding person as its foundation.
- The other argues a religion rests on its system and teaching, not on a person, and that reading across the Pali canon suggests the "Buddha" may be multiple persons collapsed into one.

### The Songtsen Gampo parallel

This is where the session connected to the course. Tibetan tradition credits Songtsen Gampo with a set of great achievements from his reign, including the marriage to the Chinese princess. But the mission was carried out by his minister, **Gar Tongtsen**. Van Schaik tells this in detail: Songtsen sent an ambassador in 634 with little effect, then in 638 sent Gar Tongtsen, his prime minister and right-hand man, to ask for the hand of a Chinese princess, and the importance of the mission is measured by the rank of the man sent.

The instructor's general claim: this is how we understand ancient history. A society attributes a cluster of achievements to one figure, and over centuries the collective remembering shapes a person who may never have existed in the form we inherit. And then the historian's job is not only to seek the truth of what happened, but to ask **why** people remember it this way, and how those interpretations shift across the present and the future.

### The group exercise

Four groups, four topics, roughly seven to eight minutes of preparation, five minutes each to present. Instructions given during setup:

- Four people per group. Raise your hand when formed, and earlier hands get first pick of topic.
- Divide the topic so each person delivers two or three sentences. That is enough for the time available.
- Decide your speaking order before you come to the front.
- Do not read from a laptop. Notes on paper or an iPad only, and even then, try not to read.
- Audience: laptops closed, full attention on the presenters.
- While each group presented, the instructor projected related sculpture and site photographs.

### Group 1: Queen Maya's dream and the birth

Maya was carried by four celestial beings to the Himalayas and cleansed in a lake. In the dream she saw a white elephant carrying a white lotus. Both elephant and lotus signify purity, and the dream marked the conception of an extraordinary being. The king summoned priests to interpret it; they predicted a son who would become a universal monarch if he stayed in the palace, or an enlightened Buddha if he left.

Instructor follow-ups after the presentation, which ran under time:

- The white elephant is a royal and superlative animal. **Why a white elephant** is a live interpretive question and a usable paper topic.
- In the iconography, the Buddha emerges not from the birth canal but from Maya's side.
- Maya died seven days after the birth. A student supplied this.
- The infant did not behave like an infant. He immediately walked, taking seven steps in each of the four directions, then pointed one index finger to the heavens and one to the earth and declared himself supreme between heaven and earth. The instructor's framing question: do you interpret that religiously or historically?

### Group 2: The Great Departure

After Maya's death, the father tried to steer his son away from the path of the enlightened sage by keeping him inside the palace with every material comfort and no exposure to suffering. He married and had a son. At about 29 he left the palace and encountered the **four sights**: an old man, a sick man, a corpse, and a holy man. For someone that sheltered this was entirely new, and it showed him that aging, sickness, and death are unavoidable. He then left his royal life, his wife, and his newborn son, cut off his hair, removed his royal clothing, and set out to find the cause of human suffering.

The presenter continued into the ascetic period and the enlightenment; the instructor stopped them, noting that was beyond the assigned topic, while saying the presentation itself was good.

His own emphasis afterward: the key point of this episode is the turning point. He had a wife, an infant son, and a life of luxury, and he abandoned all of it. What pushed him were the four facts nobody escapes: birth, aging, sickness, and death. No technique has yet made anyone live forever. Twenty-nine is neither very young nor old, which he read as the right stage of life to reflect on what a life is for.

He closed with a personal story from his first visit to **Bodh Gaya** and the Mahabodhi Temple. He had thought of praying to experience the four sights, and then on the path outside the temple he encountered them in sequence: a dead dog, an old man, a sick man lying and apparently near death, and a cow giving birth. His conclusion was the opposite of what he expected. These things are not unique to Bodh Gaya. They are everywhere and completely mundane, and we simply neglect them because we are young and expect a future. That, he argued, is why the Buddha's story keeps working on people. It is not a narrative to repeat. It is a prompt to apply to your own life.

### Group 3: Becoming an ascetic

Surrounded by worldly pleasure, then confronted with sadness, grief, and aging in the outside world, he saw the gap between his luxuries and the world's suffering and was deeply disturbed. He cut off his hair, left the palace with only a charioteer and a horse, and went to seek what he had glimpsed in the holy man. He began a six-year quest. He first studied meditation under two teachers, whose names the group could not recall, and mastered what they taught, but it did not resolve the problem. He then joined five ascetics and practiced extreme austerity until he was severely emaciated, and still did not reach awakening. Remembering a childhood experience of sitting under a tree and the state of mind he had felt there, he concluded that extreme asceticism does not work.

Instructor's summary: austerity does not produce awakening, and awakening here means finding the truth of the world.

He then showed two images. One is a famous **Gandhara-period fasting Buddha** in a museum in Pakistan, depicting the effects of *tapas*. Alongside it he read a passage describing the emaciation, in which touching the stomach means touching the spine, getting up means falling backwards, and rubbing the limbs makes the hair fall out. He attributed the passage to a well-known sutra; the title on the recording is garbled and sounds like Lalitavistara. The sequence he drew: two teachers, failure, frustration, then the decision to find his own way, which is what takes him to Bodh Gaya.

### Group 4: The Great Enlightenment

He regained his strength, which caused the five ascetics to abandon him for having given up austerity, and sat under the tree he remembered from childhood. He resolved not to be moved by material temptation. **Mara** came to distract him, deployed his daughters to tempt him, and threw weapons that turned to flowers on contact. When Mara challenged his right to enlightenment, he touched the ground and called the earth to witness the good deeds of his past lives, and the earth accepted. Having defeated Mara, he spent the rest of the night in meditation, recollecting his past lives, understanding how beings are reborn through karma, and realizing the Four Noble Truths. By dawn he was enlightened.

The instructor flagged one clear error: the tree is not an apple tree. A student supplied the correction, the **bodhi tree**.

### Closing: the Mahabodhi Temple and the bodhi tree

The site of the enlightenment, in Bihar in the Indian interior, very hot, and not to be visited in summer. Inside the Mahabodhi Temple is the Buddha statue; outside is the tree. He raised his hand question to the class and no one had been.

The tree there now is not the original. It is roughly a third-generation descendant. The original was destroyed, and a replacement descends from a cutting or seeds that had earlier been carried to Sri Lanka in connection with Ashoka's daughter, then brought back to Bodh Gaya and replanted. He attributed the destruction of the original to an Islamic invasion. None of this is covered in the assigned readings, so treat the details as lecture content rather than something you can cite.

He finished with photographs of pilgrims and offerings at the site, noting the robe colors identify pilgrims by country and tradition.

## Connections to the readings

**Ashoka and the Tibetan script.** Kapstein p. 2034 area: the Tibetan writing system derives from sixth- or seventh-century North Indian or Central Asian scripts descended from Brahmi, "which, in an early form, had been used to inscribe many of the edicts of the famous Indian monarch of the third century B.C.E., Ashoka." The same king whose pillar is the class's only evidence for the Buddha is also the reason the script chain reaches Tibet.

**Bodh Gaya in the Tibetan record.** Kapstein p. 4111 area: in 648 the Tibetans rescued a stranded Tang mission that penetrated as far south as Tirabhukti in northern Bihar, and that Chinese mission "succeeded in installing a memorial at Bodh Gaya, the site of the Buddha's enlightenment." Later Tibetan histories also claim Songtsen's troops erected a stele on the Ganges, which Kapstein says "cannot be dismissed out of hand." A seventh-century Tibetan presence at the exact site the class ended on.

**Attribution and collective memory, the Songtsen case.** This is the tightest link to the lecture's argument.

- Van Schaik p. 1339 area: Songtsen sends an ambassador in 634 to no effect, then four years later sends Gar Tongtsen, "Songtsen's prime minister and general right-hand man," to ask for a Chinese princess. The rank of the envoy is the measure of the mission.
- Van Schaik p. 1452 area: the bards' version of Gar's exploits, the beer test and the identification of the princess from a line-up of a hundred women. Van Schaik's own verdict is the phrase to remember: these stories are "entertaining and, if hardly historical, at least show us how affectionately Gar's resourceful nature was remembered by the Tibetans." That is the instructor's point in the source's own words, and it is quotable.
- Kapstein p. 4083 area: "Later tradition **credits** the princess with bringing the first Buddhist sacred image to Tibet, that of the Jowo ('Lord') Shakyamuni, and thus introducing Buddhism to that land." Note the verb. It is the same hedging move the class opened on.
- Kapstein p. 4067 and p. 4093 area: the princess of Wencheng was in fact intended for the crown prince Gungsong, not Songtsen. When Gungsong predeceased his father in 646 she became her father-in-law's queen, "and Tibetan posterity would always remember her primarily in this role, as the Chinese wife of Songtsen Gampo." A documented case of memory simplifying a person's history onto a single famous figure.
- Van Schaik p. 1493 area: "a (perhaps legendary) Nepalese princess." Kapstein p. 4115 area agrees that Tritsün, or Bhrikuti, "is so far only known from much later accounts and not at all from the earliest" sources. The same evidentiary problem the class applied to the Buddha, running inside the Tibetan material.

**The Jowo image.** Kapstein p. 15611 and p. 16854 areas track the Jowo Shakyamuni statue in Lhasa's central temple as a pilgrimage focus across centuries, which is a Tibetan instance of the Shakyamuni figure the etymology lesson was unpacking.

## Writing and reading-skills guidance

**Diction carries commitment.** "He is attributed as the founder" reports a tradition's claim without endorsing it. Before you write a sentence about a contested figure, decide how much you want to assert, then pick the verb that asserts exactly that much. Watch how the assigned authors do it: Kapstein writes "later tradition credits the princess," van Schaik writes "if hardly historical." Neither writer disowns the story, and neither vouches for it.

**Summary needs anchors, not recall.** The core lesson of the exercise. Faced with a mass of narrative, do not try to remember and reproduce every detail, and do not start writing at the first thing you recall. Identify the key events and turning points first. Those are your anchors. Then organize the summary around them. The instructor asked, of each episode, what the turning point is and what pushed the change, and that question is what generated a usable structure in a few minutes.

This matches the UNC handout on summary: an essay that "moves through the plot, history, or author's argument in chronological order, or in the exact same order the author used" is summarizing, not analyzing. Arranging evidence around your own points instead of the source's order is the analytical move.

**Presenting.** Decide the speaking order before you stand up. Give each speaker two or three sentences. Keep to the time. Do not read from a screen; work from memory and organize the words in your head. You will be nervous, and the instructor said so plainly rather than telling anyone not to be. The group that finished well under time was told the presentation was too short, so filling the allotted time is part of the task.

**Interpretation over recitation.** After the Maya's dream group finished, the instructor's questions were all interpretive: why a white elephant, how do you read the infant's declaration religiously versus historically. Description is the floor, not the deliverable. This applies directly to the midterm and final essays, both of which the syllabus says must not be summaries of lecture or discussion and must advance a new topic or a new analytical perspective.

**Precision matters in front of an audience.** Two factual slips surfaced during presentations, the apple tree for the bodhi tree, and cutting off his hands for his hair. Both were caught publicly. Check the specific nouns before you present or submit.

Nothing new was assigned and no deadlines were announced in this session. What was said:

- The exercise was explicitly framed as **training** for a skill that recurs. He noted he has asked for summaries repeatedly and will again, so the anchor method is meant to be reused.

- **Paper topic implied:** the student's point about Siddhartha being named in defiance of the prophecy drew "I will assign you this topic." The audio is broken around it, but it was treated as a real prospect.
