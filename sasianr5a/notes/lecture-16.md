## Session at a glance

Almost the whole session was a line-by-line workshop on a student's opening paragraph about the marriage of Songtsen Gampo and the Tang princess of Wencheng. The instructor used that one paragraph to teach four things: formatting and identification conventions, precision in word choice, logical transitions, and historical accuracy. Content-wise, the class covered the scale of Tibet versus Tang China in the seventh century and why Taizong preferred a marriage settlement to a war.

Course context: the midterm essay draft is due Fri. Oct. 23 at 7:00 a.m., with the Diagnostic Essay peer review Oct. 28 and the polished version Nov. 2 (syllabus, Weeks 9 to 11). Everything below is aimed at that draft.

## Formatting and mechanics

- US academic convention: no indent on the first paragraph, indent every subsequent paragraph. The instructor checked the sample on screen for this.
- Identify every historical person the first time they appear: who they are, when they lived, where they sit in the political order. Put lifespan or reign dates in brackets. The instructor said many students still skip this, and that getting it right "demonstrates you are well trained."
- The reason is functional, not decorative: dates tell the reader where in the historical framework you are standing, and the identification tells them how the person connects to everyone else.
- Several students gave a time period once and then dropped the habit for later figures. Apply it to all of them.

Identifications to have ready, confirmed against the readings:

| Figure | Identification |
|---|---|
| Songtsen Gampo | Tibetan ruler, c. 605 to 650, reigned from c. 617 (Kapstein, *The Tibetans*); van Schaik has him dying in 649, the same year as Taizong |
| Princess of Wencheng | Tang Chinese princess, brought to Tibet in 641 by the minister Gar Tongtsen; originally intended for the crown prince Gungsong Gungtsen, and after his death in 646 she became Songtsen Gampo's queen (Kapstein) |
| Taizong | Tang emperor, co-founder of the dynasty with his father after toppling the Sui, died 649 (van Schaik, "At the Court of the Great Tang") |
| Gar Tongtsen | Songtsen Gampo's prime minister and the envoy to the Tang court |

Note the wrinkle the instructor did not raise but the readings do: Wencheng was first married to the crown prince, not to Songtsen Gampo. If your essay treats her as his wife from the start, Kapstein contradicts you.

## Word choice and precision

- Prefer **ruler** to king or emperor. King and emperor carry different hierarchies in different political contexts, so they smuggle in claims you did not mean to make. "Ruler" is the neutral general term.
- Do not write **bodhisattva** when you can name the figure. Later Tibetan tradition made the two foreign brides emanations of goddesses, and Kapstein names Tara alongside Avalokiteshvara in the monkey-and-ogress origin myth. If you know which one, say which one. If you do not, "a Buddhist great being" is at least honest.
- Say **Tibet** or **the Tibetans**, not a vague "them," when the sentence is about Tibet. Several of the transcriber's renderings here are garbled, but the instruction is clear: name the referent.
- Name the relationship. If you mention the princess without mentioning that she was married to Songtsen Gampo, the reader cannot see why she matters.

## Transitions and the logical map

Transition words encode a specific logical relation, so use the one that matches:

- **therefore, thus, thereby, consequently** for cause and consequence
- **however, nevertheless** for contrast or denial
- **additionally** for a further, parallel point
- **specifically** for detail that belongs to the sentence just before, which is a different move from "additionally"

The failure the instructor diagnosed in one draft: three consecutive sentences using *however*, *however*, *nevertheless*. The first *however* works because it contrasts with the preceding sentence. The second and third leave the reader unable to reconstruct what contrasts with what. If sentence two negates sentence one, and sentence three also signals negation, is it negating sentence one or sentence two? The chain collapses.

The rule to carry into revision: when you write each sentence, know where you are on the logical map. Are you comparing, negating, qualifying, or adding? Pick the word that says so.

## Worked example: diagnosing one bad sentence

The draft sentence, roughly: "During the reign of Songtsen Gampo and his marriage to Princess Wencheng, most of his government were not Buddhist despite her efforts at conversion."

Four separate problems:

1. **False parallelism.** Joining "his reign" and "his marriage" with *and* presents them as parallel items. They are not. The reign is the time frame; the marriage is the event you actually want to discuss. Foreground the marriage and subordinate the reign: "his marriage to the princess of Wencheng, deliberately arranged during Songtsen Gampo's reign, ..."
2. **Missing information.** The marriage was intentionally arranged. Saying so adds the causal content the sentence needs. The readings support it: Gar was sent to the Tang court to request a princess, and Songtsen took Amdo by force and threatened to march on China when the first request was refused (van Schaik).
3. **Wrong noun.** "His government" is an abstract entity and cannot hold a religion. You mean **his ministers**.
4. **Historical inaccuracy.** There is no evidence in the readings that the princess of Wencheng intended to convert Tibet into a Buddhist state. What the readings say is narrower: she brought a statue of the Buddha, the first to arrive in Tibet, placed in the Ramoche temple and later moved to the Jokhang, where it is known as the Jowo (van Schaik p. 10; Kapstein). You can argue from that toward an intention, but you have to mark it as your inference, not report it as fact.

And a fifth point about evidence: the sentence states a conclusion without showing where it came from. Attribute it. "According to van Schaik's *Tibet: A History*," or according to the annals, or according to whichever specific material you are using. The instructor's phrasing: you are telling your reader, not asking them to take your word for it. (The transcript's "tongue annals" is ambiguous. It is either the Tang annals, which Kapstein cites for the princess's arrival, or the Old Tibetan Annals, which van Schaik lists among the Dunhuang manuscripts. Check which one you actually mean before citing it.)

## The introduction paragraph pattern

Three moves, in order:

1. **Context and topic.** What are we going to talk about?
2. **Narrowing.** Indicate the key point of the topic.
3. **Your position.** Do you support, agree, or disagree, and what materials are you using?

Detail does not belong here. Mention the evidence; save the specifics for the body paragraphs. An introduction that unpacks a full example has stolen a body paragraph's job. This matches the UNC *Introductions* handout, which calls the introduction a road map that conveys topic, importance, and plan, and carries the thesis.

The sample thesis the instructor praised, roughly as written: *their marriage was not a success in spreading Buddhism into Tibet as a religious conversion; the marriage mainly provided peaceful relationships and cultural exchange between early Tibet and Tang China.* It works because the writer stated and highlighted a main argument.

## Argument and revision workflow

- **Highlight your main argument** in the submitted draft. The instructor asked for this on the last assignment; about 90% of the class did it. The point is not to help the grader. It is to force you to know what your argument is.
- **Test every body paragraph against it.** Does this paragraph connect to the main argument, support it, or complicate it?
- **Do not stop at the finished draft.** Revise in two passes, in this order:
  1. **Structure.** Do the paragraphs and the evidence actually connect to and support the argument?
  2. **Language.** If time allows.
- "Polish your language" does not mean fixing your English. It means expressing your thoughts accurately, precisely, and clearly, and removing ambiguities. The instructor was explicit that this second pass is optional given how busy the class is, and that structure plus a clear main argument is the requirement. Compare the UNC *Revising Drafts* handout: revision "should be concerned with the large issues in the paper, not the commas."

## Academic register

- **Drop "so."** It is colloquial. Use *therefore, thus, thereby, consequently, in short, in brief*.
- **"In my opinion"** is usable but reads like high school writing.
- **"I think"** is allowed but should not open sentences repeatedly. It signals "this is my opinion," which is true, but overuse makes the whole essay sound subjective. Use a marker like this once, and let the reader infer the rest from your framing.
- **Hedge deliberately.** "Not successful" imports a value judgment about good and bad. "Did not largely spread Buddhism in Tibet" is neutral and describable. Prefer the neutral, softer formulation before the extreme one. This is not false modesty: you genuinely do not have enough evidence to assert 100%.
- Similarly, "a religious conversion" is vaguer than "to convert Tibet into a Buddhist state," and the second version is also the one you can actually test against the sources.

## Historical accuracy: scale and motive

The instructor's substantive warning was about anachronism. Students wrote "the Tibetan empire" and treated seventh-century Tibet as a peer competitor of Tang China. Corrections:

- At this stage, call it the **nascent Tibetan state**, not the Tibetan empire. van Schaik's own framing: as far as the Chinese court was concerned, "Tibet remained an obscure little kingdom in the barbarian south."
- Through the seventh and into the mid eighth century, before the An Lushan Rebellion, Tang China was among the most powerful states in the world, with influence extending into Central Asia. Its territory dwarfed Tibet's. It also had a far longer institutional and literate history behind it. Tibet at this point lacked a comparably systematic administrative and ideological apparatus. Songtsen Gampo's reign is when writing itself arrives, via Tonmi Sambhota, and when the empire is first organized into the five *ru* or "horns" (van Schaik, "Cultural Capital," pp. 11 to 12).
- Even from a Tibetan perspective, the honest claim is that Tibet could mobilize force to **harass** China's borders, not occupy or conquer China.
- Why did Taizong settle? The instructor's account: Taizong's strategic focus was Korea, and he wanted to move military resources northeast for that campaign, so a marriage alliance was the cheapest way to keep the border quiet. Flag this one. The readings assigned so far do not contain the Korea campaign as Taizong's motive; van Schaik mentions Korea only as the eastern limit of the earlier Han empire. Treat it as lecture context and do not cite a reading for it.
- What the readings do support about the marriage's effects: it "ushered in two decades of peace," Tibetan aristocrats went to study in Chang'an, Chinese papermakers and ink-makers came to Tibet along with silkworms and millstones, and the princess persuaded the Tibetan nobility toward Chinese silk (van Schaik p. 10). That is cultural exchange and peace, which is exactly the sample thesis.
- On Buddhism, van Schaik's caution is worth quoting in a draft: the princesses, their statues, and their encouragement of temple-building "became the defining images of Songtsen's rule. At the time, though, neither Songtsen nor his courtiers are likely to have perceived things in this way." Buddhism was "only one of many new cultural imports." Kapstein calls Songtsen Gampo's personal faith in Buddhism "perhaps the greatest mystery" and notes that even Tibetan Buddhist historians sidestep it.
- The devotional reading of Wencheng, including her identification as a divine emanation and the Jowo as Tibet's greatest pilgrimage object, is **later** Tibetan tradition. Kapstein is explicit that in these traditions "the political history of the empire appears as a matter of secondary importance." Kapstein also notes that the later princess of Jincheng was probably the stronger figure, and that her legacy was conflated with her aunt Wencheng's. If your essay is about what the marriage accomplished, distinguish the seventh-century record from the tradition built on top of it.

## Revision checklist for the midterm draft

1. First paragraph unindented, all others indented.
2. Every person named on first appearance with dates, role, and relation.
3. "Ruler" instead of king or emperor unless the specific title is the point.
4. Main argument stated in the introduction and highlighted.
5. Each transition word matched to an actual logical relation, no chained contrastives.
6. No *and* joining items that are not parallel.
7. Every conclusion attributed to a specific reading.
8. "Nascent Tibetan state," not "Tibetan empire," for the seventh century.
9. Claims about intent marked as inference.
10. No "so" as a connective; "I think" at most once.
11. Structure pass before language pass.
