# Lecture 17: Reading Scholarship and Diagnosing Opening Paragraphs

## Summary
A writing-heavy session, held as a Review and Discussion class. The instructor opened by walking through an online Tibetan canon reading room (translation / compare / source views) and asked students to read a sutra translation cold, then re-read with the glossary, paying attention to both content and the physical format of Tibetan scripture. He then set out two assignments: a summary-and-analysis of an assigned scholarly paper, structured around three questions (main argument, historiographical intervention, organization), and a midterm paper proposal. The second half was a workshop on student opening paragraphs, mostly showing what fails: sentences with no logical link, main arguments that appear with no setup, lists of causes with no stated relationship between them, note-like prose, "Tibetan empire" used anachronistically for the seventh century, and badly unbalanced paragraph lengths.

## Lecture notes

### The online canon reading room and reading a sutra twice
The instructor shared a link to a digital translation of a Tibetan canonical text. The page has three views: **translation**, **compare**, and **source**. The link lands on the translation by default, but he does not want students to ignore the other two.

- The front matter of the page explains the traditional formats of Tibetan books, including bound booklet form and scroll form. He asked students to glance at this. Format matters, not just content: what the scripture physically is, and how the canon arranges its texts.
- Titles appear in Tibetan with the Sanskrit title and the English translation inserted alongside.
- **compare** places the English translation next to the classical Tibetan. Students who read some classical Tibetan can consult it; everyone else should look at how the translation is organized.
- Linked names in the translation open glossary entries: Tibetan spelling plus an explanation. The example he clicked described a bodhisattva as one of the eight close sons of the Buddha and as the embodiment of compassion. From the named interlocutors (Śāriputra, Avalokiteśvara) and the mention of *prajñāpāramitā* (perfection of wisdom), the text on screen appears to be the Heart Sutra. The transcript garbles the names, so treat the identification as likely rather than certain.
- Method he prescribed: read the text once with no glossary and no notes, gauge how much you actually understood, then read it again with the links open and write down what still does not make sense. Know who the figures are.
- Why now: the next chapter begins with Buddhist philosophy, which is what this sutra text is about. He also named a second topic for the next chapter; the transcriber renders it "Chosun-Diltsa," which is unintelligible. From context (a ruler closely tied to Buddhist philosophy in the next stretch of van Schaik) it is most likely Trisong Detsen, but the audio does not confirm it. Check bCourses.
- Relevant to the midterm: the paper does not have to be political or military history. Buddhism, Buddhist history, individual figures, and scriptures are all acceptable topics.

### Assignment: summary and analysis of a scholarly paper
"Analysis" here does not mean critique. It means analytical work on how the paper functions. The paper's author name is garbled in the transcript, so confirm which paper on bCourses. Organize the paper around three questions, in order:

1. **What is the paper's main argument?** The single most important thing to extract from any academic paper. It is usually condensed into a sentence or two, so it can be hard to spot.
2. **What historiographical or scholastic intervention does the author seek to make?** Phrased other ways: what is the author's contribution to academia? Who is the author trying to communicate with? What question is the author trying to solve? The instructor conceded that genuinely evaluating a contribution is very hard for undergraduates, because you do not have the rest of the field in view. So answer the narrower version: what question does the author want to answer, what problem concerns them.
3. **How is the paper organized?** The structure: once there is a main argument, the author needs a framework of sub-arguments that establish it. Then step back and ask why the author arranged it that way, and whether the arrangement actually does establish the main argument.

He connected this to the passage read in the previous Friday's class, which he described as: main argument in the first sentence, then two sentences on Korea and two on Japan under colonial capitalism, then a one-sentence conclusion. Roughly seven sentences total. Mapping a structure like that is the first step, but not enough on its own.

### Reading is the harder skill
He reminded the class that when asked earlier in the semester whether reading or composition is harder, most students said writing. At first that feels true, because writing takes visible effort. But the reason writing is slow is usually that the concepts are not clear in your head: you have fragments and no map, so you are guided and distracted by your thoughts instead of controlling them. You cannot find the logical connection between the pieces because you did not read closely enough to know where each concept begins and ends, from what perspective it is argued, and what it connotes.

So: do not read passively. A dramatic scene holds your attention on its own; dry academic prose does not, and you have to activate the reading yourself. The same three questions (argument, contribution, structure) are the fundamental steps for all academic reading, books or articles, in any field, and they are also the steps to follow for your own composition.

### Assignment: midterm paper proposal
Next week's work is not the midterm paper itself but the proposal for it. The proposal is a genre worth practicing: it is the same move as a statement of purpose for an MA or doctoral application, or a fellowship or grant application.

Finding a topic. Count the material covered so far and find something either never mentioned in class, or mentioned but never elaborated on, or approached from a different angle. His example: the marriage alliance approached not from the Tibetan side but by asking what China gained from it. That was never discussed in class and is still on topic. The midterm is a summary-and-research paper, so it summarizes, but it also has to contain something you found and some idea of your own.

The proposal answers three questions.

**What is your topic?** Not yet a main argument. State the focus narrowly, and above all avoid a topic that is too broad. His anti-example: claiming the Tibetan empire became the most powerful empire of its era, which would require comparative knowledge of states the class has not studied. The midterm is only about 1,000 words and the final about 1,500, so a specific topic is easier to support with evidence.

**Why have you chosen this topic?** Do not simply describe your personal interest. Instead explain its academic significance: what question, problem, debate, gap, or perspective does it address. He pushed back on vague answers like "it contributes to the field," since nobody at this stage knows the field's trends or history well enough to say that honestly. Name a problem, debate, or gap instead. Close with a modest statement of aim ("I aim to," "I attempt to"), and he dislikes "I try to" as clumsy.

A student asked whether personal opinions can appear in the analysis. Yes. A personal opinion is not the same as a personal interest. Interest can be part of it, but "I am interested in this" alone is not academic writing.

**How will you approach the topic?** This is your evidence, in three parts: what materials you will use (he gestured at the Heart Sutra as an example of a possible source), what evidence those materials yield (go check the actual chapters and sections), and how you will organize the paper.

On method: do not start from an argument and then hunt for sources to prop it up. That is backwards. Research starts from a topic that looks promising, then goes to the materials, with an explicit methodology. Different subfields use different methods and source types: broader economic history, political history, religious history.

Note the parallel: topic / why / how in the proposal maps onto main argument / intervention / structure in the reading assignment.

### Workshop on opening paragraphs
He said the class has improved markedly since the first essay, and that the big-picture framing of a paper is mostly working. Now the focus shifts to detail.

**Opening paragraph length.** Two paragraphs of lead-in before the argument is too much. If the first paragraph takes up too much of the paper, the rest loses its weight.

**Example 1 (too short, disconnected).** Roughly: in the early seventh century the Tibetan state transformed from a fragmented state into a centralized and unified empire; the unification was completed during the reign of Songtsen Gampo; the transformation can be credited to alliances, the introduction of Buddhism, and the development of a formal written language.
- A student's first reaction: it lacks context. He agreed.
- Sentences one and two have no logical connection and could be merged into a single sentence, something like: in the seventh century a new state emerged in the Himalayan region under Songtsen Gampo.
- Always supply who Songtsen Gampo is. Almost everyone remembers him by now, but readers outside the field will not, and he is not a figure every reader will recognize.
- The jump to the main argument in sentence three is abrupt. Signal first that the emergence of this state had causes that need analysis, list the candidate causes, then narrow: among these, A, B, and C are the most crucial. Then state the argument.
- "Alliances" is unspecified. Marital alliances? Military alliances? Identify which.
- The three causes are presented as a list with no stated relationship. If they are connected, say how. Better versions: name the actual states in the marriage alliances; frame Buddhism as the introduction of a more advanced culture; frame the writing system as an improvement in administrative instruments.

**Example 2 (better opening, weak transitions).** Roughly: before Songtsen Gampo the region was divided into castles and small powers engaged in violent feuding; Songtsen Gampo inherited an unstable kingdom after his father's death triggered rebellion.
- He liked the first sentence: it gives the reader a picture of the geography and the social situation at once.
- It still needs transition words. Something like "after X, he successfully established the empire," and then a framing of the strategies, divine legitimacy, strategic expansion, cultural base.
- He corrected a slip in the student's wording: not "divine leadership" but divine legitimacy.

**Example 3 (too long, anachronistic, note-like).** The opening ran long, mixing consolidation of competing polities, development of governing institutions, expansion, and Songtsen Gampo described as ruler of an empire in the seventh century.
- **The anachronism, which many students made.** In the seventh century Tibet was not an empire. It was a small kingdom that gradually annexed surrounding states and grew, but at that point it could not compete with India or China. Tibet becomes an empire later. When he says "Tibetan empire," he means that later period, not this one.
- Be deliberate with political vocabulary. State, country, kingdom, empire have distinct meanings and boundaries in political history, even if the edges are fuzzy. Roughly: state as in the United States; country sometimes for a smaller unit; kingdom for an independent small political unit; empire for something genuinely large, Roman, Chinese. "Empire" also carries a connection to colonialism.
- **Claims about intention need hedging.** "Had very strong political ambitions" is subjective and the student had no concrete evidence. Use qualifying language: it seems he intended to, based on the materials it appears that. The claim is about your reading of the evidence, not about what was in his head. This is accuracy.
- The sentence about military victories and foreign relations contributing to growth while control required practical government read like notes stitched together, not academic prose. Restructure the sentences and use academic register.
- When he asked whether the class could tell what the paragraph was about, the answer was no. He said he was lost in it too. Too much information, no clear picture, and especially hard on a reader with no background in the field.

**Example 4 (argument buried, wrong signposts).** Roughly: the early Tibetan state formed during the reign of Songtsen Gampo, who was the thirty-something ruler; before his reign the region was divided among tribes and regional powers; "the main argument of this essay is that military expansion was one of the most important forces behind the formation of the early Tibetan empire"; then back to how he strengthened his rule by military expansion and transformed the Yarlung kingdom; then "to understand why force comes first, however, one must begin with the political world he inherited."
- **Order is wrong.** The sentence about his rank and the sentences about transforming the Yarlung kingdom belong before the main argument, not after it. The argument should not sit in the middle of the paragraph.
- **Do not write "the main argument of this essay is."** Academic usage: "I argue," "I contend," "in my view," "to my mind." "This essay argues" is acceptable and common. And do not overuse "I think," once is fine, not throughout.
- **The final transition sentence belongs at the start of the next paragraph**, not at the end of this one.
- The sequence for an opening paragraph: context, then raise the topic, explain it briefly, then the main argument.
- **Number conventions in the humanities** (not the sciences): spell out one through ten. Above ten, if the number is still one word, such as twenty, spell it out. If it cannot be written in one word, use digits. Scientific writing just uses digits throughout.
- **How many sentences per paragraph?** High school rules about five or six do not apply here. It depends on what the paragraph needs to do.
- Where the main argument sits in the intro: not in the second sentence. The second sentence raises the topic, and the topic is not the argument. His illustration: the topic is UC Berkeley as a public university; the argument is that its education, teams, or structure make it the best.

**Example 5 (the model).** Roughly: early Tibet was divided, ruled by feuding chiefs with no administrative system and no writing; this lasted until Songtsen Gampo unexpectedly took control at thirteen, after his father's sudden death; he was given a fragile kingdom constantly disrupted by widespread rebellion; however, he transformed the divided land into a powerful and unified state; instead of relying on one method, he used many tools; through a connected system of political legitimacy, smart marriages, a written language to run the government, and Buddhism to connect with more developed and powerful neighbors.
- A student identified why it works: the sentence before the main argument establishes that these elements connect to each other rather than standing alone.
- The context sentence does real work, and the sentence on his accession compresses the age, the unexpectedness, and the father's death into one place, details the class had covered.
- "Instead of relying on one method, he used many tools" is a working transition into the argument.
- The argument is specific in every term. Not legitimacy in general but **political** legitimacy, distinguished from inherited or economic legitimacy. Not marriage but **smart** marriage, or marital alliances. Not a writing system in the abstract but a writing system **for running the administration**, which both clarifies things for the reader and narrows what the writer has to prove. Not Buddhism in the abstract but Buddhism as a medium for engaging more developed and powerful neighbors, that is, Buddhism as **foreign relations and diplomacy**, not as personal conviction.
- Laying out an argument this way makes the rest of the paper much easier to develop, keeps the logic coherent, and keeps irrelevant information out.
- He added a caveat: he hopes it was not produced by AI.

### Paragraph balance in a full paper
With five minutes left he put up a paper that was good except for one problem, and asked for impressions of its first, second, third, fourth paragraphs and conclusion. A student said it felt long. The sequence was: first paragraph fine, second short, third longer, fourth very long.

- Keep paragraph lengths roughly balanced. He compared it to mathematics or economics: count the sentences in a paragraph and make each one carry a specific meaning. No irrelevant additions, no missing steps.
- Very long paragraphs are something a seasoned scholar can get away with and you should not. They bore the reader and lose their attention; you end up skimming.
- The rule for splitting: **one crucial point per paragraph.** Within that point you may touch several perspectives. Perspectives you only mention can stay together in one paragraph. If you elaborate on a point at length, give it its own paragraph. If you elaborate on two points, separate them.
- The opposite failure, from a former student, was every paragraph only two or three sentences. Too short.
- The model remains the passage from the previous Friday: main argument one sentence, the point one sentence, Korea two sentences, Japan two sentences, conclusion one sentence. Seven sentences, neither long nor short, and completely clear.

## Key people, places and terms

- **Songtsen Gampo** (transcribed variously as "Sunan Temple," "Sun Zhang Jiangbo," "Sultan Kanko," "Sung San Rambo"). The ruler under whom the Tibetan state was unified. Van Schaik: he was thirteen when his father was poisoned, and the parts of the kingdom absorbed from other clan leaders rose in insurgency ("Tibet, A History," around the pages covering Songtsen's accession). Note that van Schaik says **poisoned**; several student drafts said "assassination," which is a stronger and less supported word. He later stepped down when his own son turned thirteen, and resumed the throne when the prince died.
- **Yarlung.** The valley in Ü that was the original seat of the dynasty; Kapstein tracks the transition of "the Yarlung kings from local chieftains to Inner Asian emperors." Lhasa lies in a neighboring valley system of the Yarlung Tsangpo.
- **Pre-imperial fragmentation.** Kapstein cites the frequently repeated list of **twelve minor kingdoms and forty principalities**. The student draft discussed in class said "45 castles"; check Kapstein before using any such figure.
- **Tsenpo.** The title of the Tibetan ruler, used throughout both van Schaik and Kapstein, more precise than "king" or "emperor."
- **Empire vs kingdom vs state vs country.** Distinct categories in political history. Seventh-century Tibet is a kingdom; the empire comes later.
- **Avalokiteśvara.** Bodhisattva of compassion, one of the eight close sons of the Buddha. Kapstein spells it Avalokiteshvara and makes him Tibet's special protector, with Songtsen Gampo understood as his manifestation. He appears in the Heart Sutra alongside **Śāriputra**.
- **Prajñāpāramitā**, the perfection of wisdom. The textual family the sutra on screen belongs to.
- **Divine legitimacy**, as distinct from inherited legitimacy, economic legitimacy, and political legitimacy. The instructor insisted on the distinction.
- **Marital alliance** (not "alliance" unqualified).
- Two names in the transcript are unrecoverable: the author of the paper assigned for summary and analysis, and the second topic of the next chapter ("Chosun-Diltsa," most likely Trisong Detsen). Confirm both on bCourses.

## Connections to the readings

- **Songtsen Gampo's accession at thirteen and his father's poisoning**: van Schaik, "Tibet: A History," in the chapter on the rise of the Tibetan kingdom. This is the detail the model opening paragraph compressed well.
- **Pre-unification fragmentation**: Kapstein, "The Tibetans," chapter on the imperial period, where the twelve minor kingdoms and forty principalities appear, along with the Drigum Tsenpo legend and the conflict between the Yarlung ruler and the Lord of Zingpo.
- **Avalokiteśvara and Songtsen Gampo**: Kapstein, chapter four, on Songtsen Gampo as a manifestation of Avalokiteshvara, with his Nepalese and Chinese queens. This is the point where the sutra reading and the political history meet: Buddhism as both doctrine and an instrument of foreign relations, which is exactly the move the model paragraph made.
- **Buddhism as diplomacy with more developed neighbors**: the framing the instructor praised lines up with van Schaik's account of the empire's dealings with Tang China and with India.
- **The paper-reading protocol** (argument, intervention, organization) is the method behind the UNC "Reading to Write" handout, and the Rong Xinjiang and Valerie Hansen Dunhuang article, "The Nature of the Dunhuang Library Cave and the Reasons for Its Sealing" (Cahiers d'Extrême-Asie 11, 1999-2000, pp. 247-275), is the clearest example in the course readings of a paper with an explicit historiographical intervention: it surveys and rejects earlier theories before advancing its own.
- **One crucial point per paragraph**: UNC "Paragraphs" handout, which calls this the "controlling idea" and lists unity and coherence as the criteria. Its troubleshooting section names the exact failure the instructor diagnosed, a paragraph with more than one controlling idea.
- **Opening paragraph structure and length**: UNC "Introductions."
- **Checking whether each paragraph has one idea**: UNC "Evidence" recommends a reverse outline for this, and UNC "Reorganizing Drafts" makes the same point.
- **Hedging claims about intention**: UNC "Evidence" and "Quotations" on not overstating what a source supports.

## Writing and reading-skills guidance

Reading:
1. Read once cold. Gauge your real comprehension.
2. Re-read with glossary and notes. Write down what you still do not understand. Identify the figures.
3. Attend to form as well as content: how the canon is arranged, how the translation is organized, what the physical book is.
4. For any academic paper: main argument, intervention, structure. In that order, every time.

Writing:
- Opening paragraph sequence: context, topic, brief explanation of the topic, main argument. Argument at the end, not the middle, and not in the second sentence.
- Keep the opening short enough that the body of the paper still carries weight.
- Every sentence in the opening must connect logically to the one before it. Merge sentences that are really one idea.
- Identify every figure you name, even familiar ones.
- Never list causes without saying how they relate. If you claim three factors, say why those three and how they connect.
- Specify every abstraction: which kind of alliance, which kind of legitimacy, what the writing system was for, what Buddhism was doing.
- Do not call seventh-century Tibet an empire. Choose political vocabulary deliberately.
- Hedge claims about intention and motive. "It appears that," "based on the materials."
- Say "I argue," "I contend," "this essay argues." Not "the main argument of this essay is." Use "I think" at most once.
- "Attempt to," not "try to."
- Put transition sentences at the start of the next paragraph.
- One crucial point per paragraph. Group the points you only mention; split the ones you elaborate.
- No fixed sentence count per paragraph. Balance lengths across the paper and make every sentence carry meaning.
- Humanities number convention: spell out anything expressible in one word, digits otherwise.
- Avoid note-like prose. Academic register, restructured sentences.
