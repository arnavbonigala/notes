# Lecture 21: Causes and Conditions, Samsara and Nirvana, and the Marks of Existence

## Summary

This was a philosophy session with no Tibetan political history in it. The instructor continued the analysis of causes and conditions begun in the previous class, working through four test cases in order: an external object (a tree, carried over from last time), an abstract collective entity (this class), the person and the body, and finally a thought. Each turns out to be produced by causes and conditions, so the tentative conclusion is that everything internal and external is conditioned, which is the same thing as saying everything is impermanent.

From impermanence the lecture moved to why impermanence is unsatisfactory, which is the description of samsara, and then to nirvana, defined entirely by negation as the unconditioned. The last third introduced the three marks of existence (impermanence, unsatisfactoriness, no-self) and the Mahayana four Dharma seals, which add the assertion of nirvana as peace. Along the way the instructor made two arguments worth keeping: that karma is not a single-cause line but a dense and partly unknowable web, and that because no one short of a Buddha can see the full set of causes and conditions, nobody has standing to explain your suffering back to you as your own karma.

Reference points from class: the instructor describes the session as the Monday 11 to 12 slot in Valley Life Sciences, which matches the syllabus (MWF 11:00 to 11:59 a.m., 2066 Valley Life Sciences).

## Lecture notes

### Recap: the tree as a gathering of causes and conditions

The session opened mid-thought, continuing the previous class. An external phenomenon such as a tree is a gathering of causes and conditions, not a thing with its own independent existence. The instructor then moved deliberately from something concrete to something abstract.

### Second case: is "this class" an entity?

You tell your family you have to go to a classroom in Valley Life Sciences on Monday morning, so in ordinary speech the class sounds like it has an identity. The class then listed its causes and conditions:

- the students' wish to take it and their enrollment
- the instructor's own wish and obligation to teach it
- the syllabus, the assignments and the meetings
- the time slot, 11 to 12 on the scheduled days; after that the class does not exist
- the room, the screen, the projector and the slides

The instructor then sorted main from subsidiary causes by removal. If the room is blocked, the class moves outside, he drops the PowerPoint and talks instead, and the class still happens. Room and screen are therefore subsidiary. If the instructor does not show up, the class cannot continue, so he is a main cause. If one student disappears, the class continues, so no single student is a main cause.

The conclusion: apart from the concept "a Tibetan class," there is no concrete unchanging entity, no "meta-class," that we can point to. The causes and conditions rely on each other. He relies on the students, they rely on him to organize it, and all of it relies on a supportive environment.

### Comparing the tree and the class

Asked whether the class has more complicated causes than a tree, students argued both sides. Yes: a class needs people, and multiple independent causes that each have to function. No: the same is true of a tree, since every condition has to hold (no seed, no tree, just as no student and no teacher, no class). The instructor's point was not to settle it but to show that causes and conditions come in multiple levels and kinds, and that we do not need to manipulate or control all of them in order to get a result.

### Karma: two common mistakes

This is the key point of the session on karma.

Mistake one: thinking karma is linear, one main cause plus a few conditions producing one result. It is not. Karma is as complicated as a state or an international relationship, and it is collective. Much of it we cannot identify or enumerate at all. The analogy offered was perceptual training: you cannot currently hear the movement of your own internal organs, but with practice you could. What you cannot detect is not therefore absent.

Mistake two: thinking that because karma is complex, nothing can be done. Buddhism and other traditions hold that you can change karma, because to a certain extent you can manipulate the main causes and conditions, and you do not have to accept the result as given.

### The large language model analogy

The instructor's modern example of how karma functions. Engineers train a model: they feed in instructions, which play the role of causes and conditions. The machine produces an output, which is the result. They evaluate whether the result is satisfactory, and if not they adjust the instructions and certain parameters and feed them back in. But they do not know how the model actually learns or organizes what it learns. It is a black box. They can reliably get satisfactory results without knowing the process.

That, he argued, is the structure of karma: we can partially manipulate causes and conditions to get a result while remaining unable to see how the process breaks down internally. His framing was that karma is not a pre-modern idea that science has retired; the same logic operates in our external and scientific world now.

### The ethical warning: who gets to explain your suffering

A practical caution for anyone entering Buddhist or other religious settings. People will tell you that you suffer this because you did that, converting your suffering into a karmic or causal explanation. When you are in a bad state or low mood, that story can sound plausible, and then you have blinded yourself. He said he has encountered a great deal of this.

The rebuttal he offered is procedural rather than doctrinal. Ask them who they are and on what authority they claim to know your karma. In Buddhism only one figure, the Buddha, sees all causes and conditions clearly and completely. The point is not to glorify the Buddha but to tell people that since we are not Buddhas, none of us has the omniscient view required to make that claim. A causal story about your suffering might be true to some extent, but it is very unlikely to be the full set of causes and conditions.

He extended this to gendered versions of the same move: labels and expectations assigned to women on the grounds that they are women. He described East Asia as very conservative on this and then said that after coming to the US he sometimes found America equally conservative or more so, citing the script in which a woman should marry well, stay home and have children.

### Third case: the person and the "I"

Is there an independent entity called a person? Students offered aging, having to eat, and getting sick. The body depends on nutrition, food, sleep, clothing for warmth, and work, and the instructor added medical systems and insurance later in the session.

Then the thought experiment. Cut off an arm or a leg and you survive, inefficiently (a student's word), so the "I" does not reside in a limb. Cut off the head and you do not survive, which suggests the "I" is in the head. So where is the mind?

Students split between head and heart. The instructor's illustration: when athletes win at the Olympics and the anthem plays, they put a hand on the heart, not on the head. A student suggested the heart is the center of emotion. But heart transplants succeed in many cases, and the recipient's mind continues, so the physical flesh heart cannot be the mind. Brain transplants do not exist, though a student raised head transplant experiments on monkeys, in which case the "I" might travel with the head. Coma was raised as another unclear case: we do not know whether the "I" is still present.

He left the question open as a reflection exercise. What was settled is the smaller claim: the body is conditioned, dependent on food, air and much else.

### Fourth case: thoughts

The subtlest level. He first tied it to the writing assignment: you all have views, and behind your main argument there is a view, so is your main argument conditioned or unconditioned, and if conditioned, how? A student answered that their views are shaped by their upbringing and by what the course has told them about Tibet. The instructor agreed and expanded the list: the lectures, the assigned readings, his stated expectations, plus each student's background knowledge, ethnicity, religion, culture and language.

Then a one-minute in-class exercise. Take the thought you are having right now and ask whether it is conditioned or unconditioned. The class answered conditioned; nobody defended the unconditioned side. He suggested keeping the practice up after class and trying to find a counterexample.

### Conclusion of the four analyses

Tree (external object), class (collective concept), person and body (the most apparent case, since we can see and touch it), and thought (the deeper, subtler case) are all conditioned. The brain and coma question was explicitly left unresolved in this class.

Tentative conclusion: everything, the internal "I" and the external world alike, is subject to conditions and produced by causes and conditions. We can therefore construct the whole world on this logic. The formula he wrote: causes plus conditions produce the result, A plus B equals C.

### From conditionality to impermanence

If the result depends on the conditions, then changing one condition or several changes the result. Hence everything in the world is changing, which is impermanence. His chemistry example: add an element to water or a solvent and the color changes.

Definition requested from the class and accepted: impermanence means always changing. Nothing constant, nothing eternal. He told the class to just remember this as the essence.

### Why impermanence is unsatisfactory

The harder question. Change is not only change for the worse. Someone poor wins the lottery and becomes rich, which is change from bad to good. So why is the conclusion unsatisfactoriness?

Answers and arguments from the room:

- we are more afraid of loss than we are gratified by gain
- nothing keeps you happy for long, and you cannot maintain a good state

The instructor's version is a treadmill argument. There is always something to fix, and the fixing is endless. When you are young you want a dog or a toy or a game console and believe it will make you happy. Then your goal moves: get into a good middle school, then a good university, then Berkeley, then Wall Street or Silicon Valley for the money, then a big house, then marriage. The prince and princess do not live happily ever after. Couples attracted to each other tire of each other, quarrel, divorce, and start again, and each time with a new thing or a new partner they say this time it will be happiness. Eventually you find there is no such thing.

The reason is structural. You are changing, the world is changing, and it is out of your control. Because you are conditioned, you cannot maintain a peaceful state. That is samsara.

### His practical advice

He explicitly said he is not asking students to be pessimistic about life or to abandon samsara. His advice was to graduate, find a good job rather than jumping straight into a graduate program, build relationships, earn money. Meanwhile keep this analysis in mind and bring it to bear on your life. Knowing the nature of samsara means not pouring all your energy and all your expectations into the success of one single thing. You take part in the game knowing the result could be good or not good, and you are not entangled by it. That, he said, is already the first step of wisdom.

### Nirvana and the method of negation

The definition shown in class: unconditioned, not produced from conditions, not arising from samskaras, unchanging. (The exact wording on the slide is partly unintelligible in the recording.)

The methodological point is the interesting one. Describing a person directly gives you a picture: she is an Indian woman, light hair, glasses, earrings. Nirvana is not described that way. It is defined as the opposite of samsara, which is to say by negation. It does not assert anything positive.

Can you find anything unconditioned in this world? He offered two candidates:

1. Space. Debatable, because it is unclear whether space is a concept or a phenomenon. He set this aside rather than resolving it.
2. The statement "everything in the world is impermanent." This is the one truth that cannot be negated. Every other so-called truth can be found and attacked. (Note: the transcript is garbled here, and the self-referential problem, whether that sentence is itself impermanent, is raised but not resolved.)

### Nirvana as peace, and the limits of language

He asked the class to describe the blissful or peaceful state of nirvana from personal experience. Students tried (one offered a quiet room where you lack nothing) and the attempts did not land, which was the point. Nirvana is beyond ordinary human experience. We are still in samsara, so we cannot taste a state that lies outside it, and language can only gesture at it metaphorically.

This produces the real difficulty of Buddhism as he framed it. The Buddha has to teach something beyond human experience using human language. But anything delivered through human language and human thought is itself conditioned, and therefore cannot be the ultimate truth. He noted that Buddhist scholars have fought over this for more than a thousand years, trying to clarify what the ultimate truth means in human words.

He returned to the image used in class: samsara is depicted in rich descriptive detail, and nirvana is the empty space around it that people ignore because there is apparently nothing there and no information to get from it.

### The three marks of existence

How the early Buddhist and mainstream traditions categorize this material:

1. All conditioned things are impermanent (anitya; nitya means permanent, and the transcript garbles the pairing here).
2. All conditioned things are unsatisfactory (duhkha).
3. All things, conditioned or unconditioned, are without self (anatman).

He spent time on the grammar, since several students already knew Indic religions. The negating prefix is "a-," and before a vowel it becomes "an-," which is why the term is anatman and not a-atman. The "a" is again negation rather than assertion.

### Atman and no-self

Atman means self or soul. He pressed the class on whether it is equivalent to the soul in a Christian context, and the answer was no. The reflection exercise he assigned for that night: when you lie in bed, ask where the soul dwells, what its home or palace is, and whether you have a soul in dreams.

Anatman means no self, where "self" has a technical sense: an independent entity that does not rely on causes and conditions. Nothing conditioned has that, so no conditioned thing has a self in that sense.

### The indivisible particle argument

A recap and extension of the previous week's discussion of the smallest unit that cannot be divided (the Sanskrit term is unintelligible in the recording). The argument runs by reductio:

- The smallest unit is the basic building block of the world, so units must accumulate and combine to construct gross matter.
- To accumulate, they must come into contact.
- Contact requires a surface. A student objected that particles do not touch directly because of electron fields; the instructor granted the mechanism and answered that the fields are themselves material and the contact still obtains through them.
- Contact is not one-directional. A unit contacts others above, below, front, back, left and right, so it has six sides.
- Having six sides means having parts, which means it is divisible.
- Divide it, and divide the result, endlessly. You never reach something that transcends having sides.

Therefore a truly partless indivisible particle does not exist physically. It exists only in our minds as a concept. So there is no substantial stuff in the world in that sense, just as there is no self in us, which loops the argument back to anatman.

### The Mahayana four Dharma seals

Mahayana traditions often use four seals instead of three marks:

1. All compounded and conditioned things are impermanent (same as the first mark).
2. All conditioned and contaminated things are unsatisfactory (same as the second mark).
3. All things, conditioned or unconditioned, are empty and without self (same as the third mark).
4. Nirvana is peace, ultimate peace beyond concept and beyond suffering.

The only difference is the fourth. The three marks do not include it. So the Mahayana asserts the existence of nirvana, while the early Buddhist traditions do not assert it in that positive way.

## Key people, places and terms

- **Causes and conditions**: the analytic frame for the whole session. Main causes versus subsidiary causes, distinguished by removal (take it away and see whether the result survives).
- **Karma** (Sanskrit; Tibetan *lé*): action and its result. Kapstein glosses it as past meritorious and demeritorious deeds determining one's condition in a given lifetime (The Tibetans, ch. 7, "Buddhist Basics," p. 243). The instructor's emphasis was on its non-linearity and partial unknowability.
- **Samsara** (Tibetan *khorwa*): the perpetual round of rebirth, and in this lecture the whole condition of being unable to maintain peace because one is conditioned.
- **Nirvana** (Tibetan *nyangdé*): the unconditioned; defined by negation. Kapstein pairs it with *sanggyé*, the enlightenment of a Buddha (p. 243).
- **Impermanence** (*anitya*; *nitya* = permanent): always changing, nothing constant, nothing eternal.
- **Duhkha**: unsatisfactoriness, suffering. The second mark.
- **Anatman**: no-self. Self here means an independent entity not relying on causes and conditions.
- **Atman**: self or soul, explicitly not equated with the Christian soul.
- **Samskara**: appears in the slide definition of nirvana as what nirvana does not arise from. The transcript garbles the wording.
- **Three marks of existence**: impermanence, unsatisfactoriness, no-self. Early and mainstream Buddhist traditions.
- **Four Dharma seals**: the three marks plus "nirvana is peace." Mahayana.
- **Mahayana** (Tibetan *thekchen*, the Great Vehicle): in Kapstein it is the bodhisattva path of compassion for all beings, considered the basis of religious life in Tibet (p. 244).
- **The indivisible particle**: the hypothetical partless smallest unit, refuted by the six-sides argument.
- **Buddha's omniscience**: the sole position from which the full set of causes and conditions is visible. Kapstein notes that *künkhyen*, "omniscient," was used as an honorific for great Tibetan scholars (p. 274 region of ch. 7).

Terms not verifiable from the recording: the Sanskrit name for the indivisible particle from last week ("quack" in the transcript), one phrase about controlling conditions, and the exact second Dharma seal wording. Flagged rather than guessed.

## Connections to the readings

**Kapstein, The Tibetans, chapter 7, "Buddhist Basics" (pp. 242 to 244).** This is the direct textual anchor for the whole session. Kapstein writes that in most forms of Buddhism "the impermance of conditioned reality and the resulting inevitability of suffering and death are matters of particular concern," which is exactly the move the lecture made from conditionality to impermanence to unsatisfactoriness. The same pages give the vocabulary set the instructor used in Sanskrit with the Tibetan equivalents: nirvana / *nyangdé*, samsara / *khorwa*, karma / *lé*, emptiness / *shunyata* / *tongnyi*. Kapstein's quoted nineteenth-century sermon on the six realms, in which even birth among the gods carries "the pain of death and the fall to a lower condition," is a traditional version of the instructor's argument that good change is still unsatisfactory because it cannot be maintained. Kapstein also calls insight into emptiness "insight into the radically contingent nature of conditioned things," which is the reading's phrase for what the four in-class analyses were demonstrating.

**van Schaik, Tibet: A History, pp. 107 to 108 (Nagarjuna and the Madhyamaka).** The lecture's method is Madhyamaka method, though the instructor did not name Nagarjuna in this session. van Schaik describes Nagarjuna's philosophy as "a critique of the idea that anything exists independently," and notes that Nagarjuna "claimed that he himself was putting forward no theories, but only analysing other people's to show that they were untenable." That is the instructor's point that defining nirvana is pure negation and asserts nothing. van Schaik's moon-reflection example, where the reflection depends on the moon, the pool and the observer, is structurally the same demonstration as the class, which exists only through instructor, students, time and room. And van Schaik's account of the two levels of truth covers the lecture's hardest claim: "emptiness goes beyond the range of language, since language is based on the existence of things and their having attributes. Yet we need language in order to communicate about the world." That is precisely the instructor's argument that anything the Buddha says in human language is conditioned and so cannot be the ultimate truth.

**van Schaik, pp. 108 to 109 (Sakya scholars and Tsongkhapa).** Useful if you want to write about the lecture's unresolved points. van Schaik shows that Tibetan scholars disagreed about how far the negation goes: for most Sakya scholars conventional truth is "merely labels, all of which when subjected to analysis were found to be empty," while Tsongkhapa thought the laws of logic should hold at every level and that one should refute only the attribute of true existence rather than existence itself. The instructor's remark that Buddhist scholars have fought for thousands of years over how to state ultimate truth in human language points at exactly this debate.

**Kapstein, chapter 7, on monastic scholasticism (pp. 249 to 250).** Explains why a Tibetan history course spends a session on definitions and a reductio about particle surfaces. Kapstein describes Tibetan colleges as emphasizing "a highly rationalized approach to Buddhist doctrine," with Dharmakirti's logic as the methodological tool, close attention to the definition of key concepts, logical consistency, and mandatory mastery of debate. The six-sides argument and the insistence on a precise definition of impermanence are that tradition's style of reasoning reproduced in a seminar room. Kapstein also warns against assuming monastic debate was "a mere ritual, devoid of intellectual content," which is a good corrective if you were tempted to treat these arguments as decoration.

## Writing and reading-skills guidance

The writing instruction in this session came packaged inside the philosophy rather than as a separate segment.

**Turn the analysis on your own thesis.** The explicit instruction: behind your main argument there is a view, so ask whether your main argument is conditioned or unconditioned, and if conditioned, explain how. He named the conditions you should be able to identify: the lectures, the assigned readings, his stated expectations for the assignment, and your own background knowledge, ethnicity, religion, culture and language. A student modeled the answer well by saying their view of Tibet is shaped by their upbringing plus what the course has told them. Practically, this is a self-audit of your sources and assumptions that you can run before you draft.

**Analysis, not summary.** He said directly that if you write an essay you need to know how to analyze something. The syllabus requires the same thing in stronger terms: the midterm and final "must not be a summary of lecture content or class discussions" and must present a new topic or a new analytical perspective. The UNC Evidence handout gives the practical test: highlight your evidence in one color and your analysis of that evidence in another, and you should have at least as much analysis as evidence, or the paper risks being summary rather than argument.

**Definition as an analytical move.** The lecture modeled two different ways of defining a thing and made the difference explicit: direct description, which gives you a picture (an Indian woman, light hair, glasses, earrings), and negation, which gives you only the denial of its opposite. Asking what method a definition uses, and what that method can and cannot deliver, is a transferable close-reading question. Apply it to the sources: notice when van Schaik or Kapstein defines a term positively and when they define it only against something else.

**Insist on a precise definition and then hold it.** He pushed the class until they produced a workable definition of impermanence (always changing, nothing eternal) and then told them to remember it as the essence. This mirrors the scholastic practice Kapstein describes, and it is the habit that makes a key term usable in an argument instead of decorative.

**Argue both sides before you settle.** The tree versus class comparison was run as a genuine two-sided exercise, with students asked to defend yes and then no, and the instructor did not declare a winner. Useful structurally: build the counterargument at full strength before you dismiss it.

**Challenge an unfalsifiable claim by asking about standing, not just content.** The rebuttal to someone explaining your suffering as your karma was not a counter-doctrine but a question about what position would be required to know that, and whether the speaker occupies it. This is a real evaluative move, and it maps onto the source-evaluation work in the UNC Evaluating Print Sources handout: who is making the claim, and what would it take to be entitled to it.
