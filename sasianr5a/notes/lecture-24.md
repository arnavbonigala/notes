# Lecture 24: Tri Songdetsen's Name and the Second Chinese Marriage Alliance

## Summary

The session opened with a new participation policy: the instructor will end each class five minutes early and ask one student to summarize the lecture as if it were a text, identifying the main points, the argument, and the connections between them. Volunteers earn an extra point toward the final grade.

The lecture itself began the unit on the second of the "dharma kings," Tri Songdetsen (742 to c. 797), and spent the whole session on two things. First, the meaning of his name: the instructor argued, following the scholar he called "Brenton Dawson" (Brandon Dotson, cited in van Schaik's notes to Chapters 1 and 2), that the element *tsen* in royal names is not part of a personal name but a title marking the occupant of the throne, possibly a practice influenced by Chinese imperial naming. Second, the reign of Tri Songdetsen's father, Tri Detsuktsen, and the career of Princess Jincheng, the Chinese bride sent to Tibet in 710. The instructor divided Jincheng's legacy into two categories, material and cultural transmission (Chinese classical books, translation practice) and Buddhism (a monastic community, translation of scriptures, the recovery and reinstallation of Wencheng's Buddha statue). He closed by arguing against the later Tibetan claim that Jincheng was Tri Songdetsen's birth mother, and by insisting that Buddhism in this period entered Tibet mainly as Chinese Buddhism rather than Indian Buddhism.

## Lecture notes

### New in-class practice: summarizing the lecture as a text

The instructor will stop five minutes early and call on one volunteer to summarize the session. The student should treat the lecture like a text and state the main points covered, the main argument, and whether the points are interconnected (he noted a lecture is not a paper, so the connections may be loose). Names are recorded on the attendance sheet; a volunteer earns one additional point on the final grade. Priority goes to students who have not yet volunteered. Nothing has to be written down in advance.

### Where the course is in the sequence

The class has already covered the first dharma king (Songtsen Gampo) and some basics of Buddhist dharma. Today begins the second king, Tri Songdetsen, whose dates the instructor gave as 742 to 797, a lifespan of roughly 55 years, which he noted was normal for the medieval period. Kapstein gives 742 to c. 797 and dates his reign 755 to 797; van Schaik says he was enthroned in 756, in the same year An Lushan declared the end of the Tang.

### The element *tsen* in royal names

The instructor hyphenated the name on the board to isolate *tsen*, noting that printed editions sometimes separate the syllables and sometimes run them together. Points made:

- *Tsen* corresponds to a Tibetan syllable whose root sense he glossed as something like "saint" or an awe-inspiring, numinous power.
- The father's name also contains it (Tri Detsuktsen), and *tsen* recurs across generations, which earlier scholars read as a personal-name element with a supernatural meaning.
- Dotson's argument, as presented: *tsen* is not part of the name at all. It functions as a title indicating the throne or seat, so the full name marks "the one who occupies the seat," that is, the ruler. The instructor used the Iron Throne in *A Song of Ice and Fire* as an analogy for a seat standing in for the office.
- Supporting evidence given: the earliest rulers in the line do not carry *tsen*; and one or two figures believed to have ruled but never formally enthroned are not given the element.
- Possible Chinese inspiration: a Chinese emperor had a personal name, an honorific or reign title taken at accession, and a posthumous or temple name. The example given was Tang Taizong, the emperor who dealt with Songtsen Gampo. "Taizong" is a title, not his given name, and "Tang" is the dynastic abbreviation.
- A student asked why earlier scholars assumed *tsen* was a personal name. The instructor's answer: the surviving records are thin, much of what we have comes from the Dunhuang manuscripts, and Chinese records transcribed the names phonetically without recognizing the element as a title.

He then applied the same logic to Songtsen Gampo: the name is probably not a birth name but a posthumous or honorific title, and each syllable is political rather than ordinary. He glossed the parts roughly as powerful, protector, and wise or profound, and contrasted this with ordinary Tibetan names, which tend to invoke flourishing, longevity, and a happy life.

### The painted image of the king

The class looked at a painting of Tri Songdetsen. The instructor asked whether it looked like a historical portrait or an iconographic figure, and pushed students toward the Buddhist reading: the figure holds a book (*pustaka*) on a lotus and a sword. These are the standard attributes of Manjushri, the bodhisattva of wisdom, the book standing for knowledge and the sword for cutting through ignorance to reach wisdom and emptiness. His conclusion: the image tells us how later Tibetan monastic scholars imagined and reframed the king, not what he looked like. Note that this iconographic reading was the instructor's own; van Schaik and Kapstein do not make the Manjushri identification in the assigned pages.

### Tri Detsuktsen and the second marriage alliance

The instructor moved back a generation, as he had done with Songtsen Gampo. He mentioned only one thing about Tri Songdetsen's father, that he was shrewd enough to build a second marriage alliance with Tang China.

To set the context he spent several minutes on Wu Zetian, the only woman to rule China as emperor rather than as empress consort. His points: Chinese politics was structured patrilineally, and women could normally enter political life only as a wife, mother, or stepmother of a ruler, that is, through attachment to a male. Wu Zetian was different: her husband gave her space to develop political power and the two were styled as a parallel pair of sovereigns. After his death she passed over and removed her own sons, eventually taking the throne herself. The instructor asked the class to guess her age at accession, rejected guesses of 13, 16, 30, and 40, and gave roughly 67, with death around 85 and something like fifteen years of personal rule. He stressed her economic and military legacy, including the maximum extent of Chinese territory into Central Asia, and drew an explicit lesson for students: do not let a traditional script define what a woman is supposed to do. None of this Wu Zetian material appears in the assigned readings, so treat the specific figures as lecture background rather than citable course content.

### Princess Jincheng: arrival and situation

Jincheng, not Wencheng, was the princess of the second alliance, and the instructor noted the two names sound similar in English transcription but are written with different Chinese characters. She was married to Tri Detsuktsen and went to Tibet. He placed her three or so generations from Wencheng and said both came from the same family. Kapstein in fact calls Wencheng her aunt, and van Schaik's note adds that Jincheng's relationship to the emperor was more distant than "daughter," though she was treated and addressed as his adopted daughter.

He then sorted her contribution into two categories and took them in turn.

### Contribution one: cultural and material transmission

Jincheng wrote to the Tang emperor (the instructor placed him as a cousin or brother figure, the emperor known as Xuanzong) requesting that classical Chinese books be sent to Tibet, naming the *Spring and Autumn Annals* as an example. He then explained why a request for books matters in the eighth century: there was no printing yet in this period, since printing becomes significant later, so every copy had to be hand-written. Paper was expensive, trained copyists were scarce, and such work was effectively a state-sponsored project. Owning books was therefore a marker of elite status.

He connected this to the Dunhuang material: Dotson and Sam van Schaik found Chinese historical figures and stories turning up in Tibetan documents from the library cave, which indicates active cultural exchange between Tang China and Tibet in this period, plausibly channeled through the two princesses and their courts and spreading among the Tibetan intellectual elite.

### Contribution two: Buddhism, and her death

The instructor asked the class why Jincheng died, and when the room was slow, gave the account: monks from Khotan fleeing an Islamic invasion came to Tibet because it was close; Jincheng personally protected them, building for them and supplying food; the monks also brought a plague, which spread in Lhasa and killed her. He was careful to label this a belief rather than an established fact, noting there is no record of the cause of her death in the Chinese sources, that she lived in Tibet about thirty years, and that the story circulates in later Tibetan historical texts of roughly the twelfth to fourteenth centuries.

### Was Jincheng the mother of Tri Songdetsen?

The instructor laid out the later Tibetan claim that Jincheng was Tri Songdetsen's mother, and explained the background rule: Tibetan rulers married Tibetan noblewomen who bore children but were not raised to the title of queen, while foreign princesses took the higher title but bore no children. He asked for a show of hands and no one supported the claim, then asked for evidence against it. Student answers, which he accepted and restated:

1. Jincheng died in 739 and Tri Songdetsen was born in 742, which leaves a three-year gap.
2. Even granting that the birth year might be fabricated or shifted, she was in her forties by the time she died, which makes a birth unlikely in that period.

He added that even if Tri Songdetsen did carry Chinese ancestry, Jincheng herself was not "purely" Chinese, since the Tang royal family included substantial non-Han ancestry. From there he made a methodological point: modern nationalism makes "China" and "Han Chinese" slide into each other, but in the medieval period there was no pure Han ethnic bloc, including among the ruling houses.

A student suggested Jincheng might have been meant as a spiritual rather than biological mother. The instructor agreed this was plausible and added the key analytic move: ask in what social and political context later writers wanted to assert a tie to China. Such claims carry a political agenda or at least a political wish, and Tibet's position between China and India means its religious history is continually entangled with competition between the two neighbors.

### The translation project, and how translation actually worked

Jincheng's second Buddhist contribution was organizing translation of Buddhist scriptures from Chinese into Tibetan. The instructor stressed that unlike the claims made for Wencheng, where there is no concrete evidence, there is concrete evidence in the Chinese sources for Jincheng's translation activity.

To explain why translation matters, he described the Tang translation enterprise around the monk Xuanzang (the transcript garbles the name): he left China secretly and illegally, traveled to India, studied at Nalanda for several years including Abhidharma and Yogacara ("mind only"), and brought back scriptures and statues. Tang Taizong, though probably not a personal believer, welcomed him and sponsored the translation work as a cultural and soft-power project at considerable expense.

He then walked through the procedure, prompting the class. It was not a solitary translator at home, nor one monk in a monastery, but a committee of the most talented scholars in the empire, selected by examination:

1. Xuanzang read the Sanskrit and explained it sentence by sentence, sometimes word by word.
2. A group of scribes, not one, recorded the rendering. A student correctly identified the reason: multiple recorders guard against drift. The instructor compared it to the telephone game and connected it to the Buddhist formula "Thus have I heard," the disclaimer at the opening of a sutra by which the reciter transmits what was heard without guaranteeing it.
3. Xuanzang read the resulting text himself, and a verifier checked it for accuracy.
4. A stylist with the best command of literary Chinese polished it so it would read well. He compared this step to polishing a composition draft and remarked that many students skip it and submit the raw draft.

His inference: Jincheng probably learned this committee practice in China and brought it with her, and the large royally sponsored translation projects of Tri Songdetsen's reign may have a line of descent from her initiative.

### Chinese Buddhism versus Indian Buddhism in this period

The instructor insisted on a distinction. The traditions reaching Tibet in this period were largely the prominent traditions of Tang China, and he named Tiantai, what sounds in the transcript like Huayan, and Chan (the transcript is unreliable on these names, so verify them before using them in writing). These began from Indian materials but were developed in China, so he would call them Chinese Buddhism rather than Indian Buddhism. Meanwhile in India, Mahayana was contracting and tantric Buddhism, the Vajrayana, was flourishing; he then referred to the collapse of Buddhism in India after invasions, dating it to after the thirteenth century. He added a disclaimer that this is an academic distinction, not a nationalist one.

### The Buddha statue, Ramoche and the Jokhang

The last substantive point concerned the statue Wencheng brought to Tibet, installed in the temple the instructor called by a name the transcript renders variously as "Namoche" and "Lamo chain," which is Ramoche. He glossed the name as containing a word for goddess or princess, referring to Wencheng, plus an element meaning big or elder, marking her as the senior or elder princess in contrast to Jincheng, who came later. The transcription here is badly mangled and the etymology he gave does not match the Tibetan spelling in Kapstein's glossary (*rwa mo che*), so this gloss needs checking with him directly.

The narrative he gave: after Songtsen Gampo and after Wencheng's death, Buddhism suffered persecution from ministers loyal to the old religion, who destroyed statues and scriptures and expelled monks, and who targeted this statue in particular because it came from China. Tibetan Buddhists hid the statue inside a wall cavity, where it stayed for years. When Jincheng arrived she remembered her ancestor's statue, searched for it, found it in the wall, cleaned and redecorated it, and installed it in the Jokhang, where it is today.

### Closing anecdote and advice

The instructor described his first visit to Lhasa and an early-morning walk to Ramoche. He was struck that the door was so small for a holy place, and that it faces west, toward Chang'an. Standing there alone he heard what he took to be a woman's sighing voice and thought of Wencheng. His framing was a method point rather than a factual claim: he asked students to imagine being sent from home to marry a stranger in a remote place with unfamiliar language and food and no prospect of return, and to recognize that these women, used as diplomatic instruments, still left traces in history. He mentioned that a student in the class wants to write the paper on how women took part in political life in the medieval period. His advice on visiting a new place for the first time: capture the feel of the city with your senses and your attention before loading up on information.

### Student summaries at the end

Three students contributed. The instructor pushed the first to restructure the summary into an ordered sequence rather than a narrative, and confirmed the structure he wanted:

1. The etymology of the name, especially the element *tsen*.
2. The potential mother, Jincheng, and therefore the second marriage alliance between Tibet and Tang China.
3. Buddhism: persecution by the old religion, Jincheng's reintroduction of Buddhism from China, the translation work, and the Buddha statue.

A student noted he had said he would start with the father. He clarified that the point of the father's reign is not the father himself but the Chinese connection formed during it, and that this Chinese influence is crucial for the discussions to come. He also corrected one student's phrasing, noting that the Buddhism arriving in this period and under Tri Songdetsen was Chinese Buddhism, more influential at this stage than Indian Buddhism.

## Key people, places and terms

- **Tri Songdetsen** (van Schaik: Trisong Detsen), 742 to c. 797, reigned from 755 or 756. The second of the three dharma kings and the subject of the new unit.
- **Tri Detsuktsen** (van Schaik: Tride Tsugtsen), his father. Given the imperial title in 712 at age eight; deposed by a ministerial coup in 755, the year of the An Lushan rebellion. Also known in legend as Me Aktsom, the "bearded ancestor."
- **Princess Jincheng** (Tibetan *gyim sheng kong jo*), Chinese bride sent in 710, originally intended for Tri Detsuktsen's father Dusong. Died in 739 after roughly thirty years in Tibet.
- **Princess Wencheng**, the earlier Chinese bride, associated with Songtsen Gampo. Kapstein calls her Jincheng's aunt.
- **Tri Malo**, Tri Detsuktsen's mother, who ran the empire as unofficial empress until her death in 712. Named in van Schaik, not in the transcript, but relevant to the power situation Jincheng found on arrival.
- **Songtsen Gampo** (*srong btsan sgam po*), c. 605 to 649/650, the first dharma king.
- **Tang Taizong**, the Tang emperor contemporary with Songtsen Gampo. Used in class as the example of multiple imperial names.
- **Wu Zetian**, the only woman to rule China as emperor. Lecture background only, not in the assigned readings.
- **Xuanzang**, the Tang monk whose translation bureau was the instructor's model for committee translation. Not in the assigned readings.
- **Brandon Dotson** (transcribed as "Brenton Dawson"), the scholar credited with the argument about *tsen* as title. Cited in van Schaik's endnotes, as Dotson 2009.
- **Sam van Schaik** (transcribed as "Sam Panjik"), author of the primary text.
- ***tsen*** (*btsan*), the disputed element in royal names, argued in class to mark the throne or office.
- ***tsenpo***, the title of the Tibetan ruler, described by van Schaik as the embodiment of the divine in this world, a semi-divine figure descended by "sky cord" from heaven via the sacred mountain.
- **Ramoche** (*rwa mo che*) and the **Jokhang** (*jo khang*), the two early Buddhist temples of Lhasa. The statue brought by Wencheng, the **Jowo** or "Lord," was placed in Ramoche and later moved to the Jokhang.
- **Khotan**, Silk Road city-state under Tibetan control from the 670s, source of the refugee monks in the standard account and, in the eleventh century, forcibly converted to Islam.
- **Manjushri**, the bodhisattva of wisdom, identified by his book and sword.
- ***pustaka***, the book or scripture held by the figure in the painting.
- **Tiantai, Huayan, Chan**, the Chinese Buddhist traditions named as the forms of Buddhism reaching Tibet. The transcript garbles the first two; verify.
- **Vajrayana / tantric Buddhism**, what the instructor identified as the developing form in India during this period.
- **Testament of Ba**, the Tibetan historical account that credits Jincheng with introducing the seven-week Buddhist funeral service. Kapstein's detail, not mentioned in class, but directly supports the instructor's claim that Jincheng tried to get the Tibetan court to adopt Buddhist funerals.

## Connections to the readings

- **The second marriage alliance.** van Schaik, Chapter 2, pp. 21 to 23 in the printed pagination, covers Jincheng's arrival, the emperor's farewell letter, her "little Chinese cultural satellite" in Lhasa, and her isolation under Tri Malo. Kapstein, Chapter 3, under "Later Monarchs and the Promotion of Buddhism," gives the marriage of 710, the legend that reverses the story, and the correction that Tri Detsuktsen was six when his teenage bride arrived.
- **The request for Chinese books.** van Schaik gives the episode the instructor was working from: when Jincheng asked for books to be sent to Lhasa, a minister in charge of the public records urged the emperor to refuse, warning that the Tibetans were "intelligent and sharp, and untiring in their love of study" and that "by reading these books they will certainly acquire a knowledge of war." He invoked Confucius on giving away cities rather than literary classics, and argued the request did not really come from the princess. The emperor waved the protest away. This is the single best piece of evidence for the lecture's claim about cultural transmission, and it also shows the Chinese side was aware of the risk.
- **Translation of Chinese classics.** Kapstein notes that Tibetan translations of Chinese historical classics, now known only in fragments, may date to Jincheng's period, along with translations of popular Chinese Buddhist scriptures and works on divination and medicine. This is the textual support for the lecture's "concrete evidence" claim.
- **The refugee monks and the epidemic.** van Schaik describes monks from India and Central Asia expelled from China, Jincheng obtaining the imperial seal for her plan, the refugee camp swelling into monasteries, and the disease with its "nasty rash," which he says was probably smallpox, killing the princess after she found a pustule on her breast. Her dying wish was that her hundreds of Chinese attendants be ordained. Kapstein instead calls it bubonic plague and dates the anti-Buddhist reaction to 739, and says the Khotanese community Jincheng founded was probably the first sangha in central Tibet. Note the disagreement between the two readings on the disease, and van Schaik's endnote caution that although most scholars call the refugees Khotanese, the texts suggest they only briefly stayed in Khotan before moving on to Tibet. The lecture's flat "Khotanese monks fleeing Islamic invasion" compresses this.
- **Was Jincheng the mother.** Kapstein states the position the class argued for: later histories generally consider Tri Songdetsen to have been the son of the princess of Jincheng, "but this is evidently a pious legend inspired, no doubt, by the strong roles both played in establishing Buddhism in Tibet." van Schaik's endnote 4 to Chapter 2 adds that the traditional story was transposed onto Jincheng from Wencheng, and that identifying either her expected or actual spouse is very difficult, pointing to Yamaguchi 1996, Kapstein 2000, and Dotson 2009. Endnote 5 adds Vitali's argument that the princess was assassinated in a political struggle, a third option the lecture did not mention.
- **The statue, Ramoche and the Jokhang.** van Schaik, Chapter 1, says Wencheng brought a statue of the Buddha, the first to arrive in Tibet, placed in Ramoche and later moved to the Jokhang, where it remains. In Chapter 2 he says Jincheng restored the temple her predecessor had established, performed services for that ancestral spirit "as a good Confucian," and saw that the statue received regular offerings "as a good Buddhist." Kapstein adds that anti-Buddhist factions under Tri Detsuktsen even sought to do away with the Jowo image. The lecture's detail of the statue hidden in a wall cavity and rediscovered by Jincheng is not in the assigned pages; treat it as the traditional account unless he gives a source.
- **"Bon ministers."** The lecture spoke of "bone ministers" persecuting Buddhism. van Schaik's section "That Old-Time Religion" cautions directly against this framing: in the earliest documents Bon is a kind of priest or ritual, not an organized religion, and the opposition is better described as aristocratic factions defending existing practice. Worth flagging if you write on the persecution.
- **The Dunhuang library cave.** The lecture used the cave as the source for Chinese stories circulating in Tibetan. The Dunhuang article dates the Tibetan rule of Dunhuang to 786 to 848 and notes its lasting impact on Buddhism there, and that the Tibetan manuscripts should not be dismissed as discards. That gives you the chronological frame for the kind of evidence the instructor invoked.
- **Tri Songdetsen as the figure ahead.** van Schaik's "The Dharma King" section covers the 762 edict making Buddhism the official religion, the oath copied and sent from Zhangzhung to Amdo, and the contrast with the earlier "guarded support for the refugee monks." He also notes that modern scholars read the conversion politically, since the ministers who opposed his accession came from the anti-Buddhist faction. Kapstein's Chapter 3 on Samye gives the monastery's Indian, Chinese and Khotanese storeys, which is a direct test of the lecture's Chinese Buddhism versus Indian Buddhism claim.

## Writing and reading-skills guidance

- **Treat a lecture, or any source, as a text with a structure.** The end-of-class exercise is explicitly a summarizing drill: name the main points, name the argument, and state whether the points connect. When the first volunteer gave a narrative, the instructor asked her to restructure it as "first, then, and also." This is the same demand UNC's **Reading to Write** handout makes: read with questions in mind and record reactions as you go, rather than reproducing the order of what you heard.
- **Summarize to understand, then argue.** The instructor's drill is practice in summary, but the syllabus states both essays must not be summaries of lecture content or class discussions. UNC's **Summary: Using it Wisely** is the handout to reread: summary can set the stage or illustrate evidence in a few sentences, but a paper that moves through the author's argument in the author's own order is summarizing, not analyzing. The diagnostic questions are useful: am I stating what would be obvious to any reader, and have I arranged evidence around my own points?
- **Polish the draft.** The last step in the Xuanzang translation committee was a stylist whose only job was to make the Chinese read well, and the instructor turned this into direct advice: most students submit the raw draft without polishing. UNC's **Revising Drafts** and **Reorganizing Drafts** handouts are the course's resources for that step, and the midterm's three-version structure (draft, diagnostic, polished) is built around it.
- **Ask who wrote a claim, when, and why.** The instructor modeled this twice. On the painting, the question is not what the king looked like but how later Tibetan monastic scholars imagined him. On the maternity claim, the question is in what social context later writers wanted a tie to China, since such claims carry a political agenda or at least a political wish. This is the analytical move that turns a fact into an argument, and it is exactly the kind of thesis the midterm asks for.
- **Use the dating evidence.** The two student answers that worked were both arithmetic on dates: 739 against 742, and the princess's age at death. Specific, checkable evidence beat general impressions. See UNC's **Evidence** handout for how to deploy this in a paragraph.
- **Distinguish what a source asserts from what it merely reports as belief.** The instructor repeatedly marked his own claims: "I'm not saying this is a historical truth, people believe that." Reproduce that distinction in writing, and watch where van Schaik and Kapstein do the same.
