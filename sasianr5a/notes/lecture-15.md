## 1. The exercise, second half

### The Japanese-perspective branch, closed out

Lecture 14 ended on this branch. The instructor's closing check on it: is there any extra or redundant information here? No. Everything is straightforward, and it coincides with the main argument. That is the test he wants applied to a paragraph, not "is this interesting" but "does every sentence coincide with the argument."

### The Korean-perspective branch

This is the second of the two administrations or bodies the passage works through. The transcript mangles the phrase describing its agenda (something like "major thrust toward industrial" plus a word that comes out as "resistance"), so the exact wording needs recovering from the page. The instructor glossed it as referring to the Korean colonial government.

A student's summary, accepted: there were real instances of change and industrialization, but they took roughly 15 to 20 years to arrive.

The instructor's two-part reading of the branch:

1. **Position in the imperial hierarchy.** The imperialist empire had several globalized zones. Korea was one. Manchuria, the northern part of China, was another, organized as Manchukuo. The Japanese placed a high value on Manchuria and put development there. In that ranking Manchuria sits above Korea, so Korean industrialization was a second priority.
2. **Timing.** Korean industrialization was strongly tied to the Sino-Japanese War of 1937. The passage under discussion concerns a much earlier moment. The transcript renders the year as "99," which is unintelligible, so the actual date in the passage needs checking.

Both parts land on the same conclusion: first, this is an early stage, and second, full industrialization was still 20 years off. That is the claim the passage's opening argument made, that the received view of well-developed Korean industrialization is exaggerated.

### The A and B technique, for reading past terms you do not know

Most students had no idea what Manchukuo was or where it came from. The instructor's method for exactly this situation: you do not need to know what the term means. Label the unfamiliar items A and B, then find the logic between A and B. Knowing the background helps and improves your understanding, but not knowing it does not stop you from reading the structure of the argument.

This is worth holding onto for van Schaik and Kapstein, where unfamiliar Tibetan names and place names arrive faster than you can learn them.

### The conclusion, delivered as a citation

The passage closes by citing another scholar, transcribed variously as "Bruce Cummings" and "Bruce Comet," on Korean industrialization in this period. The name is almost certainly Bruce Cumings, a historian of Korea, but the transcript is unreliable and the source is not in the course readings, so confirm the spelling from the footnote in the actual passage. There is a footnote for the citation at that point.

The instructor's observation about the move: the quotation the author chose is oddly wordy, close to redundant, and it works anyway because the author's own framing sentence connects it to the argument. The citation is doing the job of the conclusion.

This matches the UNC handout on conclusions, which lists "include a provocative insight or quotation from the research or reading you did for your paper" as a legitimate closing strategy. The same handout warns against putting evidence in the conclusion that belongs in the body, and against closing with a rephrased thesis and nothing more. The distinction is the framing: a quotation you have positioned is a conclusion, a quotation dropped in is not.

## 2. The model of academic writing this exercise produces

The instructor called the passage a genuinely good example of academic writing and named its parts:

- a main argument
- evidence, organized rather than listed
- establishment and proof of the argument through that evidence
- a conclusion

His emphasis on the evidence step: the evidence is not simply listed. You have to reveal why this is evidence, what the detail proves, and how it relates to the main argument. Whether a given piece of evidence comes from the Japanese side or the Korean side does not matter. What matters is that it serves the author's main argument for that passage.

This is the same claim as the UNC evidence handout, which states flatly that evidence does not speak for itself, that what turns a fact into evidence is the connection it has with a larger claim, and that you should have at least as much analysis as evidence or the paper becomes summary rather than argument. The handout also names "just listing" as the symptom instructors flag.

**The scaling claim.** If you can write one passage with this structure, you already have the shape of a longer paper. The rest of the essay is expansion: more detail on the Japanese perspective, more on the Korean perspective, more on the conclusion. He put the ceiling at roughly 20 pages with nothing added but detail. The UNC paragraphs handout describes the same structure at paragraph scale as a five-step pattern, controlling idea, elaboration, example, explanation of the example, and it notes explicitly that the example-and-explanation steps repeat as needed. The paragraph and the essay are the same object at different sizes, which is what makes the expansion possible.

## 3. The same move in reverse, for reading

When you read a paper, run the process backwards. The instruction, in his words:

- Focus out. Do not sit too close to the paper.
- Do not just read words. Take a step back.
- Ask what the author is trying to talk about, and what the main argument is.
- Ask what the author's work is here: what kind of materials they used, and how their establishment supports the argument.

Compare the UNC "Reading to Write" handout, which pushes in the same direction: identify the genre, consider the author and what sources or methods they used, and read with your eventual writing question in mind.

## 4. Feedback on this week's reading responses

He referred back to praising an assignment last week for including details from the readings, then split the verdict on what came in this week.

**Good:** a lot of you supplied many details from the readings. That demonstrates real engagement with the class and the reading.

**Not good:** too many details that are not directly relevant to the argument, and they were listed rather than organized. You have to organize them and reveal why each one is there.

His formulation, in garbled but recoverable terms: do not **place** the evidence, **employ** it. Placing a detail on the page is not using it.

Practical read for your next response: for every detail you keep, add the sentence that says what it proves. If you cannot write that sentence, cut the detail. That is also the UNC test for "plop quotations."

## 5. New policy: whose paper gets read in class

Last week's disclaimer stands: if you do not email him asking to be excluded, he reserves the right to choose any assignment to discuss in class.

He then reframed it. Because there is not enough time for face-to-face conversations about everyone's writing, having your paper read carefully with the whole class is closer to a privilege than an exposure. The person whose paper is read probably learns the most.

**The new option, from now on:** email him to ask that your paper be read in class. Terms as stated:

- Not the entire paper. Certain passages, gone through sentence by sentence.
- Your name is hidden, along with anything that would let people guess who you are.
- If several people email, he will likely take the first ones.
- One opportunity per person.
- If nobody emails, he keeps picking whichever assignment he wants.

**On what being chosen means:** it is not an endorsement. It does not mean the paper is a perfect model to imitate. It is simply an example. Do not be proud of it, and do not be ashamed of it.

The instructor is addressed as Lawrence in the transcript, which matches the name on the library orientation handout in the readings folder.

## 7. Action items

- Take the structure literally on the next response: one stated main argument, organized evidence, an explicit line for each piece of evidence saying what it proves, and a conclusion that returns to the argument.
- Cut every detail you cannot attach to the argument in one sentence. This was the named problem with this week's submissions.
- Consider the citation-as-conclusion move, one well-chosen quotation from van Schaik or Kapstein that you frame in your own words, rather than a restated thesis.
- Write a short title that names your key points.
- Decide whether to email asking for your paper to be read in class. First come, one chance, name hidden.
- When an unfamiliar Tibetan or Chinese term blocks you, label it A, keep reading for the logic between A and B, and look it up afterward.
- Recover from the actual passage: the two administrations or bodies named in it, the date the transcript renders as "99," and the correct spelling of the scholar cited in its conclusion.
