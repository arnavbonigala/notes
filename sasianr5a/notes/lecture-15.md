# Lecture 15: The Dunhuang Manuscripts, Recto and Verso, and Philology as a Method

## Summary

The session opened with a short documentary on the International Dunhuang Project (IDP), then moved into a live walkthrough of how the Dunhuang materials were dispersed across the world's libraries and how a student can actually find and read one of them online. The instructor pulled up the Bibliothèque nationale de France's digital catalogue, opened a Tibetan manuscript by shelfmark, and used it to teach a set of material questions: is this printed or handwritten, which direction does the script run, which side of the sheet is the front, and what social situation produces a single sheet of paper carrying Chinese Buddhist scripture on one face and Tibetan history on the other. The second half was methodological. The instructor introduced philology as the dominant method in Buddhist studies and classical studies, contrasted it with Saussure's structural linguistics and the social and cultural turn that followed, and asked the class to evaluate both rather than crown a winner. He closed by framing AI and large language models as a live challenge to philology's monopoly on language expertise, assigning a summary of a Sheldon Pollock paper for next week, and telling students to start choosing midterm essay topics.

There is also a political thread running through the lecture: who controls access to these materials, and whose voice gets to define an Asian Buddhist past.

## Lecture notes

### The IDP video and what Dunhuang is

The video described the Dunhuang finds as manuscripts, paintings and artifacts recovered at the beginning of the twentieth century from the Mogao cave site near Dunhuang. The cave had been sealed since the eleventh century, so its contents sat untouched for roughly a thousand years. The video's framing: a time capsule or accidental archive opening a window onto the lives of people who lived in or passed through the Dunhuang area.

The video's argument for digitization ran in three parts. First, the collection is physically scattered across institutions in China, the UK, France, North America and elsewhere, so reuniting it online is the only way to make it whole. Second, conservation protects the material for future generations. Third, Dunhuang studies models the kind of border-crossing scholarship the medieval Silk Road itself already practiced, which the speakers presented as a lesson for modern scholars.

### How the collection got scattered

The instructor's reconstruction, given quickly and with some gaps in the audio:

- A Chinese caretaker discovered the small sealed room off a larger cave complex.
- Western expeditions acquired portions of the material and removed it. The instructor used the word "purchase" with visible hesitation about whether that word is accurate.
- Stein came first. His collection leans toward Chinese scriptures.
- The second collector knew some Tibetan and paid more attention to the minority-language material, which he carried back to France.

The relevant reading names these figures directly: Aurel Stein, who reached the cave in 1907, and Paul Pelliot, who followed in 1908 and took over six thousand manuscripts to the Bibliothèque nationale. The article also notes that the Russian Oldenburg expedition bought many fragmentary manuscripts. The transcript is garbled at the point where the instructor named the French collector, but Pelliot is who the description fits, and it is why Tibetan manuscripts in Paris carry the shelfmark prefix "Pelliot tibétain."

### The holding institutions, one by one

1. **British Library.** Sam van Schaik, author of the course's main text *Tibet: A History*, works there on the Dunhuang material. Jacob Dalton, now a UC Berkeley professor, worked with van Schaik at the British Library, and their job was to read the manuscripts and build catalogues of them. The instructor offered to forward the catalogue, which describes what each item is and gives a brief introduction. Everything in London has been digitized.
2. **Bibliothèque nationale de France, Paris.** You can still request the physical text in the reading room there, which you cannot do in London.
3. **National Library of China, Beijing.** The instructor's sharpest point: Beijing has not digitized or shared its holdings, and access is effectively limited to Chinese citizens. The Chinese speaker who appeared in the video, he noted, is the head of that library.
4. **Institute of Oriental Manuscripts, St. Petersburg.** Holds part of the Dunhuang material and also does not share it broadly.
5. **Berlin-Brandenburg Academy of Sciences and Humanities.** Holds a small portion.
6. **Ryukoku University, Japan.** A Buddhist-founded university, visited by the instructor this past summer. Its holdings trace to the Japanese Ōtani expedition, which followed the earlier Chinese and Western collecting.

### The politics of access and of voice

Two connected critiques, both offered as things to keep in mind rather than settled positions:

The first is about access. Beijing and St. Petersburg withholding their collections looks biased and narrow, but the instructor immediately generalized: this is not only a Chinese or Russian behavior, it happens at Western institutions too. Power operates inside knowledge production and academia, not just around it.

The second is about authority. Reading this literature, the instructor said, it is striking how much of the interpretive voice belongs to Western and specifically white scholars. These are Buddhist materials and Asian cultures. Why should people with no inherited relationship to that history and culture be the ones who define it, with the descendants of that tradition given no autonomy even to contribute? He called the politics complicated and beyond the scope of the course, but insisted the surface is not the whole story.

### Two branches of Dunhuang studies

Working from student answers, the class sorted the field into two halves:

- **Material and visual.** Paintings, murals, statues, the caves themselves. Archaeological and art-historical evidence for what medieval Asian people actually thought about and dealt with.
- **Textual.** Manuscripts, books, writings.

The IDP website provides search tools and software resources for both.

### Finding a manuscript: the live catalogue demonstration

The instructor opened the BnF digital library (Gallica) and searched by shelfmark. To find anything, you need the code. He entered the number and the record came up.

The record page gives you: the shelfmark, the type (manuscript), the number of lines, a physical description, and two scans of the same item, one color and one black-and-white.

**A correction worth noting.** The instructor said "for our Old Tibetan Annals, the code is 1287." In van Schaik's own notes, Pelliot tibétain 1287 is the **Old Tibetan Chronicle**. The **Old Tibetan Annals** survive in a different set of manuscripts: Pelliot tibétain 1288 and IOL Tib J 750 for version one. Both are Dunhuang documents and both are foundational, so the demonstration still works, but do not mix up the shelfmarks in your essay.

### Reading the physical object

Questions the instructor put to the class about the scan on screen:

- **Printed or handwritten?** Handwritten.
- **Which direction does the Tibetan run?** Horizontal.
- **Two sides.** One face carries Chinese, written vertically. The other carries Tibetan, written horizontally.
- **Which side is more carefully executed?** The Chinese. It is neatly and regularly arranged. The Tibetan is more casual.
- **Which side is the front?** Students guessed Chinese on the front, Tibetan on the back, reasoning from how a scroll unrolls. The instructor accepted the conclusion but pushed back on the reasoning, since scrolls can be opened in more than one orientation and a royal edict opens differently again.

The better basis for judgment is social and cultural context. Chinese book culture favors the scroll. Tibetan book culture does not; Tibetans preferred a loose-leaf format of separate sheets. The instructor could not recall the technical term for it in class, and the readings I checked do not name it either, so leave a blank there rather than guessing.

The terms he did give: the front side is the **recto**, the back side the **verso**.

### Why one sheet carries two languages

This was the heart of the session. The class worked toward the answer together.

The Chinese side is a copy of a Buddhist sutra. The Tibetan side is historical writing, not a translation and not a parallel text. The contents are unrelated. A student proposed that the two languages reflect Tibetan takeover of the Dunhuang region, which the instructor accepted as part of the setting.

The full explanation runs through the function of sutra copying:

1. Copying scripture is a practice with a purpose beyond preservation. The purpose is **merit**. The instructor drew a careful distinction here: karma is a neutral term, merit is positive. Copying generates merit for the sponsor.
2. Copying at this scale was sponsored. It could be a state-sponsored project, or an individual, a family, or an official who paid for it and hired scribes.
3. The copies were then checked by supervisors or literate reviewers. A copy with errors, or with calligraphy that was not good enough, was rejected and became useless as a scripture.
4. Paper was expensive and hard to obtain, and paper good enough for scripture copying was the best-quality stock available.
5. So rejected sheets were not thrown away. The blank verso got reused, and Tibetan speakers in Dunhuang wrote their own material on the back.

The sealing-of-the-cave article supports the wider picture: the cave contained damaged manuscripts, waste paper, wooden items and silk fragments, not only pristine scripture.

The instructor closed the section with a caution about disciplinary attention. Because the course centers Tibet, it is tempting to put the Tibetan side at the center of the stage. In practice most scholars work on the Chinese side, and only Tibetologists attend to the Tibetan side. That asymmetry is itself part of the field's situation.

### Philology

Having shown the document, the instructor asked how anyone turns it into historical knowledge. A student answered: translate it. Correct, and translation requires deep language knowledge. That opened the topic.

Reading and composition, he argued, are not just "read the text, then write something." Both reading and research follow a method, and method is the backbone under any piece of writing. When you read, ask what framework the reading sits in and how the author organized it. When you write, you also need a method and a theory. This is unavoidable in the humanities, even though theory and method feel abstract.

**The etymology**, worked out with the class. *Philology* comes from Greek. *Philo* means love, as in *philosophy*, love of wisdom or knowledge. *Logos* covers word, speech and learning. So philology is love of words, love of learning.

**What that emphasis implies.** Philology is grounded in texts and languages themselves, not in social context or history. It studies past languages and their historical development on the evidence of the languages.

**Its history.** The techniques originated with Catholic scholars interpreting the Bible and scripture. In the late eighteenth century, around 1780, a German doctoral student petitioned his university to be enrolled not in the religious faculty and not in the sciences but in philology as its own field. That is when philology became an independent discipline, in Germany.

**What philologists actually do.** Two kinds of version-gathering:

1. **Versions in the same language.** The same text transcribed or published in different regions, different periods, different formats. Collect every version you can.
2. **Versions across languages.** Texts with the same title or an interrelated content, written in different languages. For Buddhist material that means classical Tibetan, classical Chinese, classical Sanskrit, classical Pali, and Middle Indic languages.

Then you compare, term by term, sentence by sentence, asking whether a given passage is identical, slightly different, or flatly opposed across versions. It is meticulous work. It also produces **critical editions**, the scholar's own reconstructed text built from the comparison.

This is the method that produced the detailed history of the Tibetan empire we have been reading. Van Schaik can narrate imperial events in fine detail because that knowledge was built philologically out of the Dunhuang documents.

### The challenge from structural linguistics

Philology held a golden age of roughly a century and a half and became dominant in the humanities. In the twentieth century its position was challenged by Saussure, a Swiss linguist, who proposed structural linguistics.

The objection: you cannot reconstruct history from one text, one passage, or one language taken alone. Saussure still focuses on language, but he treats language as historical rather than static. Language evolves. The instructor's example: pronunciation varies by region even within one language, and so does vocabulary. American English says **elevator**, British English says **lift**. Same language, different states of it.

Later scholars extended this toward systems of language, discourse analysis, and social and cultural interpretation.

**The contrast as the instructor drew it:**

| Philology | Structural linguistics and after |
|---|---|
| Text-centered; everything is about the text | Puts the text back into social, historical, cultural context |
| The person is reachable only through the text | Understand the context first, partly through archaeology and other techniques, then read the text |
| Treats the text as static, not evolving | Treats language as evolving through history |

### The class debate

The instructor asked which method is better, and explicitly asked students to agree, disagree, or partially agree with each other. This is the participation he grades.

Positions voiced in the room:

- One student preferred the contextual approach because it takes in more of the material and gives a wider view.
- Another agreed only partially: contextual interpretation carries a risk, because if you get the cultural context wrong you get the history wrong. Philology adds less interpretation, so there is less to misread, though you can still mistranslate.
- Daniel agreed with the contextual approach as accounting for social and cultural factors and everything surrounding the text.

The instructor pressed the second student's qualification, noting she still wants a text-based approach that draws on social and historical context to understand the text.

### Sheldon Pollock and the AI problem

The instructor introduced a Columbia professor, an expert in Sanskrit literature, who used Sanskrit texts and philological method to reconstruct histories of interaction across Southeast Asia, including Burma, Thailand and Laos, on the premise that these countries belong to an Indian cultural sphere much as Korea and Japan belong to a Chinese one. The transcriber mangles the name repeatedly; from the description and the readings this is **Sheldon Pollock**, who appears in Kapstein's bibliography as the editor of *Literary Cultures in History: Perspectives from South Asia*.

Pollock argues about the future of philology as a method, and the instructor stressed that he is not simply saying philology is the best. He makes several distinct arguments, which is part of why the class is being asked to summarize him.

Then the twist. Ask which method is stronger **in 2026** and you have to reckon with the tools that now exist. Students offered the internet, then AI, then large language models. LLMs are language models. Philology is a language discipline that dominated scholarship for two centuries and whose practitioners are proud of it. The instructor noted that in American universities essentially one institution carries that tradition, and the class named it: **UC Berkeley**. Berkeley also has substantial Dunhuang strength, scholars working on the Dunhuang manuscripts, on the Khara-Khoto material the course will reach later, and on translation of Buddhist commentaries.

The squeeze: a student of classical Sanskrit needs at least six months to absorb the grammar before reading anything. An LLM absorbs that in minutes and can produce a fluent translation. So what is the role of a scholar who spends ten years in language training, out of a finite life?

His answer is not surrender. The question is how to use AI to redevelop or adjust the method, and how to keep a balance between the two approaches rather than declaring a winner.

### The instructor's own critique of both camps

He was candid, and flagged his own sarcasm, about scholarship he has been grading in recent weeks. He criticized a Harvard PhD dissertation on the grounds that its author could not handle primary sources in the original language, and built everything on secondary literature in a language they already knew. That is a real deficiency.

But Berkeley has the opposite problem. It is overly fixated on the materials and can completely ignore social and historical context. When that happens, the translation may be right and the perception of the world behind it is lost.

Hence balance, plus a live question about what new tools let you do.

## Key people, places and terms

- **Dunhuang.** Oasis city on the Silk Road. Tibetan forces besieged it for eleven years before taking it, after which a few Tibetans held the highest posts and Chinese inhabitants had to wear Tibetan clothes, learn the Tibetan alphabet, and staff the Tibetan civil administration (van Schaik).
- **Mogao Caves, Cave 17 (the library cave).** Discovered 1900, sealed in the eleventh century by the older account.
- **Aurel Stein.** First Western collector at the cave, 1907. His holdings are weighted toward Chinese scriptures. Material went to London.
- **Paul Pelliot.** Reached the cave in 1908. Selected the best material, shipped over six thousand manuscripts to the Bibliothèque nationale in Paris. This is why Tibetan manuscripts in Paris are cited as "Pelliot tibétain."
- **Oldenburg / the Russian expedition.** Bought many fragmentary manuscripts; those holdings are in St. Petersburg and remain largely unpublished.
- **Ōtani expedition.** Japanese collecting expedition; material connected to Ryukoku University.
- **International Dunhuang Project (IDP).** The digitization and cataloguing collaboration shown in the video. Van Schaik thanks its director, Susan Whitfield, in the acknowledgements to *Tibet: A History*, without whose support he says he could not have started or finished the book.
- **Sam van Schaik.** Author of the course's main text; works on the Dunhuang collection at the British Library.
- **Jacob Dalton.** UC Berkeley professor, formerly worked with van Schaik on cataloguing the Tibetan Dunhuang manuscripts. They co-published "Lighting the Lamp" in 2003, and van Schaik thanks him in the book.
- **Pelliot tibétain 1287.** The **Old Tibetan Chronicle**. Includes the passage van Schaik cites at lines 45–48 on cutting the tsenpo's hair, and lines 501–54.
- **Old Tibetan Annals.** A different document, surviving in Pelliot tibétain 1288 and IOL Tib J 750 (version 1). Van Schaik's plate 9 reproduces IOL Tib J 750 from the British Library. Brandon Dotson published an annotated translation in 2009.
- **Recto / verso.** Front and back of a sheet.
- **Merit.** The positive result sought by sponsoring or performing acts like scripture copying. Distinct from **karma**, which the instructor stressed is a neutral term.
- **Philology.** From Greek *philo* (love) and *logos* (word, speech, learning). Text- and language-centered method built on collecting versions, comparing them minutely, and producing critical editions.
- **Critical edition.** A scholar's reconstructed text produced by comparing all available versions.
- **Ferdinand de Saussure.** Swiss linguist; structural linguistics; language as an evolving system rather than a static object.
- **Sheldon Pollock.** Columbia Sanskritist. Used philology on Sanskrit texts to reconstruct Southeast Asian history. Subject of next week's assigned paper.
- **Mahayana Buddhism.** The instructor's gloss: its core is compassion and wisdom, with compassion understood as kindness toward persons and a turn away from violence. He distinguished it from the other major stream, which the transcript renders unintelligibly.

## Connections to the readings

**The sealing-of-the-cave article.** The video says the cave was sealed in the eleventh century, and the instructor repeated that. The article is an argument about exactly this. Pelliot's dating, which is the older and more widely accepted one, puts the sealing at 1035, when the Xi Xia invaded. The article's author argues for **1002 or soon after** instead, on the basis of dated manuscripts in the cave and the absence of any document written after 1002. If you want an essay topic that is genuinely open rather than settled, this disagreement is one.

The article also confirms the material picture behind the recto-verso discussion: what got deposited was not only finished scripture but damaged manuscripts, waste paper, wooden items and silk fragments. It further notes that after the Tibetan period the Mogao Caves were an important site for donors, which is the sponsorship economy the instructor described.

On the politics of dispersal, the article documents that both the Stein and Pelliot collections were split on arrival, and that the Russian holdings have not been published, which is the concrete evidence for the access critique the instructor made.

**Van Schaik.** For the Dunhuang setting itself, see the eleven-year siege and the Tibetan administration of the city. For the shelfmarks, see his endnotes, which distinguish the Old Tibetan Chronicle (Pelliot tibétain 1287) from the Old Tibetan Annals (Pelliot tibétain 1288 and IOL Tib J 750) and list them again in his abbreviations of primary sources. His acknowledgements page is worth reading for this lecture specifically: it names the IDP, its director, Dalton, and Tsuguhito Takeuchi, so the book's own dependence on the collection is on display.

**Kapstein.** Refers to the Old Tibetan Annals and to a remarkable narrative history alongside it, cites Pelliot tibétain 1287 as OTC, and lists Ariane Macdonald's 1971 reading of Pelliot tibétain 1286, 1287, 1038, 1047 and 1290, which is a classic piece of exactly the philological work described in class. Kapstein also cites Pollock's *Literary Cultures in History* more than once, including a chapter on Tibet, so the Pollock connection is already inside your assigned reading.

**UNC handouts.** "Evaluating Print Sources" defines a primary source as one produced at the same time as the events it describes, which is precisely what makes a Dunhuang manuscript different in kind from van Schaik's book. "Reading to Write" and "Summary: Using It Wisely" are the relevant handouts for the summary assignment below.

## Writing and reading-skills guidance

**Reading is where the work happens.** The class voted that composition is harder than reading. The instructor disagreed, firmly, and said it is the opposite. If you develop effective reading skills, then by the time you finish a text you already have the main argument, the logical structure, and the kinds of sources the author used in your head. Writing is then a matter of getting what is already in your head onto the page, whether as summary or as critique. Students who find composition hard are usually paying for a weak reading pass.

**How to read a difficult academic paper.** Read it once. If you get lost, read it a second time. Before you start, look at the guiding questions, hold them in your mind while reading, and then answer them in your own words. The summary is written out of those answers.

**What a summary should contain.** Not details. The main argument, the materials and sources the author uses to support it, and the structure of the logic.

**How to write a critique of competing positions.** The instructor's explicit instruction, delivered while the class was arguing about philology versus context:

- Do not say one is simply better than the other and stop at your first reason.
- Set a boundary. Specify a historical moment and a social context, then compare inside that boundary. "Which method is better" is unanswerable; "which method serves better for this material under these conditions" is answerable.
- Keep distance from your object. Do not let emotion drive the conclusion.
- Academic writing tends toward measured conclusions, but a measured conclusion is not "both are good, the end." You owe the reader the specific strengths of each position and then your own judgment about the present.
- Summarize your key point in one sentence. He asked a student to do this on the spot.

**Method is not optional.** Every reading sits inside a framework and every piece of writing needs one. Read for the framework, not only for the content. So far the course has given you content plus information about Dunhuang, and this was the first session on method.
