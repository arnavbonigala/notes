# Lecture 25: The Dharma King and the Chakravartin: Two Buddhist Models of Kingship

## Summary

This session set up the conceptual vocabulary needed to read van Schaik's chapter on Trisong Detsen, the second of Tibet's so-called dharma kings. Rather than narrating Trisong Detsen's reign, the instructor spent the class unpacking the two Indian Buddhist models of ideal rulership that later Tibetan historians applied retrospectively to the tsenpos: the *dharmarāja* (dharma king) and the *cakravartin* (wheel-turner, universal monarch). The class opened with a question the instructor deliberately left unresolved: who actually gave Trisong Detsen the title of dharma king, he himself or later writers? Students answered "the rulers after him," and the instructor told the class to hold that hypothesis and test it against the reading rather than settle it by vote.

The bulk of the class was an analysis of the dharma king model as a political structure with three features (a two-part division of dharma and politics, a hierarchy placing dharma above politics, and the union of both in one enlightened person), followed by an extended argument that this model is an ideal rather than a workable system. The final stretch covered the cakravartin, the etymology of *chakra*, and the figure of Ashoka as the first human cakravartin, including the conversion narrative that turns a violent usurper into a Buddhist world-ruler.

## Lecture notes

### Recap of the previous session

The instructor briefly reviewed Monday's material on the religious situation under Trisong Detsen's father. The audio here is heavily degraded and several clauses are unrecoverable, but the recoverable thread is:

- Tibet's power in this period was climbing toward a peak without yet reaching it, and still could not compete directly with Tang China.
- After the death of Princess Wencheng, anti-Buddhist ministers ("bön ministers" in the transcript) gained ground and pushed Buddhist influence back.
- The second Chinese marriage, that of Princess Jincheng, brought a renewed Buddhist push: inviting monks and sponsoring translation of the Buddhist canon.

The phrase rendered as "build up another American ally to China" is a transcription failure and the intended point is not recoverable. Based on the reading, the subject was Tibet's search for allies against China.

### Framing question: who called Trisong Detsen a dharma king?

The instructor introduced Trisong Detsen as the second landmark king, the first being Songtsen Gampo, and immediately raised the problem of titles. Songtsen Gampo never called himself a dharma king. The instructor argued he probably did not even possess the concept, which means the title in his period was not an ideological weapon or a tool for earning political benefit. It was attributed to him later by monastic and Buddhist scholars.

The question put to the class was whether Trisong Detsen's case is the same. Candidate answers canvassed in class:

- He claimed it himself.
- A minister or family member gave it to him.
- A foreign state conferred it.
- Later people assigned it posthumously.

Student response: the rulers after him. The instructor noted most people think this, then explicitly refused to close the question: hold it as a hypothesis, read the chapter with it in hand, and compare reasons afterward. This is the method the session modeled throughout.

### Origins of the dharma king concept

The term derives from an Indian concept predating Buddhism, in the instructor's description a Brahmanical one. In that original setting it designates a righteous king who upholds the right principles and right law of the world. Buddhism adopted the term, and in the adoption the nuance shifted: the dharma king becomes the protector of Buddhism.

### The Buddha's birth prophecy: two paths

The instructor connected this to the life story of the Buddha, Shakyamuni. At his birth, astrologers summoned by his father gave a prophecy with two branches. If the prince renounces the world, he becomes a Buddha. If he remains in secular life and ascends the throne, he becomes a great ruler, and the class established that the scope is not India but the entire world.

The term for that second path, supplied by a student, is **chakravartin**. The instructor drew out the tension this creates:

- The dharma king is associated with the Buddha and belongs to a transcendent or spiritual sphere.
- The chakravartin appears to belong to a separate, worldly sphere.
- The scopes of the two concepts are different.
- The Buddha's father tried to screen his son from all suffering precisely to block the renunciant path. Becoming a Buddha and becoming a chakravartin look like opposite outcomes.

Holding both of these together in one royal figure is the problem the rest of the lecture works on.

### The Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra and the content of the dharma king model

The instructor located the dharma king concept in the *Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra*. The class worked on translating *nirvāṇa*: suggestions included ultimate transcendence and, settled on, "awakened" or "enlightened." The sutra records the Buddha's last discourse before passing away, and the instructor stressed its reach: it is popular across the whole Buddhist world, Tibet, China and Japan alike, which means its account of kingship counted as common sense across all these regions. This sutra is not in the assigned readings, so this material is lecture-only.

The dharma king as described there has these features:

1. He is not a common person. He is an enlightened being.
2. He presents himself not as a Buddha but as a **Bodhisattva** (the instructor stressed the capital B): already enlightened, though to a lesser degree than a Buddha, and still far above ordinary people.
3. He possesses a degree of omniscience and the power to rule the country.
4. He is a king, and his rule extends to the entire world.

### Dharma and politics: the structure

The instructor then drew the structure on the board. Medieval people had no concept or term matching "religion"; they had **dharma**, and dharma in this sense occupies the place religion occupies for us. The political structure is then a combination of two elements:

- **chos** (dharma, religious affairs)
- **srid** (politics, secular affairs)

In Tibetan this pairing is a standard formula. Kapstein transliterates it as **chösi nyiden** (*chos srid gnyis ldan*), and van Schaik's bibliography cites Cüppers on *chos srid zung 'brel*, the relationship between religion and state. The instructor noted the pronunciation differs from the common written transliteration.

The relationship between the two is not one of equal partnership:

- Politics serves the dharma. Politics propagates the dharma.
- Dharma guides and leads politics.
- There is therefore a hierarchy, with dharma above politics.

And then the third element, which is the hard one: both roles are united in a single person. One individual performs both functions, holding what the instructor called a hand of religion and a hand of politics. This is a unity, not a division of labor between two offices.

The instructor contrasted this with post-Enlightenment Europe and **secularization**, the separation of religion from politics. Most modern states, including the United States and most Asian and North American governments, are secular. The instructor noted that most Islamic countries in the Middle East retain some union of religion and state, placing religion above politics so that religion guides, determines and controls politics.

Summary of the Buddhist ideal prototype, as the instructor restated it: two components, a hierarchy between them with dharma on top, and their union in one enlightened person.

### Why the model is not feasible

The instructor pressed the class on this. The model is an ideal, meaning a high standard and a goal people aim at, not something easy to realize. A student offered the first objection: politics always involves violence and killing, which contradicts Buddhism's commitment to nonviolence. The instructor accepted this and asked the class to push to a more structural level, from the person, from the structure, and from the surrounding social context.

**Problem one, the person.** The model requires an enlightened ruler. How many such people can you actually find? The instructor made this concrete and comic by inviting students to check their own acquaintances, parents and grandparents, and joked about the resemblance between his own name, Lawrence, and "ignorance," noting that ignorance is the root cause of samsara. In roughly two thousand years since the Buddha, how many Buddhas have been recognized? Many people have claimed enlightenment, and problems are eventually found with all of them. The instructor used the Dalai Lama as his example: widely regarded as highly realized, but not a perfect or complete person, and criticized for decisions and policies regarding the PRC and the Chinese Communist Party. There is no candidate who meets the standard.

**Problem two, verification.** Who has the right or the capacity to verify that someone is enlightened? The answer the class reached is: only a Buddha. Ordinary human beings cannot do it. This rules out democratic procedure, and the instructor used an American example to make the point, noting he was not making a political statement: we cannot vote on whether a president is an enlightened being. The recognition mechanism the model needs does not exist.

**Problem three, the social context.** The model requires a Buddhist environment. It cannot work in the United States, which is religiously plural and neither Buddhist nor Buddhist-dominated. China is instructive here: historically pro-Buddhist, with many officials who personally practiced Buddhism, but never a Buddhist state. Traditional Chinese state ideology before the Communist Party was Confucian, and publicly officials would not govern on Buddhist ideological grounds. The model does not survive outside a Buddhist context.

**Problem four, scale and power.** Even where genuinely Buddhist states exist, they are small and weak. The instructor asked for examples and a student named **Bhutan**. The instructor added the former kingdom of **Sikkim**, now annexed by India. These are small kingdoms that really do govern on religious ideological grounds, and they are not strong. When they conduct diplomacy with non-Buddhist states they must negotiate toward mutually acceptable terms, which the model itself does not supply.

The conclusion: the dharma king is an ideal model that works only in a highly spiritual register.

### Aside on the instructor's future course

The instructor mentioned planning a course for next semester on later Tibetan history rather than the imperial period, organized around *chos* and *srid*. The concept becomes more complicated later: the Dalai Lama tradition and the **tulku** system used the religion-politics pairing as a tool to organize their own position and their relations with the Mongols and with China, in order to build political hegemony in Tibet. (The transcript renders the place name as "Japan" at this point, which is a transcription error; the context is Tibet.) Van Schaik explains the tulku system on p. 89, and Kapstein attributes the mature *chösi nyiden* system to the Fifth Dalai Lama.

### The chakravartin: etymology

Asked for an impression of the term, a student offered that it is a title given to a ruler. The instructor built from there, noting that Sanskrit compounds can usually be separated into parts and read for meaning:

- **chakra**: wheel, or cycle (supplied by a student).
- **-vartin / -vat**: a possessive suffix. A chakravartin is one who possesses or is endowed with a chakra.

What the wheel means is the harder question, and the instructor was explicit that he does not have a decisive answer. He reported consulting **Robert Goldman**, recently retired founder of Berkeley's South and Southeast Asian Studies department (the transcript renders the first name as "Rolf"), who offered two explanations:

1. **The wheel as vehicle.** The chakravartin sits in his vehicle and tours the world to inspect his entire territory. The wheel is the wheel he rides on.
2. **The wheel as celestial weapon.** Not an ordinary human weapon, but a powerful, beautiful and precious wheel that turns. When a chakravartin appears in the world, this wheel appears to him and is offered to him. He holds it as ornament, as legitimacy, and while inspecting his realm. In this reading the wheel is a symbol of his position.

The instructor declined to commit to either and moved to the human case. In the original Indian context the term applied to beings who were not ordinary people. Eventually human figures emerged who were characterized as chakravartins.

### Ashoka as the first human chakravartin

The instructor presented Ashoka as the first human chakravartin, and the story deliberately begins with violence, not piety. Kapstein mentions Ashoka only in passing, in connection with the third century BCE Brahmi script, so this narrative is lecture-only.

**Before conversion.** According to the scriptures, Ashoka was not a legitimate successor. His father had many sons and heirs. Ashoka was ambitious and aggressive, initiated wars, killed his brothers including his elder brother, and murdered his father in order to seize the throne. He is also described as extremely ugly. The instructor told the garden episode: lying under a tree, Ashoka overheard his concubines saying he was too ugly to want to sleep with, and in rage and humiliation he had them all killed. He built a torture house, punished people according to his own will rather than by law, and killed one of his ministers. The instructor reached for a Japanese word he had just learned, *kimama*, for acting purely as one pleases. He also recounted, with explicit uncertainty about the details, that one of Ashoka's queens had an affair with one of his sons, and that Ashoka killed both, burning the queen in a hut. The transcript here is tangled and the instructor himself said he did not remember clearly.

The point of all this: a dharma king or chakravartin should be moral, nonviolent and good-looking, and Ashoka began as none of these.

**Good looks as merit.** The instructor digressed to explain that in Buddhism physical beauty is not superficial. It is the karmic result of merit: offering flowers or incense becomes a cause for good appearance in a future life. He gave a comparative example from imperial China, where candidates who passed the civil examination still had to pass an inspection of appearance before being selected as officials by the emperor, which excluded disabled people. He called this biased and characteristic of the medieval world.

**Conversion.** A Buddhist monk, described as the Buddha without marks (*Buddha* without the *lakṣaṇa*, the physical marks of a Buddha) and regarded as the second Buddha, taught Ashoka and converted him. The instructor could not recall the monk's name during class despite having written a paper on him, and offered to look it up, so the name is not available from this session. The monk told Ashoka about his previous life. As a child in that life, Ashoka encountered the Buddha and was drawn to his appearance. Wanting to make an offering but having nothing, having been playing in the dirt with friends, he offered a handful of dirt as if it were food, and vowed that by this merit he would become a chakravartin in a future life. The Buddha confirmed the vow.

After conversion, Ashoka gave up his violent policies and devoted his energy to protecting and propagating Buddhism. He is a chakravartin because of this. In his time Buddhism began to spread across vast areas of Asia.

**The Ashoka Temple anecdote.** Ashoka is said to have collected the Buddha's relics, divided them into small pieces, and distributed them in ten directions across the world. One portion was sent to a temple near the instructor's hometown in eastern China, roughly a two-hour drive away, still called Ashoka Temple today. The instructor's point was the reach of this: relics sent from India to eastern China around two thousand years ago, still preserved.

He then told a personal story. He visited the temple with friends, some Catholic and some Christian, to see the relic. The monks first said the relic had been sent to Beijing. He persisted, and the monk asked him to prove he was a Buddhist. He recited a text from memory (the transcript garbles the title; from context it is a short, very widely memorized sutra, likely the Heart Sutra, since the monk commented that memorizing it in English or ideally Sanskrit would also count). The monk then revealed that the relic in the public treasure hall is a replica, and took the group to a third-floor room containing a well-decorated shrine with a small stupa inside, viewed through glass. Photography was not allowed.

Each visitor looked in turn, and the monk asked them separately and afterward what they had seen. The instructor deliberately did not tell the class what he saw, so that anyone who goes would not be influenced and would see for themselves. The result: everyone saw something completely different, different shapes and different colors. The explanation given is that each person has different merit and karma, and the relic appears according to them. The instructor's framing was that this functions as a psychological and mental phenomenon serving the religious community.

### Closing and preview

The instructor recapped: Ashoka is the figure regarded as the first human chakravartin. The next session will treat the second human chakravartin, who is not a he but a she, a female emperor. The instructor also flagged two questions for next time: the relationship between a female ruler and the chakravartin or dharma king model, and how the chakravartin concept developed. He did not name her in this session, and no reading assigned so far names her, so the identification should wait for the next class.

## Connections to the readings

**Van Schaik, Chapter 2, "The Holy Buddhist Empire, 700–797," section "The Dharma King" (from p. 30).** This is the chapter the opening question was aimed at. Van Schaik gives you exactly the material needed to test the hypothesis that the title came from later writers. Three things in that section matter:

- Trisong's 762 edict declaring Buddhism the official religion was a political act taken as supreme leader, and it was courageous because the old religion supplied the ritual and mythological culture supporting the tsenpos' divine authority. Van Schaik notes that Trisong invoked Songtsen Gampo's temple-building as precedent, but adds that no previous tsenpo had made anything like Trisong's commitment. That is a retrospective use of Songtsen Gampo by Trisong himself, which complicates the simple "later people did it" answer.
- Van Schaik explicitly contrasts two explanatory traditions: Tibet's traditional Buddhist historians read the conversion as a purely personal religious matter, while modern scholars read it politically. He notes the anti-Buddhist faction of ministers who opposed his accession, and that adopting Buddhism would place them beyond the pale. This is the political-benefit reading the instructor said Songtsen Gampo could not have had.
- The international argument: Buddhism was a cultural language spoken by India, Nepal and China alike, while Tibet's own religion was parochial and could not hold a multicultural empire together. This connects directly to the lecture's fourth feasibility problem, the question of what social context the model needs.
- Van Schaik, p. 32: "The establishment of Buddhism in Tibet by Trisong Detsen became a kind of foundation myth for later Tibetan Buddhists, an idealised time of pure religious aspirations." This sentence is the reading's direct answer to the opening question, and it should go in your notes verbatim.

**Van Schaik, p. 204 (quoted from the Thirteenth Dalai Lama).** The phrase "the union of religion and government, going back to the time of the Buddhist tsenpos of the Tibetan empire, the 'dharma kings'" and the warning that "the tradition of government exemplified by the three ancestral dharma kings will degenerate into mere words" show the chos/srid structure still doing political work in the twentieth century, as a claim of legitimacy against Communism. This is the clearest evidence in the readings that the dharma king title is an ideological tool, which is the instructor's framing.

**Van Schaik, p. 89, on the tulku.** Directly relevant to the lecture's verification problem. Van Schaik notes that Chinese patrons called tulkus "living Buddhas" (*huo fo*) but that this misrepresents the Tibetan understanding: the tulku is not usually considered a fully enlightened being. The reading confirms the instructor's point that even Tibet's own institutions stopped short of claiming a fully enlightened ruler.

**Kapstein, *The Tibetans*, chapter 4, on Pakpa and Khubilai Khan.** Pakpa granted Khubilai tantric initiation, "thereby symbolically annointing him as a Chakravartin, a 'wheel-turner,' or universal monarch," and Kapstein calls this an important precedent for later ceremonial relations between Tibetan hierarchs and China's rulers. This is the historical payoff of the chakravartin concept the lecture built: the title is conferred by a religious figure on a secular ruler, which is the chos/srid relationship operating between two people rather than inside one.

**Kapstein on the Fifth Dalai Lama and *chösi nyiden*.** Kapstein describes Tibet's political system as "based in principle upon a reciprocal relationship between the religious and secular branches of government (chösi nyiden), with the Dalai Lama or his regent directing the affairs of state." Note the difference from the lecture's ideal model: Kapstein says *reciprocal*, and describes two branches, not one person holding both hands. This is a productive discrepancy to think about, and the instructor's preview of his next-semester course is aimed at exactly this complication.

**Van Schaik, p. 204 area and the Qianlong material (p. ~150).** Qianlong is described as recognizing that "the Tibetans and Mongols would accept Manchu power much more readily if they perceived him as a defender of the faith, a dharma king," with a scholar quoted on his simultaneous personal devotion and strategic use of Tibetan Buddhism. This is the sharpest case in the readings of the title used instrumentally, and it is a direct answer to the class question of what political profit the title earns.

**Not in the readings.** The *Mahāparinirvāṇa Sūtra*, the Ashoka conversion narrative, the Buddha's birth prophecy, and the etymological analysis of *chakravartin* are all lecture-only. Kapstein mentions Ashoka only once, in a note on the stability of the Brahmi-derived Tibetan script. If you use any of this material in an essay, the syllabus restricts you to assigned course materials, so you would need to cite it as lecture content rather than as a reading.

## Writing and reading-skills guidance

The instructor did not give explicit writing instruction this session, but the class modeled one method directly and it is worth recording as such.

**Hold the hypothesis, read, then compare reasons.** When the class converged on "the rulers after him," the instructor stopped them: do not jump to the conclusion, put it in your hand, take it into the reading, and then compare reasons. This is the structure of an argumentative essay in miniature. Your claim is provisional until the evidence in the text has been examined, and the essay's value lies in the comparison of reasons, not in arriving at the popular answer. The UNC handout on Evidence in `readings-text/UNC Writing - Evidence.txt` is the relevant companion here.

**Distinguish the actor's categories from your own.** The instructor insisted twice on this. Medieval people had no term for "religion," only *dharma*, and Songtsen Gampo probably had no concept of a dharma king. Writing about the past means not importing a category the sources do not have. When you write about Trisong Detsen, ask whether the word you are using is his or yours.

**Separate what a source says from what later tradition made of it.** Van Schaik does this explicitly on p. 32 when he calls Trisong's establishment of Buddhism a foundation myth, and again when he contrasts traditional Buddhist historians with modern scholars. Noticing when an author is reporting a tradition versus assessing it is a close-reading skill the midterm essay will reward.

**Be willing to say you do not know.** The instructor twice refused to resolve a question: the meaning of the wheel in *chakravartin*, where he said he could not give a decisive answer and offered two explanations without committing, and the name of the monk who converted Ashoka. Modeling this in your own writing, marking the limits of what the evidence supports, is stronger than filling the gap.
