# Lecture 33: Why Kings Sponsored Buddhism, and Why Lang Darma Turned Against It

## Summary

The first half of the session was course business: the final paper requirements and deadline, and the rules and sign-up for the in-class final presentations. The second half returned to the end of the Tibetan empire. The instructor laid out why Tibetan rulers patronized Buddhism (royal education under a Buddhist master, personal merit, merit dedicated to ancestors and family, and Buddhism as a protective religion for the state), then argued that Lang Darma was not the lifelong anti-Buddhist villain of later Tibetan tradition. Evidence from a Tibetan monastic history and from manuscript records shows Lang Darma and his wife sponsoring monasteries. The instructor proposed that the later turn against the monasteries was a fiscal and military policy (confiscating monastic land to restore state revenue, and freeing young men for military service), by analogy with the Chinese emperors who persecuted Buddhism for the same reasons. He closed with the Buddhist theory of the three ages of the Dharma and argued that Lang Darma became a symbol of the degenerate age.

### Why rulers supported Buddhism: royal education

The first cause is education. By this point in the dynasty it had become a royal tradition that princes received their teaching from a kalyāṇamitra. The instructor glossed the Sanskrit: *kalyāṇa* is virtue or the good, *mitra* is friend, so literally "virtuous friend" or friend of the good. He suggested the compound is not originally a Buddhist technical term but was adopted into Buddhism with an assigned meaning: the person who teaches you and guides you on the path to enlightenment, an image of the master. Its opposite is the bad friend, who pulls you away from the path of the Dharma and toward evil deeds.

The instructor then asked students to reflect on their own upbringing: the language, values and habits absorbed from family and local culture. His example was the rule in his own hometown that children do not stay out after 9:00 p.m. As an adult he still feels the pull to go home at nine, and discovered his friends from the same place share it. Education received in childhood is embedded in consciousness in that way, which is how Buddhism became the immersive, native education of the royal family.

### Why rulers supported Buddhism: merit as currency

Second cause: patronage and the benefit the patron receives. The instructor framed it as a question. Monastics practice for enlightenment, but lay people are not expected to live like the monastic sangha. They stay married, raise families, work, are not celibate and not necessarily vegetarian. So how does a lay person practice and progress?

The answer he drew from the class is the first of the six perfections, generosity. Lay people offer possessions, money, wealth, property and labor, and what they receive back is merit. He called merit a kind of currency: you can keep it for yourself to purchase a good rebirth, or you can dedicate it to others, including people already dead. Dedication of merit is widely attested in the sources. He cited inscriptions on copied scriptures and images that dedicate the merit to the donor's parents or deceased parents, and a Dunhuang document in which a man whose beloved cow had died donated money to monasteries for copying, dedicating the merit to the cow with the wish that it be reborn as a human being, in a good family, as a boy.

Scaling that logic up from the individual to the ruler: a king who supports Buddhism gains merit he can dedicate to his royal ancestors and to his family.

### Why rulers supported Buddhism: protection of the state

Third, and not reducible to personal piety, is the political function. The instructor described a common ideology across medieval China, Japan and Tibet: Buddhism has the power to protect the state. That is why kings and emperors sponsored monasteries, rituals and above all translation.

On the cost of translation: this was not cheap copying. He compared it to modern funded translation projects, where foundations pay professionally trained translators, often by the page, to render Buddhist canonical texts from classical Tibetan, Sanskrit and Chinese into modern English and publish them online. (He named two foundations and a project with a number in its name; the names were too garbled on the recording to transcribe reliably.) Translators need philosophical and linguistic training and cannot work as volunteers, because they have to live. In the medieval period the costs were material as well: paper, ink, sometimes gold powder mixed into the ink, plus the staff supporting the translation team. Hence manuscripts written on both sides of the sheet to economize on paper. And the product could not be sold. Normally one set was compiled and stored in a state library or palace, inaccessible even to most elites. So a translation project was pure expenditure for the sponsor, not a business.

He also pointed to the Qing court inviting Tibetan lamas to Beijing to perform rituals at the new year and on set days for the benefit of the state, attended by the emperor, the queens and senior ministers. (He dated this to the sixteenth century in passing; the Qing is seventeenth to twentieth century, so treat the figure as a slip.) The Buddhist community claims to offer protective power and auspiciousness for the state, and rulers took that claim seriously. The same state-protecting function appears in China and Japan in nearly identical form. He added an example of state revenue being spent on a single enormous monastery, so costly that the population had to be canvassed for additional donations to finish it, though the place name is unclear on the recording.

The conclusion: kings supported Buddhism both because they personally believed and because they expected auspiciousness and benefit for the state, support for their rule and stability for their reign. He then invited disagreement and pushback, noting that undergraduates rarely challenge the instructor, and modeling the graduate-student move: "what you say is very persuasive, however, I find something new."

### Ralpachen's imbalance and his murder

Ralpachen broke the balance between politics and religion, and between the secular and Buddhist ministers, and the fiscal balance as well, pushing the state toward the edge of collapse and provoking conflict between political factions. He was murdered by one of his own ministers after a feast. Having no son, he was succeeded by his elder brother.

### Lang Darma: the traditional narrative and the problem with it

Traditional Tibetan narratives cast Lang Darma as anti-Buddhist: he suppressed Buddhism, closed or destroyed monasteries and exiled monks. Later historians attributed the collapse of the Tibetan empire to him and especially to his religious policy, making the fall one man's fault. The instructor called this extreme and biased.

Against it he cited a Tibetan monastic history of China and Tibet, written by a monastic scholar and dating roughly to the fourteenth through sixteenth centuries, which he highlighted in italics on the slide. The title as spoken is not recoverable from the recording, and I could not confirm it in the course readings, so get the exact title from him. Its claim: Lang Darma, while still a prince and the elder brother of the king, built Buddhist monasteries together with his wife, and after Ralpachen was murdered and Lang Darma took the throne he ruled according to Buddhist Dharma for at least the first two years. His reign was short, perhaps three to four years. The instructor noted this is exactly what one would expect: he grew up with the same Buddhist education as his brother.

Manuscript evidence points the same way. Monks at one monastery recorded that Lang Darma and his wife sponsored the monastery and gave generously, and that the monastery performed rituals for their benefit. That is close association with monasteries, not hostility.

### Why the radical turn? Student guesses and the instructor's argument

The question he put to the class: why the sudden reversal at the end of the reign?

- Student guess: conversion to another religion, possibly Bon. The instructor allowed this as one possibility, in the form of the ministers rather than the king. Ralpachen and Trisong Detsen were strong because they had strong backing from the Buddhist monks; after the assassination the Bon-aligned ministers dominated. On this reading Lang Darma is a figurehead controlled by his own ministers, representative of that faction, and obliged to satisfy his patrons, as a politician is in a democracy.
- The instructor's preferred explanation comes from Chinese parallels. Four Chinese emperors persecuted Buddhism, and two of them were themselves Buddhists, so personal religious hostility is not the driver. Start by asking what a persecution actually consists of: expelling monks and nuns from the monasteries, and confiscating monastic land. Then ask what the ruler gains. The answer is money, land and manpower. Confiscated land returns to the state and becomes taxable, improving revenue. Monks returned to lay life become young men available as soldiers. For rulers, religion is low on the list of priorities compared with revenue and manpower.

Applied to Tibet: Ralpachen's policy had pushed the state to the edge of collapse, so Lang Darma needed a radical correction. Confiscate land from the monasteries, tax it, and get soldiers. Because the previous policy had gone to one extreme, the correction swung to the other. The instructor added, candidly as speculation, that Lang Darma may also simply have been impatient, which made the policy look brutal.

A further point from the Chinese cases concerns material: the metals used for Buddhist images, gold, silver and copper, can be melted into weapons. Chinese emperors discovered monks melting down images for weapons kept in the monastery. A monastery with young men, an institutional base, land, money and weapons is a political threat. And the richer monasteries grew on lay donations, the more they could buy.

The instructor explicitly offered this reconstruction as speculation, his own plus the students' guesses, and said students may use it in their papers.

### The end of the empire

Lang Darma was himself assassinated, by a Buddhist monk. After his death the empire fell into internal conflict and competition between two sons, a younger and an elder, who fought for roughly thirteen years. That conflict is what brought the empire down.

### The three ages of the Dharma and Lang Darma as symbol

Final question: why did later Buddhist monastic writers need to depict Lang Darma as a bad king, the synonym of the evil ruler set against the Dharma kings?

The answer he gave uses the Buddhist periodization of the ages:

1. True Dharma age: the Buddha is alive in the world, his teaching is available, the sangha is sound. Enlightenment comes easily, and the early scriptures show people awakening on receiving direct teaching.
2. Semblance Dharma age: after the Buddha's death, roughly the next five hundred to a thousand years by the traditional figures. No Buddha, but scriptures, teachings and a functioning sangha remain to guide people. Practitioners can attain partial results.
3. Dharma-ending age, also called the degenerate age: traditionally the last ten thousand years, though he warned against trusting these numbers too literally. No Buddha, very little Dharma accessible to ordinary people, and a sangha that cannot keep its precepts. People of other religious allegiances infiltrate the sangha and try to destroy it from within.

He showed the Chinese and Japanese pronunciations of the term on the slide. The theory was popular across East Asia and shaped what each tradition offered as the only workable practice in such an age: in Japan, Pure Land practice with the recitation of the Buddha's name; in China, Pure Land as well; among Tibetans, the Great Perfection as the one vehicle that can still carry a practitioner through. (Some names in this stretch of the recording are badly mangled and I have not reconstructed them beyond what the readings confirm.)

The payoff: later Buddhist writers associated Lang Darma with the onset of the degenerate age, using the political turmoil of his reign as its symbol. Within Buddhism this functions as exhortation, urging people to cherish and practice the Dharma to counter the degeneration. For political writers, Lang Darma simply becomes the man who brought the degenerate age on.

## Key people, places and terms

- **Ralpachen** (Tri Ralpachen, reigned 815 to 838 by Kapstein's dating; properly Tritsug Detsen, nicknamed "the long-haired" after his braids). Credited with unprecedented Buddhist expenditure and with harsh penalties protecting monks. Portrayed by van Schaik as feeble and dominated by his monk minister.
- **Lang Darma** ("Darma the Ox", Tibetan *glang dar ma*), regnal name **Üdumten** (van Schaik: Darma Wudunten). Ralpachen's brother, placed on the throne after the assassination, killed in 842.
- **Palgyi Yonten** (Kapstein: Trenka Pelgi Yönten). Monk prime minister who recited the Sino-Tibetan treaty and held the real power under Ralpachen. Slandered as the queen's lover, exiled and killed; his vengeful ghost was later blamed for the uprisings.
- **Lhalung Palgyi Dorje**. Ninth abbot of Samye and the monk who assassinated Lang Darma with a bow and arrow, then escaped by reversing his white-and-black cloak and washing the soot off his horse.
- **Ösung** and **Yumten**. The two claimants after Lang Darma's death, each backed by different clans. Ösung remained a Buddhist patron.
- **Kalyāṇamitra** (Tibetan *geshé*, "spiritual benefactor"). The teacher who guides a student toward enlightenment; the class also discussed its opposite, the bad friend.
- **Merit** and **dedication of merit**. Merit gained through donation (Skt. *dāna*, Tib. *jinpa*), temple building, image making and scripture copying, which can be kept for one's own rebirth or transferred to others, living or dead.
- **The six perfections**: giving, morality, patience, energy, meditation and wisdom. The class used the first, generosity, as the lay person's path.
- **Degenerate age** (the third of the three ages of the Dharma). Used by later Tibetan writers as the frame for Lang Darma's reign.
- **Great Perfection** (*dzokchen*, Tibetan *rdzogs chen*). Named in class as the vehicle Tibetans hold still effective in the degenerate age.
- **Bon**. The pre-Buddhist and non-Buddhist Tibetan religious tradition; in Kapstein's telling the assassin disguised himself as a Bönpo sorcerer, and in class the Bon-aligned ministers were proposed as the power behind Lang Darma.
- **Tang persecution of 845**. The Chinese parallel: thousands of monasteries closed, monks returned to lay life, statues melted down.

## Connections to the readings

- **van Schaik, pp. 43 to 47 ("Keepers of the Flame, 797 to 1054")** covers nearly all of the historical narrative in this session: Ralpachen's monastery building, with each monastery granted two hundred households for its support; the edicts ordering mass copying of a short work on the ten Buddhist virtues and of the Perfection of Wisdom sutras; the resentment this provoked and the harsh punishments for abusing monks; Palgyi Yonten's downfall; the assassination of Ralpachen by the Ba and Chogro clans; Lang Darma's accession and the ministers' cuts, including the closure of the translators' college and the temple of Onchangdo left unconsecrated; the assassination by Lhalung Palgyi Dorje; the Yumten and Ösung split; and the fall of Dunhuang in 848 to a Chinese warlord.
- **van Schaik's statement on merit** on p. 44 is the textual anchor for the merit argument in class: donating to monks, building temples, making statues and copying scriptures all generate merit, which through karma brings happiness in this life and a good rebirth in the next.
- **van Schaik's note 6 to that chapter** confirms the point about manuscript evidence: Ralpachen's temple building and his commissioning of multiple sutra copies are well attested in the Dunhuang manuscripts, including official documents on the copying that name his reign. Note 9 is a useful caution for your paper: from the persecution onward, we rely on Tibetan Buddhist histories written in the eleventh century or later.
- **Kapstein, chapter 3, pp. 88 to 91** is the strongest support for the instructor's revisionist reading. Kapstein notes that the treaties of 821 and 823 ended Tibetan expansion and so cut off new revenue while Relpachen increased religious expenditure, contributing to a fiscal crisis. He reports that several scholars argue little more than a reduction of patronage was at stake, that Lang Darma was initially a Buddhist king with the clergy's good wishes, and that the persecution may have been a withdrawal of funding driven by a poor balance sheet. He also gives the Tang parallel in exactly the terms used in class: the objections to Buddhist institutions were that they removed men from military conscription and tax obligations and withdrew wealth from circulation. His conclusion is that insolvency, not religious dispute, was the real basis of the empire's fall.
- **Kapstein also supplies the quotation from Sonam Tsemo (1167)**, the clearest example of the hostile traditional narrative, and the detail that the persecution is said to have targeted monastic institutions only, leaving lay adepts largely untouched. Useful if you want to argue about what "persecution" means.
- **Kapstein, p. 252** confirms the Sanskrit gloss given in class: *geshé*, "spiritual benefactor", is Sanskrit *kalyāṇamitra*.
- **Kapstein on merit-making** gives the vocabulary set for the lay practice discussion: *dāna*, *karma*, *samsara*, the Three Precious Jewels, and the list of merit-generating acts.
- **The Dunhuang library cave article** is the place to look for the documentary side of the merit argument. It discusses colophons, donors and the Mogao caves as a donor site, and notes containers to which devotees could turn to gain merit. The cow document mentioned in class is not in the assigned article, so ask for the manuscript reference if you want to cite it.
- **The Qing material** is in the later chapters of Kapstein (the Qing court, the Kangxi and Qianlong reigns, Tibetan Buddhist leadership in Beijing), not in the imperial-period readings. Check the date before you repeat the century given in class.

## Writing and reading-skills guidance

- The final paper must advance an argument, not summarize lectures or discussions. The syllabus says this explicitly, and the instructor's framing of the paper as two things in mutual relation is an argumentative structure, not a survey.
- The presentation is a compression exercise: argument, approach, structure, selected evidence. Cutting analysis or evidence to fit the time, while keeping the argument intact, is the skill. That is the same judgment the UNC handouts on Evidence and on Summary ask for, and it is good practice for revising a paper down.
- Slide design follows from how attention works. If the audience is reading your slide, they are not listening to you. Few words, one point per slide, legible at a glance.
- Rehearse out loud and time it. Content you cannot fit into five minutes is content you do not yet control.
- The instructor modeled academic disagreement directly: grant the force of the other position, then say what you have found that it does not account for. He invited the class to challenge his own reconstruction and told them his speculation is usable material for their own papers, which is an invitation to argue with him in writing.
- Note the source-criticism move running through the whole lecture. The traditional narrative is one source; a fourteenth to sixteenth century monastic history is another; monastery records and manuscripts are a third. Where they conflict, ask which is closest to the events and what interest each had in the story it tells. This is the method the UNC handout on evaluating sources asks for, applied to medieval material.
