# Lecture 13: Why Build Caves? Merit, Karma, and the Tibetan Occupation of Dunhuang

*(Week 5, the session after Lecture 12. The transcript does not state the date; per the syllabus, Week 5 runs Mon. Sep. 21, Wed. Sep. 23, Fri. Sep. 25.)*

## Summary
This session finished the essay exercise begun in Lecture 12 and then pivoted back to Tibet. The first half answered the second key term of the prompt, the function of the Buddhist caves, by working through Buddhist doctrine: rebirth in six realms, the hierarchy among them, karma as cause and effect, and merit (punya) as the currency people accumulate to secure a better rebirth. Three levels of motive came out of the discussion: individuals sponsoring for their own rebirth, individuals dedicating merit to family members, loved ones and even animals, and the state sponsoring on a large scale to secure peace and prosperity in a period of war and disaster. The instructor then used physical evidence, no stairs, no windows, no soot on the murals, to argue the caves were not living or practice spaces for monks but sealed sites, possibly for ancestor worship or for offerings to invisible beings. The second half connected all of this to the course's actual subject. The An Lushan rebellion of 755 gutted Tang power, Trisong Detsen exploited the opening, and after a siege of roughly eleven years the Tibetans took Dunhuang and held it for about sixty years. That occupation is why the sealed library cave holds so much Tibetan material. The last five minutes started the discovery story: the Daoist Wang Yuanlu, the sealed door, and the Western expeditions that carried the manuscripts away.

## Lecture notes

### Finishing the physical-evidence argument from last time
- The cliffs are red rock, and crucially soft rock rather than hard rock. The transcript renders this as "stem rocks," but the sense is soft, workable sandstone. That is why cutting caves there was practical.
- Low rainfall preserved the murals and the statues. This repeats the climate argument from Lecture 12.
- Dunhuang sat in Inner Asia, a multi-ethnic zone rather than a single dominant ethnic group. Each group brought its own language, culture and values, and all of those elements combine in the caves. This is the same point the instructor returns to at the end when listing the languages in the library cave.

### The essay prompt, second key term: what were the caves for?
The instructor restated the method. The prompt breaks into two terms, Dunhuang and Buddhist caves, and each gets its own analysis. Lecture 12 covered the place. This session covered the function, phrased as: what was the agenda behind building these caves?

His framing question was blunt and worth keeping: nobody spends money and effort on something useless. So what were the builders trying to gain? He offered his own class as the comparison, studying Tibet does not convert into economic benefit, yet people do it, so "gain" has to be defined more broadly than profit.

Answers built up in three layers.

**1. Worship and donation.** Common people, not scholars, come to worship. Worship comes with donations. But donations are not given for nothing.

**2. Merit for one's own rebirth.** The chain of reasoning the instructor built with the class:
- Buddhism, and pan-Indian religion more broadly, does not hold that body and mind simply end at death. Life is a circle, rebirth after rebirth. The transcript's "reverse" is rebirth throughout.
- Rebirth happens in **six realms**, in hierarchical order: gods, demigods (Sanskrit *asura*), human beings, animals, hungry ghosts, hell beings.
- Gods enjoy sensory and material pleasure and do not need to work. Humans are below them but above animals because humans have a degree of autonomy: education, literacy, communication, the ability to manage a life and strive for a better one. The instructor's illustrations were a factory-farmed chicken that never sees sunlight, and a cat or dog that gets a loving family but still lives barely more than ten years. His own cat died at nineteen, a friend's at twenty-one.
- Everyone therefore wants at minimum a human rebirth, and not the hell realms, hungry ghost or animal.
- **Karma** is the mechanism. The instructor stressed that karma is a **neutral** word meaning cause and effect, not a word for punishment. Good causes produce good results. He flagged karma as next week's topic.
- **Punya**, merit, is the accumulated good karma. Spending money and effort on Buddhist activity generates punya, and enough punya secures a good rebirth.

**3. Merit dedicated to others.** People do not act only for themselves. Merit can be transferred to parents, family, ancestors and the dead. The instructor gave a Dunhuang document he had just read: a man, possibly a peasant, loved his cow, and after it died he sponsored a copy of a Buddhist scripture. The colophon at the end of such a copy names the sponsor and the aspiration. His wish was that the cow would not be reborn as an animal again but as a human in a good family, live happily, study Buddhism and eventually attain enlightenment. The instructor's point was as much emotional as analytical: a thousand years ago someone was attached enough to a cow to spend money on its next life. The specific document was not identified in class and I could not find this colophon in readings-text, so treat the details as the instructor's paraphrase.

**4. State sponsorship.** Individuals could fund small caves. The big ones needed the empire, the government, the elite class, in short the state. The instructor then asked why a state would fund Buddhism.
- He rejected the easy answer first. China embraced Buddhism for over a thousand years, supported its doctrines and its texts, but Buddhism **never became the state ideology** of China. The state ideology of a Chinese dynasty is Confucianism. The transcript garbles this as "a competition" and "accomplish-pitch," but the sense is clear and he promised to explain later why Confucianism rather than Buddhism was chosen. So protecting or propagating Buddhism can be a reason, but not the crucial one.
- The real answer, supplied by a student and endorsed: the medieval period was full of war, conflict and natural disaster, and people lacked the tools, techniques and knowledge to protect themselves. A state that accumulates merit on a large scale believes it is buying **peace and prosperity for the realm**. The largest-scale Buddhist projects were merit-making on behalf of the state.

### Were the caves used by anyone?
A student argued the caves were structured for visitors: big central statue, sitting areas, statues along the sides, more like an exhibit than a residence. The instructor treated this as a conclusion and asked for the evidence, then supplied counter-evidence:
- No stairs, ladders or scaffolding in the cliff-face photograph to let people climb to the caves.
- No windows at all, so the interiors are dark.
- No windows means anyone staying inside needs lamps or candles, and lamps leave **soot and smoke traces** on murals and walls. There are none.
- Conclusion: the caves were not dwellings or meditation spaces for monks. Most were **sealed**.

He contrasted this with Ajanta, where monks did live and practice.

So what were they for? Two scholarly speculations he offered:
1. A way of worshipping ancestors or the dead.
2. Offerings to **invisible beings**. Buddhism holds that the world is not only humans and animals; there are beings we cannot see. Sealing a decorated cave makes an offering to them.

The instructor presented both as speculation, not settled. A student's phrasing, "invisible protection from the state," was taken up but not developed.

### Back to Tibet: 755 and the opening
- **755, the An Lushan rebellion.** The instructor asked the class for it directly, expecting chapter one of van Schaik to have been read. An Lushan, one of the Tang emperor's most favored generals, launched a coup. The Tang never fully recovered.
- The consequence that matters here: the emperor pulled his frontier armies back from the border into the interior to fight the rebellion. The western border went weak.
- That weakness was Tibet's opportunity. The instructor was careful to say the tsenpo in question is **not** Songtsen Gampo, this is the mid-eighth century. The ruler is **Trisong Detsen**, enthroned in 756, the subject of the next chapter, the one who adopted Buddhism as the state religion.
- Trisong Detsen sent his armies onto the Silk Road. Dunhuang held out. The instructor said roughly eleven years of siege, which matches van Schaik exactly.
- The city surrendered once the Tibetans promised not to slaughter or deport the inhabitants. The Chinese commander negotiated from the walls and opened the gates.
- **The date.** The instructor gave two candidates, 781 and a later one he considered more likely. The number he preferred did not survive transcription. The readings do not give a single hard date either: van Schaik says the 780s, Kapstein says "during the 780s, and perhaps earlier." The commonly cited date is 786. Confirm this one with the instructor.
- The Tibetans held Dunhuang for about **sixty years**. The Chinese retook it in **848**, which both van Schaik and Kapstein state plainly. Van Schaik calls the Chinese force "The Return to Allegiance" army; Rong calls the same government the "Returning-to-Righteousness Army."
- During those sixty years the Tibetans invested heavily in Buddhist projects at Dunhuang. That investment, plus Dunhuang's multi-ethnic population, is why the library cave contains so many languages.
- **The languages.** The transcript loses the instructor's list almost entirely; only "Hebrew" comes through, and that one is not in the assigned article. What Rong's article does list from Stein's finds: Chinese, Tibetan, Sanskrit, Khotanese, Uighur, Sogdian and Kuchean, plus a Turkish Manichaean confession prayer. Use that list, not the transcript's.

### The discovery, started in the last five minutes
- Setting: the beginning of the twentieth century, the last years of the Qing, the Manchu dynasty. The instructor said the Qing collapsed in 1910 and that the discovery came about ten years before the collapse. The standard date for the Qing's fall is 1911. What Rong dates to 1910 is the Qing Education Ministry's telegram ordering the remaining manuscripts shipped to Beijing, so the instructor may have blended the two. The discovery itself was in **1900**.
- **Wang Yuanlu**, the Daoist. The transcript renders him as "Doris mom" and "Wong Dian-Tu." A former soldier who left the army, came to the Dunhuang area and became a Daoist monk. Nobody was maintaining the caves, so he appointed himself their guardian and set about cleaning them out.
- The slide showed a **memorial hall for a Tang monk**. The instructor first said ninth century, then corrected himself: the hall structure is nineteenth century. Rong's article supplies the substance here. The library cave, Mogao Cave 17, was originally the **meditation site of Hongbian**, the monk-supervisor of the Shazhou government, and after his death his disciples installed his statue in it. That is the memorial function the instructor was pointing at, and it is also the best support for his "ancestor worship" speculation about sealed caves.
- Wang noticed that what looked like solid wall was a sealed **door** in the side wall. He opened it and found a small cave packed with documents: scrolls, plus paintings and Buddhist ritual objects.
- He reported it to the Qing government, which showed no interest and simply made him the caretaker. He went back to Dunhuang and maintained the site on his own.
- News leaked to Western explorers. **Aurel Stein** arrived and negotiated, or paid, for a large share. He was trained in Chinese studies and could read Chinese characters but not the other languages, so he took mostly Chinese documents back to Britain. The transcript says he came in 1901, one year after the discovery. That is wrong. Stein's Dunhuang visit was in 1907, and Rong's footnote cites Pelliot 1908 for the following year. Either the instructor misspoke or the transcription mangled the number.
- **Paul Pelliot** came next, a French scholar who could read Tibetan and recognized the importance of the non-Chinese material, so he took Tibetan documents. He then held an exhibition of the material in Beijing, which is what alerted Chinese scholars to what had been sitting at Dunhuang.
- After that, Russian, Japanese and Chinese expeditions followed, buying, taking or otherwise acquiring material. The instructor's phrasing on the ethics here was blunt, "either purchase or steal or whatever," and class ended there.

## Key people, places and terms
- **Six realms of rebirth**: gods, demigods (asura), humans, animals, hungry ghosts, hell beings. Kapstein, ch. 7, p. 243, quotes a Tibetan text running through all six in the same order, including the animal realm's "pains of being slaughtered, devoured, enslaved, and by that of stupidity," which is close to the instructor's chicken example. (Kapstein ch. 7)
- **Samsara**: the cycle of rebirth, the transcript's "reverse." Kapstein: "we continuously revolve through the three realms of samsara, and in future lives will find only misery." (Kapstein ch. 7, p. 243)
- **Karma**: cause and effect, described in class as a neutral term and a foundation of Buddhist theory. Van Schaik calls it "the infallible system of cause and effect." Topic announced for next week. (van Schaik ch. 4, p. 44)
- **Punya (merit)**: the good karma accumulated through donation, temple-building, statue-making and scripture-copying. (van Schaik ch. 4, pp. 43 to 44)
- **Dedication of merit**: transferring punya to another being, including the dead and animals. The cow colophon is the instructor's example; it is not in readings-text.
- **Colophon**: the sponsor's statement at the end of a copied scripture naming who paid and what they wished for. The instructor did not use the word, but that is what he was describing.
- **Confucianism**: the actual state ideology of Chinese dynasties, as against Buddhism, which was supported but never made the state ideology. He promised to explain the reason later. (from class, not in readings-text)
- **An Lushan**: Tang general, a favorite of the emperor, who launched a coup in winter 755 and declared his own dynasty in 756. Died 757. (van Schaik ch. 2, pp. 26 to 27; Kapstein ch. 3)
- **Trisong Detsen (r. 755 or 756 to 797)**: the tsenpo who took advantage of the Tang collapse, briefly occupied Chang'an, conquered Dunhuang in the 780s, and adopted Buddhism as the state religion. The transcript's "Zanbos" is *tsenpo*, the Tibetan title. (van Schaik ch. 2, pp. 26 to 29; Kapstein ch. 3)
- **The siege of Dunhuang**: eleven years. The governor wanted to burn the city and flee, the Chinese commander murdered him and took his place, sold the city's silk to the Tibetans for food after eight years, and two years later surrendered on condition that he and his people could stay. (van Schaik ch. 2, pp. 27 to 28)
- **848**: Dunhuang falls to a Chinese warlord, ending roughly sixty years of Tibetan rule. (van Schaik ch. 4, p. 46; Kapstein ch. 3)
- **Wang Yuanlu, the Daoist Wang**: self-appointed guardian of the caves who broke through the wall and found the library cave in 1900, reported it up the chain to the Gansu governor, was told to seal it and left as caretaker, and sold off calligraphy to fund cave repairs. (Rong, pp. 250 to 253)
- **Hongbian**: monk-supervisor in the Shazhou government whose meditation cave became Cave 17; his statue was placed there after his death, and paintings were later found in a hole beneath it. (Rong, pp. 258 and 265)
- **Aurel Stein**: took the material to London, where it was split among the British Museum, the India Office Library and the Central Asian collection. His "sacred waste" reading of the cave is the view Rong argues against. (Rong, pp. 250 to 253)
- **Paul Pelliot**: French scholar, read the non-Chinese material, took Tibetan documents, and dated the sealing to 1035 against the Xi Xia invasion. Rong argues for soon after 1002 instead. (Rong, pp. 249 and 253)
- **Mogao Cave 17 / the library cave**: sealed chamber in the north wall of Cave 16. (Rong, p. 247)

## Connections to the readings
- **van Schaik, ch. 4, pp. 43 to 44 (Keepers of the Flame, 797 to 1054).** This is the passage to cite for the entire merit argument, and it is about Tibetan practice specifically: under Ralpachen "more of Tibet's wealth was spent on Buddhist projects than ever before," hundreds of copies of the Perfection of Wisdom sutras were made, and "in most Buddhist cultures there is great merit in certain activities, such as making donations to monks, helping to build temples, making statues and copying the scriptures... through the infallible system of cause and effect, or karma, they will help one towards happiness in this life and a good rebirth in the next." That sentence is almost exactly the lecture's argument, applied to Tibet rather than to Dunhuang.
- **van Schaik, ch. 4, p. 44.** Ralpachen granted each new monastery two hundred households to feed it, and the cost fell on the local population. Useful if you write about state sponsorship: it shows the scale and the cost, and it shows the resentment that followed.
- **Kapstein, ch. 7, p. 243.** The six destinies passage, quoted from a Tibetan text. Better evidence for the hierarchy than anything in van Schaik, and it gives you the bardo and the motivation for seeking liberation as well.
- **van Schaik, ch. 2, pp. 26 to 28.** An Lushan's coup in winter 755, Trisong Detsen enthroned in 756, the Tibetan occupation of Chang'an, and the eleven-year siege of Dunhuang with the surrender terms. This is the chapter the instructor expected the class to have read.
- **van Schaik, ch. 2, pp. 28 to 29.** What changed for the inhabitants after the conquest: Tibetan became the language of government, law and business, Chinese residents wore Tibetan clothes and worked in the Tibetan civil service, dating switched to the twelve-year animal cycle, and "Tibetans in the lowest government positions still outranked Chinese in the highest." The instructor did not cover this, but it is the obvious next step and likely to come up.
- **Kapstein, ch. 3, p. 5159 of the extracted text.** "During the 780s, and perhaps earlier, Tri Songdetsen's armies consolidated their conquest of Dunhuang, the major Silk Road entrepôt and center of Chinese Buddhism." Note the hedge. Kapstein will not commit to a year either.
- **Kapstein, ch. 3.** Local forces cast off Tibetan rule in 848 and the northeast became effectively independent. Pair with van Schaik on the same year.
- **Kapstein, ch. 2.** The Dunhuang Tibetan manuscripts date to the late eighth through tenth centuries, now mostly in Paris and London, and cover law, religion, literature and more. This is the direct consequence of the sixty-year occupation the instructor described, and it explains why Pelliot's and Stein's collections are split between two cities.
- **Rong Xinjiang, pp. 250 to 253.** The discovery narrative: Xie Zhiliu's 1942 to 1943 pamphlet on Wang and Mr. Yang breaking through the wall at night, the indifferent Gansu governor, Wang selling calligraphy to fund repairs, Stein's negotiation, and the language list from the "mixed bundles."
- **Rong, p. 258.** Cave 17 as Hongbian's meditation site with his statue installed after death, near the Three Realms Monastery, later used by Daozhen as a storehouse. This is the strongest reading-based support for the instructor's speculation about caves as memorial or ancestor sites.
- **Rong, p. 261.** Wang placed sutra rolls in a Buddhist hall as "sutra-turning containers which devotees could turn to gain merit." A small detail, but it shows merit-making still operating at Dunhuang in 1910, which is a nice closing bracket on the lecture's main theme.

## Writing and reading-skills guidance
- **Finish the structure you built.** The instructor did not abandon the two-term breakdown from last session; he came back and completed the second term. That is the model for an essay: one analytical section per key term, both actually written.
- **Start from motive, not description.** "Nobody spends money and effort on something useless" is a usable analytical opening move for any question about why something was built, copied or preserved. Ask what the actor expected to gain.
- **Layer your causes by scale of actor.** Individual for self, individual for others, state. The same phenomenon gets a different explanation at each level, and all three can be true at once. This is a cleaner structure than a flat list of reasons.
- **Evidence beats intuition, and absence is evidence.** The student's "it looks like an exhibit" was a reasonable conclusion, and the instructor's reply was to ask for evidence and then produce counter-evidence: no stairs, no windows, no soot. Missing traces are evidence. Notice what should be there and is not.
- **Distinguish the conclusion from the evidence.** The instructor's phrasing, "that's your conclusion, what's your evidence," is worth carrying into your own drafts. Compare the UNC Evidence handout on this point.
- **Mark speculation as speculation.** Both readings of the sealed caves, ancestor worship and offerings to invisible beings, were introduced as what scholars speculate. Neither was asserted.
- **Use a small specific case to carry a large claim.** The cow colophon does more work than a general statement about merit dedication would. Find your equivalent detail.
