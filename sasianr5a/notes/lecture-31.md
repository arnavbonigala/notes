# Lecture 31: The Samye Debate, Part Three — Three Perspectives on a Story That May Never Have Happened

## Summary

The class opened with logistics for the final-essay presentations, then finished the three-session unit on the Samye debate. The instructor first drew out the tensions the debate story encodes (doctrinal, political, linguistic, and practice versus scholasticism), insisting that students bracket modern India-China-Tibet politics long enough to see the Buddhist issues. He then compared three sets of readers of the same event: traditional Tibetan monastic historians, the Chinese historian Shen Weirong of Tsinghua University trained in German philology, and a Western scholar whose 1987 lecture book treats the debate as a question about sudden versus gradual awakening rather than about India versus China. The running argument was methodological: there is no single recoverable historical fact here, only accounts shaped by who recorded them, with what training, and for what purpose. He closed with photographs and a video of Samye monastery and its library, and with instructions on how to narrow all of this into a 1,500-word paper.

## Lecture notes

### Presentation logistics

Each student gets seven minutes total. Roughly five minutes is the presentation of the final paper's topic, argument and content; roughly two minutes is question and answer, with both classmates and the instructor asking questions to test whether the student actually understands the material. Grading rests on two things: how well you reorganize your materials (PowerPoint slides, a clear argument, accurate and precise answers to questions), and how well you manage yourself in front of an audience (confidence, not being visibly nervous, knowing how to face a public). He treats this as an exercise in public speaking as much as in content. He apologized that there is no time this semester for a practice presentation session, which he has run in previous semesters.

### Why the Samye debate is worth the trouble

He framed it as the test of whether you actually know Tibetan history. Anyone can recite names and dates. Being able to explain what the Samye debate was, what happened there, which parties were involved, and what the story reveals is the mark of someone who has understood the field.

### Recap of participants

From the previous class: the tsenpo, the Chinese monk, the Indian abbot Shantarakshita, and Shantarakshita's student Kamalashila. Van Schaik gives the setup in detail (pp. 36-39 of the chapter "The Holy Buddhist Empire, 700-797"): Shantarakshita had already died but had told the tsenpo to call on Kamalashila if any dispute arose about the practice of Buddhism; Moheyan argued the Chinese side, Kamalashila the Indian; the tsenpo sat enthroned between them with disciples lined up in two rows behind.

### Bracketing identity, and the two dimensions

He asked students, including the Indian students in the room, to set aside personal identity and modern political categories, which he said distract people from seeing what is actually a Buddhist problem. He then restated the two-dimension framework from earlier sessions:

- First dimension: what actually happened. Here the conclusion is that there is almost no possibility of recovering it. Whether a real debate with these three parties ever occurred, we do not know.
- Second dimension: how later historical actors read, retold and used the event. This is where analysis is possible.

### Tensions in the narrative

Working from student answers, he listed the tensions the story carries:

1. **Doctrinal.** Not "Chinese Buddhism versus Indian Buddhism." Chinese Buddhism contains many schools; Chan is one branch, popular but not the dominant one, and Tiantai (the transcript renders it as "Kintai"/"KENTAG") was the genuinely dominant and scholastically advanced tradition in doctrinal analysis. On the Indian side, Kamalashila and Shantarakshita are fixed on Madhyamaka, while Padmasambhava represents the tantric current. Tantra, he stressed, became the most widespread and fashionable form of Indian religious practice from roughly the seventh century and especially the eighth, and not only in Buddhism: it ran through Jainism and Brahmanism as well. So nobody in the room at Samye could claim to represent "Chinese Buddhism" or "Indian Buddhism" as a whole. It was specifically Chinese Chan against a few Indian scholastics.
2. **Political.** A student pointed out that monastics were being judged by a lay person. The instructor took this up: the post-Enlightenment habit of separating secular from religious spheres does not apply to premodern history, where religion is political in nature. At Samye there are two political powers in play, one represented by the lay ruler and one by the ministers. He flagged this explicitly as the thread to pick up next Monday.
3. **Linguistic.** The two sides did not share a language. He called this a minor tension.
4. **Practice versus scholastic study.** This he treated as the major one, and it structures the rest of the lecture.
5. **India versus China.** Unavoidable, and political again.

### Three readers of the same event

He announced three "agents" or perspectives: traditional Tibetan monastic scholars; a modern Chinese scholar; and a modern Western scholar. For each, the questions are: who are they, what is their academic identity and training, what methodology do they use, what do they conclude, and what is their focus. The point, repeated from Monday or Friday of the prior week, is that different people receive the event through different layers, from different perspectives, using different methods, and so reach different conclusions. Each conclusion is probably a piece of the real thing, and none represents the whole.

### Perspective one: traditional Tibetan monastic historiography

From the tenth century onward, after the collapse of the empire, political power in Tibet was progressively gathered up by monastic figures rather than lay rulers, and religious and political authority eventually fused in a single place. (He said repeatedly that the mechanics of this, including the reincarnation and tulku systems, belong to next semester's course.) The consequence for sources: the history was recorded and composed by monastics, all of them men.

Their account is simple. The debate happened at Samye. They do not analyze whether a victory really occurred; they assume it did. They read it as a contest between Indian scholastics and the Chinese, India wins, and the outcome proves that Chan Buddhism had doctrinal flaws. The instructor finds this incoherent on its own terms: Chan has no doctrine to be flawed. Its emblem is holding up a flower and smiling. Its claim is that whatever can be expressed is not the truth, that truth cannot be reached through study, and that it must be experienced or awakened into. Comparing the two on doctrinal grounds therefore makes no sense. But the later Tibetan tradition did not care, because the function of the story was to establish the Indian tradition as the legitimate and orthodox form of Buddhism in Tibet.

He then sketched why India mattered so much. Indian masters kept arriving in Tibet up to the thirteenth century, and as Buddhism declined and collapsed in India, scholars and yogis looked for somewhere to survive and spread the teaching. Tibet was a good place, and to win Tibetan hearts they had to offer something new, so new texts, practices and rituals were produced along the way. Some of these we can be fairly sure are later products, because they do not exist in India. The system was never closed; people kept adding to it. But Tibetans did not treat these as new, they treated them as authentic because they came from India.

After the thirteenth century, when the last Indian masters stopped coming, there were no new Indian sources to import. So Tibetans turned to their own holy mountains and lakes and produced **terma**, treasure texts said to have been concealed by Padmasambhava in rocks, caves, lakes, and sometimes in a yogi's mind, then discovered later. He said he would not discuss terma in detail here. Kapstein's chapter four covers exactly this: treasures "said to have been cached by famous teachers in earlier times and now recovered," concealment generally attributed to Padmasambhava among the Nyingmapa, with Nyangrel Nyima Özer (1124-1196) and successors building a large body of historical and legendary literature out of it.

Why did the monastic historians need to distinguish Chinese from Indian tradition at all, when Samye itself and the tsenpo did not? He emphasized that the tsenpo, whether he captured, invited or summoned the Chan monk Moheyan, showed no sectarian bias: as long as it was Buddhism, it was welcome, because he wanted his kingdom to become a center of Buddhism. Samye's own structure represents the convergence of Chinese and Indian Buddhism. The need to separate the two came later, and it was political.

Two political explanations followed:

- **Religious prestige.** If Indian Buddhism is the authentic source, then being close to India makes you authentic. A student supplied this answer and added the sharper version: Tibet was in fact closer to China, and advertising closeness to India served to obscure that actual relationship. The instructor agreed. He added that the traffic reversed: Chinese monks had gone to India to learn Buddhism, and after Indian Buddhism collapsed in the thirteenth century the Chinese sent monks to Tibet instead, using Tibet as a replacement for India.
- **The relationship with Beijing.** From the thirteenth century and the Yuan, Tibet was incorporated into the empire. Monastic-political authority (first the Sakya hierarchs, later the Dalai Lamas) held real power together with some lay secular power, but always had to answer to the central government in Beijing. He insisted on saying "the government in Beijing" rather than "China," since that government was successively Mongol, Ming Chinese and Manchu Qing. This produced a lasting bind: Tibetan rulers could not claim independence, because they needed Beijing as patron for military, financial and political endorsement, yet they wanted to maintain internal autonomy. Claiming a close religious connection to India was a balancing move. As he put it, in fact we are part of this empire, but our Buddhism comes from India, not China.

On the comparative point about India and China: he asked whether there was any unified, centralized regime in India in this period and answered no, India was a scatter of separate regimes, while China was almost always centralized. His rough figures: the Mongol Yuan about 98 years, then the Ming, then the Qing as the last dynasty, amounting to over 700 years of centralized unified rule, broken only by short periods of chaos. (The individual dynasty lengths he gave, 400 years for the Ming and 300 for the Qing, are loose; the readings do not supply these numbers, so check them before using them.) Hence there was no single Indian counterpart for Tibet to deal with at state level, and contact with the subcontinent was mostly at the level of individuals, monks and commerce. He also mentioned sixteenth-century Chinese missions toward Sri Lanka as a civil and commercial rather than state-level contact. Part of this stretch of the transcript is garbled and his exact claims about those missions are not fully recoverable.

### Perspective two: Shen Weirong, philology and the Dunhuang manuscripts

Shen Weirong is a professor at Tsinghua University, a historian who received his training in Germany. (The instructor could not recall which university.) The class guessed his methodology correctly: philology. He explained why that follows from the training: classical philological method was developed in the German universities of the eighteenth century and became their scholastic strength.

Shen's question is the first-dimension question: did this event really happen? His reasoning is that if the debate is attested only in the Tibetan accounts, and if it really happened, it should leave traces elsewhere, above all in the writings of the two men at the center of it, who would surely mention each other. So he went to the Dunhuang library cave manuscripts to check.

What he found, as reported in class: there is a text whose title concerns sudden awakening and the correct principle of the Mahayana, and there is some material about Moheyan's writing. (The title as spoken is badly mangled in the transcript and I am not going to reconstruct it; get the exact reference from the instructor.) But there is nothing showing that these two figures knew each other. No mutual reference, no sign they lived in the same place, no mention of the debate. The instructor's comment: a confrontation like that would be the biggest moment of your life. You would mention it. Even if you wanted to hide it, it would surface, because it would be lodged in your subconscious.

What Kamalashila's text does contain is a description of a question-and-answer exchange with a Chan monk whose name is not given, and whose nationality is not specified either. He may have been Chinese, he may have been Tibetan, he may have been something else. Chan by this time was popular not only in China but in Japan, Korea and Tibetan-controlled areas; Moheyan taught Chinese Buddhism in the Dunhuang region and many Tibetans studied Chan. There is concrete evidence for this: some of Moheyan's Tibetan disciples, after he left or died, were sent to Tibet, built monasteries, and spread the Chinese tradition, which survives there today. Chinese Buddhism never disappeared from Tibet.

Shen's conclusion, as presented: the debate as a formal event probably never happened. What existed was a communication or discussion involving Kamalashila and an unnamed Chan monk. Tibetans then created an image of it and kept adding details, generation by generation, until the story grew like a tree, became a collective memory, and finally became true as collective memory and as history. He showed a page of Kamalashila's text at this point.

### Perspective three: a Western reading, sudden versus gradual

The third scholar he named as "David," an American-associated scholar who taught and lived in Britain and has since died. The book he held up is the published version of a 1987 lecture series, on Buddha-nature, mind, and the problem of gradualism in comparative perspective, concerning the transmission and reception of Buddhism in India and Tibet. (The transcript mangles both the name and the title; this is almost certainly David Seyfort Ruegg's Jordan Lectures, but confirm the exact attribution with the instructor before citing it.) He said explicitly that he is not encouraging students to read it, because it is too technical at this stage. The passage he read out frames the Buddhist notion of an immanent absolute as a problem in comparative religion and philosophy, and treats the great debate between the gradualists and the simultaneists in eighth-century Tibet as a religious and philosophical issue.

The core claim: the debate was not primarily about India versus China. It was about the sudden path versus the gradual path.

Two warnings about the terms:

- Tibetan "sudden versus gradual" is not the same as the Chinese Chan sudden/gradual split. In the Chinese tradition that split runs through the sixth and seventh patriarchs, and as the instructor placed him, Moheyan belongs to the Northern, gradual line descending from Shenxiu rather than to the sudden Southern line of the sixth patriarch. (The patriarch names in the transcript are too garbled to reproduce with confidence; the structure of the point is clear, the names are not.) In Tibet, all of Chinese Buddhism got lumped together as the sudden path regardless.
- The practical way to hold the distinction: **gradual means scholastic**, because you first hear the Dharma, then contemplate it, then practice or meditate, a fixed sequence. **Sudden means going straight to practice**, skipping the hearing and contemplating.

Which maps onto two kinds of religious person already introduced in the course: scholars on one side, yogis on the other, and in Tibetan institutions the two groups of ngagpas (tantric practitioners) and monks. He showed photographs: first, the greatest yogi of the twentieth century, named in class as Dilgo Khyentse Rinpoche, a Nyingma figure in Padmasambhava's lineage and a head of a treasure-revealer tradition, not a monk but a lay practitioner, shown practicing and performing rituals. Second, the scholastic side, represented in later Tibet by Sakya and by the Dalai Lamas' tradition, with Sakya the earlier of the two.

The further claim, and the one that ties the lecture together: the Great Perfection (Dzogchen) of the Nyingma is strikingly close to Chinese Chan in both practice and method. Whether Dzogchen learned it from Chan, or the two shaped each other, or they developed in parallel, we cannot say. Van Schaik's description of the Great Perfection matches what the instructor said about it: instructions "teaching a transcendence of any sort of meditation," pointing "to the mind of this present moment as ever-present enlightenment," short and poetic, as in the Cuckoo of Awareness verse, "Since everything is done, give up the malady of seeking / And stay where you are without effort." He noted that the term "great perfection" is hard to grasp because it is held to be beyond knowledge, and that he did not expect students to understand it within five years.

So the debate between Moheyan and the Indian side, read this way, encodes a later Tibetan contest between the Nyingma (the old school, sudden, yogic) and the new schools (Sakya and others, gradual, scholastic), over who holds Buddhist orthodoxy. And because premodern religion is political in nature, whoever holds orthodoxy and legitimacy can convert that into political power and control. He made the point crudely and deliberately, asking why Donald Trump wants the presidency and the White House: power manifests as money, materials, influence, and control over other people's lives. Religion works the same way, by spreading your own doctrinal structure and excluding the schools of others.

This gave the debate a long afterlife as a weapon. Calling a rival "Hashang" or "Chinese Chan" became a way of striking at enemies, and he said the Dalai Lama who became the actual ruler of Tibet used it that way. (He placed this in the nineteenth century, which does not fit well with what the readings say about the Dalai Lamas' rise to power; treat the dating as uncertain.) He connected it to the moment after the Manchu Qing weakened and collapsed in 1912, when the thirteenth Dalai Lama moved to separate from Beijing, and the debate was invoked again. Van Schaik confirms the 1912 expulsion of Chinese soldiers from Lhasa.

### Samye monastery and its library

He showed the front gate of Samye monastery and then its library, which holds the largest collection of Buddhist texts gathered there. He pointed out that the books are not modern bound volumes: they are loose-leaf stacks wrapped in cloth for storage. A friend who visited last month shot a video for him, which he played. What is visible is a small corner of a library that fills an entire building, and the effect he described is of immersing yourself in an ocean of Buddhist material.

He also reported that the local Chinese government is now sponsoring and organizing a cataloguing effort there. If it succeeds and the results are shared with the academic world, a great deal of material that has never been read or published will become available. On access: visitors may be shown the texts but cannot photograph them, and scholars are not exempt from that rule. Part of his answer about how researchers do get access, through building relationships with the monastery, is unclear in the recording.

### Closing synthesis

Three classes on one episode, covering: the transmitted records and their differences, including the Ba family accounts, and the agendas behind them; why we need to ask where a source comes from; and now the three scholarly perspectives. Setting aside the scholars' personal identities and training, the content reduces to a few oppositions: practice against scholastic and doctrinal study, old school against new schools, and competition among the new schools themselves, in which accusing a rival of being Chinese Chan was the standard attack. Behind all of it sits the political relationship between Tibet and China, with India serving as the alternative lineage Tibetans invoked to balance that connection.

A student asked whether there is an estimate of how many scriptures the Samye library holds. He gave no number, only the experience of walking in and finding yourself in a universe of texts.

## Key people, places and terms

- **Samye (bSam yas)**: the monastery complex founded under Trisong Detsen, the site of the debate. Van Schaik notes it preserves Trisong Detsen's original design plan, with a pillar at the main temple entrance recording the court's oath to protect Buddhism.
- **Trisong Detsen (Tri Songdetsen)**: the tsenpo who presided over the debate. He sought teachings from both India and China, and both readings stress he was not sectarian before the debate story made him so.
- **Moheyan (Hwashang Mahayan)**: the Chinese Chan master who had been teaching at Dunhuang and was invited to central Tibet, where he attracted followers including members of the royal family. Kapstein's glossary gives the Tibetan as "hwa shang ma ha yan," the source of the later term of abuse.
- **Shantarakshita**: the Indian abbot, dead by the time of the debate, who had told the tsenpo to call on Kamalashila in case of dispute.
- **Kamalashila**: Shantarakshita's disciple, an Indian philosopher in his own right, who argued that real wisdom comes through analytical insight and called the Chinese approach "a practice of stupidity." Van Schaik dates him to the eighth century.
- **Ba Selnang**: the nobleman sent to Nepal to invite Shantarakshita and also to China, where he met the Korean Zen teacher known as Reverend Kim. The Ba family accounts are the source for this.
- **Chan / Zen**: the Chinese tradition of immediate awakening. Named in class as one branch among many in Chinese Buddhism, not the dominant one.
- **Tiantai**: the school the instructor called the genuinely dominant and scholastically advanced tradition in Chinese Buddhism. Not covered in the assigned readings.
- **Madhyamaka**: the philosophical position of Shantarakshita and Kamalashila, as described in class.
- **Tantra**: the dominant mode of Indian religious practice from the seventh and especially eighth century, across Buddhism, Jainism and Brahmanism, per the lecture.
- **Padmasambhava (Guru Rinpoche)**: the tantric master to whom the concealment of terma is attributed, and who becomes the "Precious Guru" of the Tibetan people in later revealed literature.
- **Terma / tertön**: concealed treasure texts and objects, and their revealers. Kapstein: among the Nyingmapa, concealment is generally attributed to Padmasambhava; Nyangrel Nyima Özer (1124-1196) built an influential historical and legendary literature out of them.
- **Nyingma ("The Old Ones")**: the older lineages, associated in class with yogis, ngagpas and the sudden path. Van Schaik explains the name arose when people began calling aristocratic lineages like the Zurs "the old ones."
- **Dzogchen (Great Perfection)**: the Nyingma system the lecture compared to Chinese Chan. Van Schaik describes it as teaching a transcendence of meditation itself, pointing to the present mind as ever-present enlightenment.
- **Ngagpa**: lay tantric practitioners, contrasted with monks. Van Schaik's Zur material shows how deep the divisions between monks and ngagpas could run.
- **Sakya / Sakya Pandita**: the scholastic order and its most celebrated figure (1182-1251), whose mission to Godan Khan in 1244 became the precedent for the later Mongol-Sakyapa alliance that made the Sakyapa the effective rulers of Tibet.
- **Dilgo Khyentse Rinpoche**: named in class as the greatest yogi of the twentieth century, a Nyingma lay practitioner in Padmasambhava's lineage. Not in the assigned readings.
- **Shen Weirong**: historian at Tsinghua University, German philological training, argued from the Dunhuang manuscripts that the formal debate never took place.
- **Dunhuang**: the Silk Road city conquered by Trisong Detsen in the 780s, where Moheyan had been teaching, and the source of the manuscript cave. Kapstein dates the Tibetan occupation at roughly three-quarters of a century and the manuscripts' discovery to 1907. The library-cave article gives the Tibetan period as 786 to 848.
- **Yuan, Ming, Qing**: the three dynasties the instructor insisted be called "the government in Beijing," since the first was Mongol and the last Manchu.
- **The thirteenth Dalai Lama**: moved to separate from Beijing after the Qing collapse in 1912.

## Connections to the readings

- **Van Schaik, "The Great Debate" (pp. 36-39 in the chapter "The Holy Buddhist Empire, 700-797")** is the backbone of the whole unit. It supplies the narrative the instructor was taking apart: the shared Mahayana ground (both sides accepting emptiness, "form is emptiness, and emptiness is form" from the Heart Sutra), the disagreement over how that insight is realized, Moheyan's black-and-white-clouds argument that both virtue and sin are products of ordinary thought, Kamalashila's counter that abandoning analysis cuts off wisdom at the root, the victory awarded to the Indian side, and the consequence that the tsenpo would support only the gradual path. Crucially, van Schaik also supplies the instructor's skepticism: "This, anyway, is the Tibetan version of the story," some ancient manuscripts give Moheyan a more nuanced view, a Chinese version has the tsenpo bless the Chinese teachers, and "modern scholars have even questioned whether a debate ever really took place, or whether there was instead a series of discussions and literary exchanges." His line "you can't keep a good story down" is essentially the collective-memory argument the instructor attributed to Shen Weirong.
- **Van Schaik on the escalation before the debate** is worth reading alongside the "political tension" discussion: the Indian teachers complained to the tsenpo that the Chinese were not teaching genuine Buddhism, and the Chinese side responded with self-mutilation and thirty armed adherents threatening murder and collective suicide. The lay ruler's decision to settle this with a formal debate is exactly the secular-power-judging-monastics problem the student raised.
- **Kapstein, The Tibetans, chapter three** gives the compressed scholarly version and supports two of the lecture's claims directly. First, the debate "may well have been in effect a ritual performance that ended in polite accolades for both sides," and the documents "differ as to just who emerged victorious." Second, on Chan's survival: although later historians insist Trisong Detsen banned the Chan teaching, "the evidence suggests that, on the contrary, it continued to thrive in parts of the Tibetan empire, perhaps even with some measure of imperial support." That is the textual basis for the instructor's point that Chinese Buddhism never disappeared from Tibet.
- **Kapstein on later historians** matches the "traditional Tibetan view" section almost word for word: they "revile Moheyan as representing an irrational doctrine of mystical intuition" and treat Kamalashila's gradual cultivation of the bodhisattva virtues as the enduring paradigm.
- **Kapstein, chapter four, on terma** supports the lecture's account of what happened once Indian masters stopped arriving, and adds something the lecture only gestured at: the revealed literature of this age is where imperial memory becomes national religious myth, with Songtsen Gampo recast as Avalokiteshvara.
- **Van Schaik on the Great Perfection and the Zurs** is the place to look for the Dzogchen-Chan comparison, including the caution that Great Perfection instructions "were rarely taken literally" and functioned as a framework for meditation and study rather than as a license to stop practicing. That nuance complicates the neat sudden/gradual mapping the lecture offered.
- **The Dunhuang library cave article** is the methodological counterpart to the Shen Weirong section. It describes what the cave actually contained, including Tibetan pothi texts and rolls alongside Chinese, Khotanese, Uighur and Sogdian material, how the original classification was disturbed, and the lasting impact of the period of Tibetan rule (786 to 848) on Buddhism at Dunhuang. If you want to argue from Dunhuang manuscripts, this is the article that tells you how messy and mediated that evidence is.

## Writing and reading-skills guidance

- **Narrow the topic hard.** He asked directly whether a paper could include everything from these three lectures and answered no. Pick one or two specific angles. The doctrinal angle (Chan against scholastic Madhyamaka, sudden against gradual) is the most tractable. The political angle is harder, because it depends on later periods the course has not covered.
- **Summarize dimension one, analyze dimension two.** His explicit division of labor: in class, his job is to teach the first dimension, what the sources say and whether the event happened. In your paper, you summarize that first dimension and then analyze the second, how the story was read and used, and contribute your own perspective. The paper has to advance your own view, not reproduce his.
- This matches the UNC handout **"Summary: Using It Wisely,"** which is in your readings and worth re-reading before you draft: most of your paper should focus on your argument, not on restating a text; if your essay moves through the history in the same order as the source, that is a warning sign; the useful questions are "Am I making an original argument about the text?" and whether you are only reporting someone else's ideas.
- **A "review paper" is one legitimate option.** He mentioned, in badly garbled phrasing, a paper type that surveys the existing scholarship and then states your own position. If you want to write that kind of essay, confirm the format with him before committing.
- **Methodological lesson worth putting in the paper.** The three-perspectives exercise is itself an argument: training and institutional position shape conclusions even when the method is the same. Traditional Tibetan monastic authors (all male, all monastic, writing after monasteries took political power) produce one account; a philologist trained in Germany produces another by cross-checking manuscripts; a comparativist produces a third by reframing the question as sudden versus gradual. Each is a piece, none is the whole.
- **Presentation craft.** Reorganize your material rather than reading your draft. Build slides. Prepare for questions, since accuracy and clarity under questioning is graded. Manage your nerves; that is part of the grade too.
