# Lecture 3: Legitimacy and the tsenpo, from the Nyatri Tsenpo myth to Songtsen Gampo

## Summary
The class opened by reviewing three points from last time: how Tibetan history is divided into periods, what the name "Tibet" might mean, and what the royal title *tsenpo* means. The main topic was legitimacy. The instructor read the origin myth of Nyatri Tsenpo, the first tsenpo, who came down from heaven. Instead of asking whether the myth is true, the class asked who tells the story, why people believe it, and what it is meant to do. The class split the story into three elements (the divine king, the Bön priest, the common people) and compared Tibetan royal legitimacy with the Mandate of Heaven in China and descent from the gods in Japan. The second half turned to Songtsen Gampo. He came to the throne at 13 after his father was poisoned. The class used his case to discuss murder and child rulers in Tibetan politics and a "mandala" model of power, in which aristocratic clans shared power with the tsenpo and often competed with him for it.

## Lecture notes

### Review: periodizing Tibet
- Tibetan history falls into three periods:
  1. **Imperial**: mid-6th century to mid-9th century. This is the focus of the course. The syllabus gives "the sixth century to the mid-ninth century."
  2. **Medieval**: 10th century to 1959.
  3. **Modern**: 1959 to the present.
- **The name "Tibet"**: nobody knows for certain what it means. One idea is that it comes from the early religion of the region. The instructor thinks it most likely comes from a tribal name. The comparison offered was the name "China," which comes from a dynasty name (Qin).
- **Tsenpo**: the title of the king or emperor of Tibet. It means "powerful" or "strong."

### The myth of Nyatri Tsenpo
- The story: a divine figure comes down from heaven onto the Tibetan Himalaya. At that moment a group of Tibetans and Bön priests are meeting to decide who should lead them. They see him coming down from the sky. Because he came from heaven and is unlike people of the earth, the Bön priests choose him as their ruler.
- **The name**: the people lifted him onto their shoulders and carried him. *Nya* means "neck." *Tri* means "seat" or "throne." So Nyatri Tsenpo is literally "the tsenpo who takes the neck as his seat." *Tri* later appears at the start of many royal names and signals royalty and strength.
- **Lha**: the imperial family is called *lha*. The first meaning is "god," and from there "divine." It then came to mean the royal family. After Buddhism arrived, *lha* came to mean "deity."
  - Prince: a *lha* term the transcriber rendered as "last step." This is probably *lhasé* ("divine son"), but the transcript is unclear.
  - Princess: *lha* plus *mo*, the female marker. The exact form is unclear in the transcript.
- **Myth versus fact**: the story is a legend, not history. At university the questions are different: who produced this story, why did it survive among people, why did people choose to believe it, and what agenda lies behind it?

### Legitimacy
- **Definition**: legitimacy is the belief or recognition that an authority, institution, or ruler has the right to govern.
- **Comparisons**:
  - United States: legitimacy comes from the people, who vote.
  - Ancient China: the **Mandate of Heaven**. The emperor is not divine. He receives heaven's mandate and becomes the "Son of Heaven," a representative of heaven but not heaven itself.
  - Ancient Japan: the **tennō** (emperor) is a descendant of the gods. This is close to the Tibetan case, where both divinity and blood matter.
- **Why legitimacy matters**: a regime without it can still govern, but it is unstable and always at risk of being overthrown. The Japanese example was the Tokugawa family. They held real power in the Edo period but never replaced the tennō, who stayed the source of legitimacy. The instructor argued this weakness contributed to their overthrow. The transcript says "6th century," which is clearly a slip, since the Edo period is the 17th to 19th centuries.
- **Habermas**: the class used the German philosopher and social theorist Jürgen Habermas and his three broad types of legitimacy. The instructor noted he is famous and much debated, not universally accepted. Only the first type was covered: **legitimacy from a myth of origin**. The slide's example was Egyptian pharaohs presenting themselves as the god Horus, son of Osiris. The Nyatri Tsenpo story fits this type:
  - He is a god, the son of a god.
  - The mythological story needs no proof.
  - With little education and few outside points of reference, people accepted it. Its importance lies in how people trusted it and lived inside it, not in whether it was factually true.

### Three elements of the myth
1. **Nyatri Tsenpo**: the source of the regime's legitimacy.
2. **The Bön priest**: the intermediary, a bridge between the gods and the people. He is the one who chooses and empowers the tsenpo. Without his authorization there is no tsenpo, which raises the question of who really holds the power. The tsenpo still contributes his own divinity, and he gains real power once enthroned. That sets up a recurring pattern: kings and priests either cooperate or compete for power.
3. **The common people**: they follow, listen, and above all keep the belief alive. The legend has to live among the public, not only inside the royal family. If people stopped believing and chose another leader, the situation would change completely.

### Bön and the word "religion"
- **"Religion" is a late concept.** It arose in Enlightenment Europe, reached Japan around 1868, and was redefined by Japanese Buddhists. China then borrowed the term from Japan and gave it new meanings again. When you use the word, define it for the context you are writing about. The class still uses "religion" for Bön and Buddhism as a convenience.
- **Before the empire, Bön was the dominant religion and Buddhism was absent.** Do not equate early Tibet with Buddhism.
- **Early Bön was not a systematized religion.** It was closer to shamanism: many local cults grouped under one name. Clans living by rivers worshipped river deities, clans near forests worshipped forest deities and demons, and so on. It had no unified doctrine, philosophy, or form of worship.
- **Later Bön was deeply reshaped by Buddhism.** It survives today in Tibet and in Sichuan, and it is often regarded as a branch of Tibetan Buddhism.

### Songtsen Gampo
- **The name**: no one knows what "Songtsen Gampo" means. The instructor guessed something like "majestic" but said it is only a guess.
- **Accession at 13**: he took the throne at 13 after his father's death. His father was most likely assassinated. Either his father's clan or his mother's clan, or both together, may have conspired, and even without firm proof they clearly benefited from the death.
- **Suppressing the revolt**: Songtsen gathered the forces his father left him, defeated his opponents, and began unifying the Tibetan region.
- **Abdication and return**: he later passed the throne to his son. The son died first, and Songtsen took the throne back.
- **Why 13?** Many tsenpos came to the throne at 13. A traditional formula says that when the prince is old enough to ride a horse, the father "ascends to heaven" and the prince takes over.
  - "Ascending to heaven" is a euphemism for death.
  - The instructor's speculation: the old tsenpo may often have been killed when the heir reached about 13.
  - The instructor called this their own theory, still being tested, and invited students to gather evidence for or against it.

### Murder and poison in Tibetan politics
- Murder, especially by poison, recurs across Tibetan history in the imperial period and later.
- **Dalai Lamas**: the instructor said only four of the fourteen Dalai Lamas grew to adulthood and became powerful figures: the 3rd, the 5th, the 13th and the 14th.
  - The 5th was the most powerful and allied with the Chinese emperor. The instructor called him "the real first Dalai Lama."
  - At the Potala Palace, many of the stupas holding past Dalai Lamas' remains are small, because many died young. The instructor said they were murdered, often poisoned.
  - The instructor named the 6th Dalai Lama as a poisoning case and called him "Mongolian." The readings disagree on both points; see Connections below.
- **Bhutan**: a small Himalayan kingdom whose foreign and military affairs are tied to India. Its people are a branch of Tibetans, and its state religion is Tibetan Buddhism (Drukpa Kagyü; see transcription fixes). The instructor said some areas still have poisoning traditions.
- **Two motives for poisoning**:
  - Political: removing a rival or ruler to take power.
  - Superstitious: the belief among certain Tibetans, possibly linked to Bön, that poisoning a healthy person transfers that person's life force to the poisoner. For that reason children were sometimes targets.
- The class will return to murder later in the course.

### Di Cosmo's crisis theory
- The instructor said Professor (Nicola) Di Cosmo holds that nomadic and semi-nomadic peoples build empires after a crisis. If a strong leader survives and manages the crisis, an empire is likely to follow.
- The instructor called this untested and suggested students test it against Tibet.
- Tibetans count as semi-nomads rather than full nomads like the Mongols. Some herded, some traded.

### The mandala model of imperial Tibetan power
- **Imperial Tibet was not centralized like China.** In a centralized state the emperor directs local areas with no intermediate power. The tsenpo, although called an emperor, was more like the head of an alliance of clans.
- **The mandala model**: the tsenpo sits at the center. Around him are aristocrats, like the deities around a central figure in a mandala. They protect him and also share his power.
- **Land**: there was "king's land," and there were estates belonging to aristocrats and ministers. Ministers ran their estates like small kingdoms, with their own economic and military power.
- **Loyalty was unstable.** Ministers could obey the tsenpo, compete with him, or overthrow him. Because whoever sits on the throne can be overthrown, ambitious ministers preferred to be the power behind the throne. They installed child tsenpos they could control.
- **Japanese parallel**: *gekokujō*, "those below overthrow those above." Another parallel is the tennō under Fujiwara control in the Heian period: an object of worship with little real power.
- **Result**: tsenpos often died violent or untimely deaths. The pattern was to kill the old tsenpo and install a young one. It continued into later periods, including the Dalai Lamas. The last phrase of the transcript ("the area of the non") is unintelligible.
- **Skipped material**: the instructor skipped the legendary kings between Nyatri Tsenpo and Songtsen and offered to tell those stories in office hours.

## Key people, places and terms
- **Tsenpo** (*btsan po*): title of the Tibetan emperor, meaning "powerful, strong." Kapstein ch. 2–3; van Schaik ch. 1.
- **Nyatri Tsenpo** (*gnya' khri*): the first tsenpo, who came down from heaven; the name means "Shoulder(-borne) Throne." In the earliest version the name is Nyaktri, "Notch Throne." Kapstein ch. 2, pp. 38–39.
- **Tri** (*khri*): "throne, seat." It begins many royal names, such as Tri Songdetsen and Tri Relpachen. Kapstein glossary.
- **Lha**: god, divine; later the royal family; in Buddhism, a deity. van Schaik ch. 1 ("Son of the Gods," "Divine Son"); Kapstein ch. 2.
- **Bön**: the pre-Buddhist religion of Tibet. Its priests acted as intermediaries between gods and people, and the later organized Bön religion was shaped by Buddhism. Kapstein ch. 2, pp. 48–49.
- **Yüllha**: "god of the land," the local deity of a valley, river or mountain. It illustrates the local cults that made up early Bön. Kapstein ch. 2, p. 48.
- **Legitimacy**: the belief or recognition that a ruler or institution has the right to govern. Lecture only.
- **Mandate of Heaven**: the Chinese source of imperial legitimacy; the emperor is heaven's representative, not a god. Lecture only.
- **Tennō**: the Japanese emperor, legitimated as a descendant of the gods. Lecture only.
- **Jürgen Habermas**: German philosopher and social theorist whose types of legitimacy include legitimacy from a myth of origin. Lecture only.
- **Songtsen Gampo** (c. 605–650): the tsenpo who took the throne at 13 after his father was poisoned and unified Tibet. "Gampo" means "the Wise." van Schaik ch. 1 ("Tibet Appears, 600–700"); Kapstein ch. 3.
- **Namri Löntsen**: Songtsen's father, assassinated by poison around 618. He was not named in class. Kapstein ch. 3, pp. 58–59.
- **Gungsong Gungtsen**: Songtsen's son. Songtsen abdicated in his favor in 641, and Gungsong died in 646. Kapstein ch. 3.
- **Mandala model**: the instructor's image of imperial power, with the tsenpo at the center surrounded by aristocratic clans who protect him and share his power. Lecture; compare Kapstein's "primus inter pares."
- **Zhanglön**: "minister who is a maternal uncle"; a noble from a clan that supplied royal brides. Not used in class, but it fits the discussion of the mother's clan. Kapstein ch. 3, p. 58.
- **Gekokujō**: Japanese term, "those below overthrow those above." Lecture only.
- **Nicola Di Cosmo**: historian of Inner Asia whose theory, as presented in class, is that nomadic empires form after a crisis. Lecture only.
- **Potala Palace**: the Dalai Lamas' palace in Lhasa, holding the stupas of past Dalai Lamas. Kapstein (Plate 18; built by the Fifth Dalai Lama).
- **Sixth Dalai Lama, Tsangyang Gyatso** (1683–1706): Kapstein ch. 6; van Schaik ch. on the Dalai Lamas.
- **Bhutan**: Himalayan kingdom where Drukpa Kagyü has been the state religion since the 17th century. Kapstein, p. near "Drukpa Kagyüpa order."
- **Yarlung**: the valley in southern Tibet where the royal line began. van Schaik ch. 1; Kapstein ch. 3.

## Connections to the readings
- **Periodization**: this matches the syllabus course description ("the sixth century to the mid-ninth century"). van Schaik's first chapter is "Tibet Appears, 600–700."
- **The name "Tibet"**:
  - van Schaik's Preface gives the Tibetan name *Bod* (pronounced "bö") for central Tibet and *Bod chen po* for "Greater Tibet."
  - Kapstein ch. 2 (pp. 48–49) reports a proposal that *bön* is related to *bö*, the word for Tibet. He adds that Dunhuang manuscripts sometimes use *bön* to name Tibet. This supports the student suggestion in class that the name may connect to the early religion.
- **Nyatri Tsenpo myth**:
  - Kapstein ch. 2, pp. 38–40, quotes the earliest version, a Dunhuang document from about 800. Tri Nyaktri Tsenpo comes down from "divine heaven" to the mountain Gyangdo.
  - Kapstein explains that "Notch Throne" was later reinterpreted as "Nyatri," "Shoulder(-borne) Throne." The story then grew to include the people hoisting him up in a palanquin. That is the "neck as seat" meaning taught in class.
  - Kapstein's comment on legitimacy fits the lecture's analysis: the kings' "semi-divine status served to justify both their rule and the hierarchical divisions of society."
- **The sky cord and "ascending to heaven"**:
  - van Schaik ch. 1 ("The Divine Destiny of Prince Songtsen"): the first tsenpos went back to heaven on a "sky cord," a connection later lost by Drigum. "Nowadays... the tsenpos died like other men."
  - Kapstein ch. 2, p. 40: the "seven heavenly thrones" dissolved into light and left no corpses.
  - Both are useful background for the instructor's reading of "ascended to heaven" as a euphemism for death.
- **Bön**:
  - Kapstein ch. 2, pp. 48–49 distinguishes the pre-Buddhist religion from the organized Bön of today, which took shape around the tenth century as "a form of Tibetan Buddhism."
  - Kapstein notes that in Dunhuang documents *bön* usually names "a particular type of priest," not a religion. This matches the Bön priest role discussed in class.
  - The *yüllha* passage (p. 48) matches the river and mountain cults described in class. Plate 31 shows a Bönpo assembly in Sichuan.
- **Songtsen's accession**:
  - van Schaik ch. 1: "When Songtsen was just thirteen, his father was poisoned." The absorbed territories rose up; Songtsen executed the traitor and put down the revolt.
  - Kapstein ch. 3, pp. 58–59: Namri Löntsen was "assassinated by poison" around 618, "leaving his thirteen-year-old son as his heir." Kapstein notes that some scholars date these events later (629).
- **Abdication**: Kapstein ch. 3 says Songtsen abdicated in 641 in favor of Gungsong Gungtsen, "though his abdication may well have been more symbolic than real." Gungsong died in 646, and Songtsen "openly resumed his rule."
- **Clans, ministers, child rulers**:
  - van Schaik ch. 1: the clan leaders "supported the tsenpo when it served their interests, and conspired against him when that seemed more useful... as the new tsenpo was usually just a child, there was ample opportunity for ministers, especially the prime minister, to become the real power behind the throne." This is nearly the instructor's argument word for word.
  - Kapstein ch. 3, pp. 58–59: the tsenpo ruled as "primus inter pares," the *zhanglön* system tied royal in-laws to power, and the monarchy was "profoundly unstable." This backs up the mandala model.
- **Sixth Dalai Lama**:
  - The instructor called him Mongolian and said he was poisoned. Kapstein says he was found in Tawang (today Arunachal Pradesh) and "died as a captive in Qinghai, perhaps not actually executed." van Schaik says only that "some said he had been poisoned."
  - Worth checking before using this example in writing.
- **Bhutan**: Kapstein confirms that Drukpa Kagyü "established itself during the seventeenth century as the religion of the state in Bhutan."

## Writing and reading-skills guidance
- **Ask different questions of a myth.** In college, whether a legend is literally true is a primary-school question. Ask who tells the story, why people believe it, why it survives, and what agenda it serves. A myth can be "a fact of how people trusted it" even when it is not historical fact.
- **Analyze by breaking your sources into elements.** The method for a paper:
  1. Gather your materials.
  2. Categorize them into their main elements instead of analyzing from every angle at once. Here the elements were the tsenpo, the priest and the people.
  3. Work through each element in turn, writing a few paragraphs on each, starting with the most important.
- **Look for the less obvious actor.** The analysis shifted from the king to the priest and asked who really held the power to confer legitimacy. Minor figures in a source can change the argument.
- **Define loaded terms in context.** Words like "religion" have histories and change meaning across cultures, so say which definition you are using.
- **Test theories instead of repeating them.** Two theories were offered for students to test with evidence: Di Cosmo's crisis-to-empire theory and the instructor's age-13 succession theory. This matches the syllabus rule that essays must bring a new topic or perspective rather than summarize lectures.
- **Use comparison to sharpen analysis.** Comparing China, Japan, the US and Egypt clarified what was distinctive about Tibetan legitimacy: divine descent plus blood.
