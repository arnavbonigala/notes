# Lecture 32: Why Tri Songdetsen Needed Buddhism, and What "Dharma King" Meant in His Own Time

## Summary

This session closed out the unit on Tri Songdetsen (742 to c. 797) by asking a political question rather than a religious one: what did the tsenpo get out of making Buddhism the state religion in 762? The instructor gave three answers. Buddhism handed Tri Songdetsen a ready-made institutional system he could staff and control independently of the old clan ministers; it gave him a pool of personnel drawn from noble families but detached from their clan bases; and it split the aristocracy along religious lines, weakening them as a bloc. The lecture then set up a comparison exercise between the "first dharma king" (Songtsen Gampo) and the "second dharma king" (Tri Songdetsen), arguing that the same title does not mean the same thing in both cases, and offering three angles for organizing that comparison. It ended with the question of imperial funeral ritual (Bon specialists, Buddhist monks, or both) and with the claim that by the end of Tri Songdetsen's reign Buddhist ideology had become the court's own language of legitimacy, not an import it merely tolerated.

## Lecture notes

### Recap: a boy king without leverage

The instructor restarted from Tri Songdetsen's accession. He came to the throne young, under ministers whose allegiance he could not assume, and with no independent power base of his own. Kapstein puts him on the throne at thirteen after the ministerial rebellion of 755 in which his father Tri Detsuktsen was assassinated, and describes him as "for some years a powerless ward of the ruling lords," lords who had banned Buddhism outright (Kapstein, ch. 3). Van Schaik dates the enthronement to 756, the same year An Lushan declared the end of the Tang, and notes the "youthful new tsenpo was able to sit back and watch as China descended into chaos" (van Schaik, ch. 2).

The point the instructor stressed is that Tri Songdetsen was lucky in the most basic way: he lived long enough to grow up and make a move. By twenty he had come into power in his own right.

### Digression: Dalai Lamas who did not grow up

To illustrate how rare it is for a child ruler in Tibetan history to survive to adulthood, the instructor broke off into the Dalai Lama lineage. His count was that only about four Dalai Lamas really reached adulthood and ruled as adults, and he named the fifth, the thirteenth, and the fourteenth. Many of the others died very young, before twelve or in their teens. One "disappeared," which matches van Schaik's account of the sixth Dalai Lama, Tsangyang Gyatso: after his death many Tibetans refused to believe it, "some said he had been poisoned," and rumours circulated "that he had actually escaped his captors and was travelling in disguise through Tibet."

He also mentioned a Mongolian Dalai Lama connected to Altan Khan who died around his early twenties. The transcript here is garbled (it says "princess" and "murdered"). The figure is the fourth Dalai Lama, Yonten Gyatso, whom van Schaik identifies as the grandson of Altan Khan, recognized in his thirteenth year, and who "did not live long enough to settle into his role," dying in 1617 in his mid-twenties after illness, not murder. He remains the only non-Tibetan to have held the position. The exact numbers in the instructor's count were not fully audible and should be checked against van Schaik ch. 6 before being used in writing.

### Reason one: Buddhism as a ready-made institution

The first political motive. Tri Songdetsen needed people who would support him personally, and Buddhism supplied not just a doctrine but an existing system: regulations, organizational structures, offices, and a model of administration already working in India and China. The instructor's phrasing was that the tsenpo "yielded up this system, this institution," meaning he adopted a going concern rather than building one.

Kapstein gives the material shape of this. After Samye was founded, "one-hundred-fifty households were assigned by the court to be the appanages of the religious community," with fixed annual allotments of barley, butter, robes, horses, paper, ink, and salt, "in effect a tax imposed for the maintenance of the religion" (Testament of Ba, quoted in Kapstein ch. 3). That is a state-funded parallel bureaucracy.

### Reason two: monks as an alternative to the uncle-ministers

The second motive follows from the first. The monks staffing this institution were often themselves former ministers or men from ministerial families, and once ordained they operated inside a separate, independent hierarchy answerable to the tsenpo rather than to the clan structure. The instructor's point: "to help me to run the country," but from a track separated from the old home ministers.

He then flagged the problem this creates, and this was the most important analytical move of the hour. Buddhism, once endorsed by the throne as a political power, becomes a competitor to lay secular power. The instructor framed this as the beginning of a long structural struggle between religious and secular authority in Tibetan history, not a one-time arrangement. The readings bear out where this leads: by the 822 treaty the chief negotiator reciting the oath is "the monk prime minister Palgyi Yonten" (van Schaik, ch. 2), and Kapstein records the same figure, "the monk-minister Trenka Pelgi Yönten," being destroyed by court conspiracy under Relpachen.

### Reason three: splitting the nobility

The third motive. The monks came from elite noble families, and those families did not agree with one another. Some backed the old religion and its ritual specialists; some backed Buddhism. The instructor's conclusion: if the nobles are not keeping the same pace or moving in the same direction, their collective power is divided and weakened. Religious division among the aristocracy was therefore useful to the throne.

Van Schaik makes the same argument from the other end: "the ministers who initially opposed his coming to the throne were from the anti-Buddhist faction, and adopting Buddhism as the state religion would have been a good way of placing them beyond the pale" (ch. 2).

### The caution: motive is not character

The instructor paused to say that none of this is a verdict on Tri Songdetsen as a person. The claim is not that he was insincere or a bad man. The claim is that in this period religion was inevitably drawn into political power competition, so a ruler's religious policy is always also a political act. Van Schaik states the historiographical split directly: traditional Tibetan Buddhist historians saw the conversion as "a purely personal religious matter," while "modern scholars have tended to see the conversion of Trisong in a more political light" (ch. 2). The instructor is working on the modern side of that split while refusing to reduce the man to his motives.

### The comparison exercise: first dharma king versus second dharma king

Both Songtsen Gampo and Tri Songdetsen are called dharma king. The instructor asked whether the title refers to the same thing in both cases. The answer given in class was no, and he deliberately did not supply the content of the difference. Instead he supplied the perspectives from which to find it. Three angles:

1. Their agenda or purpose in relation to Buddhism.
2. Their approach, meaning the concrete way each one promoted or handled Buddhism.
3. The political results each one produced.

He told the class these three perspectives are how to organize an essay on the question.

### Funeral ritual: Bon, Buddhist, or both

Students had raised the question of imperial funerals, on the view that they were conducted by Bon ministers using Bon rituals. The instructor's correction was a methodological one. For Songtsen Gampo we do have descriptions of the funeral. For Tri Songdetsen we have no evidence about the funerary rites at all. So the honest answer is that his funeral may have been performed by the old ritual specialists, or may have incorporated Buddhist rites alongside them, and we cannot say which.

To make the "both at once" option plausible he used a Chinese comparison: when someone dies in China, the family may hire both Daoist and Buddhist ritualists, sometimes performing in the house simultaneously, sometimes sequenced, with the early days of mourning handled at home and a later stage (he mentioned roughly fourteen days) taken to a Buddhist monastery for monks to perform rites for the deceased. His conclusion: we have no reason to exclude the same kind of sharing in imperial Tibet.

This lines up precisely with an episode in van Schaik's ch. 2. Under the ban, "Buddhist rituals, because funerals were the speciality of Tibet's non-Buddhist priests, were banned." When the Lhasa noble Ba Selnang lost a son and a daughter, "he arranged for a traditional Tibetan ritual to be performed outside his house, but asked an old Chinese monk to come secretly into the house to perform the Buddhist ritual as well." Two ritual systems, one death, running at the same time.

### The queen's donation and the bell inscription

The instructor then gave the class a piece of evidence not in the assigned readings, so this part cannot be checked against van Schaik or Kapstein. After Tri Songdetsen's death, one of his consorts, a queen, donated a bell to a monastery. The donation was made to fund offerings for the late tsenpo and to help accumulate merit for him. The inscription on the bell, he said, explicitly describes the tsenpo as a bodhisattva.

Two conclusions he drew from it:

First, within one generation the queen and the younger prince are already operating inside Buddhist ideology. Their instinct on how to care for a dead king is a Buddhist instinct (merit, offerings, dedication) rather than the old one. The instructor's framing was that they accepted Buddhist thought in place of the earlier tradition. Note that the transcript at this point is unclear about whether he meant full abandonment of the old ritual order or a shift in the ruling family's own idiom, and given his own argument in the previous section about ritual sharing, the weaker reading is the safer one.

Second, the bodhisattva language in the inscription means that Tri Songdetsen was regarded as a dharma king in his own lifetime and immediately after it, by the people around him. That is the contrast he was building toward: Songtsen Gampo was not called this in his own day. The identification of Songtsen Gampo as an emanation of Avalokiteshvara is a later construction. Kapstein notes that the monkey-and-ogress origin story with its Avalokiteshvara emanations "is first known only from post-tenth-century Buddhist works," and that "in later times, accounts of Songtsen Gampo's civilizational enterprise were much elaborated. The monarch came to be regarded as an emanation of the bodhisattva Avalokiteshvara." The instructor guessed a gap of one to two centuries or more and said openly that nobody knows the exact interval.

### What a dharma king is supposed to be

He closed by unpacking the Buddhist ideal behind the title. He reminded the class of the two kinds of figure introduced earlier in the course: an enlightened being, and a person on the path working toward enlightenment. The dharma king belongs to the first type, the enlightened one, at the top.

Because he is enlightened, he possesses the two qualities that define the ideal: wisdom and compassion. Compassion gives him mercy toward his subjects; wisdom gives him the capacity to guide and teach them. Both qualities are held by one person, who is simultaneously ruler and spiritual authority.

The final claim of the lecture: by Tri Songdetsen's time, Buddhist ideology and Buddhist cosmology had become the framework in which Tibetan rulership was conceived and justified. Buddhism had moved from foreign import to the court's own language of political legitimacy.

Note: the last stretch of the recording is badly degraded. Several sentences in this closing passage, including what appears to be a specific Tibetan or Sanskrit term for the dharma king, are unintelligible and have not been reconstructed here.

## Key people, places and terms

**Tri Songdetsen** (742 to c. 797, spelled Trisong Detsen in van Schaik). Enthroned 756 at thirteen after his father's assassination in the rebellion of 755. Made Buddhism the state religion by edict in 762. Occupied Chang'an in 763. Founded Samye. The "second dharma king."

**Songtsen Gampo**. The "first dharma king." Credited with the Lhasa temples and with tolerance of Buddhism rather than its establishment. His elaborate funeral is described in van Schaik ch. 1. Identified with Avalokiteshvara only in much later sources.

**Dharma king** (chos rgyal). Van Schaik's index points to pp. 30 to 33, 120, 150, 204; his section heading "The Dharma King" covers the 762 edict. Three dharma kings form a standard set in later tradition (see van Schaik's plate 3, "Statues representing the three dharma kings, Palkor Chode"), and the "three ancestral dharma kings" are still invoked by the thirteenth Dalai Lama in his political testament (van Schaik ch. 9).

**Bodhisattva**. In this lecture, the model the dharma king is measured against: an enlightened being combining wisdom and compassion, as distinct from a practitioner still on the path.

**Tri Detsuktsen**. Tri Songdetsen's father, assassinated in the ministerial rebellion of 755.

**Uncle-ministers** (zhang lon). The clan aristocrats who ran the court during Tri Songdetsen's minority. His own edict, quoted by Kapstein: "After the Tsenpo my father passed into heaven, some of the uncle-ministers had thoughts of rebellion. They destroyed the Buddha's Dharma."

**Ba Selnang**. Lhasa noble, convert, invited Shantarakshita from Nepal, later sent to China. The dual-ritual funeral for his two children is the key evidence for ritual sharing.

**Samye**. First Tibetan Buddhist monastery, completed in the 780s per van Schaik, dated c. 779 by Kapstein. Supported by 150 assigned households.

**Shantarakshita** and **Padmasambhava**. Abbot and tantric adept. With Tri Songdetsen they form what Kapstein calls "the trinity of the Tibetan conversion," representing patron, monk, and tantric adept.

**Palgyi Yonten** (Trenka Pelgi Yönten). Monk prime minister who recited the 822 Sino-Tibetan treaty. The clearest later illustration of the lecture's point about monks in government.

**Yonten Gyatso**. Fourth Dalai Lama, grandson of Altan Khan, recognized in his thirteenth year, died 1617 in his mid-twenties. The only non-Tibetan Dalai Lama.

**Tsangyang Gyatso**. Sixth Dalai Lama, the one said in rumour to have escaped rather than died.

**Bon / non-Buddhist ritual specialists**. The funeral professionals of the old order. Note that van Schaik and Kapstein are careful about the word "Bon" for the imperial period; the instructor used it loosely in this session.

## Connections to the readings

**Van Schaik, "The Dharma King," ch. 2 (pp. 30 to 33).** The direct source for the whole middle of the lecture. It contains the 762 edict, the anti-Buddhist ban of Tri Songdetsen's youth, the traditional-versus-modern explanations of his conversion, the argument about placing anti-Buddhist ministers "beyond the pale," and the observation that Buddhism was "an international religion" and "a cultural language spoken by all," whereas Tibet's own religion "was specific to Tibet" and "must have seemed rather parochial." That contrast is the clearest textual support for the instructor's first reason, Buddhism as an off-the-shelf system with transregional prestige.

**Van Schaik, "The Great Monastery," ch. 2.** The Ba clan narrative. The buried statue, the temples turned into butcher's shops, the ban on Buddhist funerary ritual, Ba Selnang's secret double funeral, Shantarakshita's quarantine in the Jokhang and cross-examination by a minister, Padmasambhava's expulsion, the Samye oath pillar ("The supports for the Three Jewels set up in the temples of Rasa, Dragmar and elsewhere must never be abandoned"), and the circulation of the oath "from Zhangzhung in the west to Amdo in the northeast." Read the Ba Selnang funeral episode directly against the instructor's Chinese Daoist-plus-Buddhist comparison. It is the same structure.

**Kapstein, ch. 3, on Tri Songdetsen.** Supplies the accession details the lecture recapped, the full text of the Karchung pillar inscription issued by Tri Songdetsen's son Tri Desongtsen (d. 815), the conversion edict, and the Testament of Ba allotments to Samye. The Karchung inscription is the best available substitute for the bell inscription the instructor described, because it does the same work: a successor publicly enrolling his father and ancestors in an unbroken Buddhist royal line, with a sworn curse against abandoning it. Kapstein's comment on it is directly usable: the descendants "were eager to regard his tolerance of the alien faith as a precedent for their own definite adoption of it, and to formulate official propoganda accordingly."

**Van Schaik, ch. 1 (pp. 14 to 15), Songtsen Gampo's funeral.** The counterpart to the lecture's funeral discussion. The months-long preparation, faces painted red and hair cut, the trapezoid earthen tomb 13 metres high and 130 metres long, the animal sacrifices, the copper coffin, the sealing pillar, and the "living dead" retainers who spent their lives inside the tomb grounds. Note van Schaik's own hedge: "Only a few scattered descriptions of these great funerals remain." That is exactly the instructor's point about evidence, applied to the king for whom we do have something.

**Kapstein, ch. 3, on later tradition.** The passage stating that the great imperial monarchs "were the emanations of bodhisattvas whose outstanding achievement was not their conquest of large parts of Central Asia, but the introduction of the civilizing influence of the Buddha's teaching." This is the retrospective frame the instructor was trying to pry apart from contemporaneous evidence.

**Van Schaik, ch. 6, on the fourth and sixth Dalai Lamas.** Use this to check the numbers in the lecture's digression. The digression's details were loose in the recording.

**Not in the readings.** The bell donated by Tri Songdetsen's queen, and its inscription, do not appear in van Schaik, Kapstein, or the Dunhuang library cave article. If you want to use this in an essay, note that the final essay must rely exclusively on materials assigned in the course, so the bell inscription cannot be a source. Cite the Karchung inscription in Kapstein instead and ask the instructor about the bell in office hours.

## Writing and reading-skills guidance

**The three-perspective frame is an essay structure, not just a discussion prompt.** The instructor said it plainly: use agenda or purpose, approach, and political results to organize your answer on the two dharma kings. Each perspective becomes a section in which both kings appear, rather than one block on Songtsen Gampo followed by one block on Tri Songdetsen. The UNC Reorganizing Drafts handout covers this: in "a compare and contrast paper like this one, where you distinguish between and explain fashions from two separate time periods, listing can help clarify both the organization and the argument." Make the list of comparison points first, then build paragraphs on the points.

**Interrogate a term before you compare the things it names.** The whole exercise depends on noticing that "dharma king" is applied to both men and may not mean the same thing twice. This is the course's central skill, stated in the syllabus as recognizing "how a single name can carry multiple meanings." Applied here: ask who used the title, when, and about whom. A title applied in a king's lifetime and a title applied two centuries later are different kinds of evidence even when the words are identical.

**Date your evidence, not just your events.** The instructor would not guess how long after Songtsen Gampo's death he was first called a bodhisattva, and he said so out loud rather than filling the gap. Kapstein's "first known only from post-tenth-century Buddhist works" is the kind of phrase to imitate. When your only source for a seventh-century claim is a twelfth-century text, say so in the sentence where you make the claim.

**Absence of evidence is a finding you can write down.** We have descriptions of Songtsen Gampo's funeral and nothing for Tri Songdetsen's. The instructor did not invent a Buddhist funeral for Tri Songdetsen, and he did not assert a purely Bon one either. He laid out both possibilities and marked them as possibilities. In an essay this looks like naming the range of plausible readings and the reason the sources cannot choose between them.

**Comparative cases can make a claim plausible without proving it.** The Chinese double-funeral example does not show anything about Tibet. It shows that two ritual systems serving one death is a normal arrangement, which removes a reason to assume exclusivity. That is a legitimate move as long as you label it as what it is. The parallel inside the reading, Ba Selnang hiring both at once, is stronger evidence than the Chinese case because it is Tibetan and eighth-century. Lead with the stronger one.

**Separate a ruler's motives from a judgment on his character.** The instructor insisted on this. Writing that Tri Songdetsen adopted Buddhism for political advantage is an argument about function. Writing that he was therefore cynical or insincere is a claim about an inner state, and van Schaik warns that "intentions are, of course, hard enough to divine in our contemporaries, let alone in figures from the distant part." Keep the first and drop the second.
