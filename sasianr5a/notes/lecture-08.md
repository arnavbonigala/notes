# Lecture 8: Why Songtsen Gampo Adopted Buddhism When Bon Was Already There (Buddhism vs. Bon as Intellectual Resources)

## Summary
The class started from a student's proposed essay topic: why Songtsen Gampo brought Buddhism into Tibet when Bon already served as an intellectual resource for the state. The instructor used it to show how to set up a research-based paper. You break the question into its elements (an agent, Songtsen Gampo, and two objects, Buddhism and Bon), pick a method (comparison), and hold off on any conclusion until the research is done. The instructor then gave a long overview of Buddhism as it stood by the mid-7th century: the Tripitaka, the Vinaya, the Abhidharma, Mahayana thinkers such as Nagarjuna and Vasubandhu, Xuanzang's journey to Nalanda, and the later rise of tantric Buddhism. A map of how Buddhism spread across Asia came next, and Bon was compared with Buddhism on doctrine and on reach. The class concluded that Buddhism was already a transnational "civilization" and a "common language" in Asia. On that reading, Songtsen Gampo adopted it to connect Tibet to its Buddhist neighbors, and he did not use it to replace Bon, which still held strong influence and political power.

## Lecture notes

### 1. Designing a research-based paper (worked example)
- **Topic under analysis:** Why did Songtsen Gampo introduce Buddhism to Tibet when Bon was already there as an intellectual resource for the state?
- **Step 1: Break the topic into key elements.**
  - Agent: Songtsen Gampo.
  - Objects (the two targets of research): Buddhism and Bon.
- **Step 2: Choose a method.** The class suggested comparison. The instructor called comparison "fundamental," though not the only tool available.
- **Step 3: Choose main and secondary perspectives.** The instructor suggested making Buddhism the main factor, since the question is about why Buddhism in particular was accepted.
- **Step 4: Decide what dimensions to compare on.** Two were proposed:
  1. **Doctrine and theory:** what each system is as an intellectual entity (doctrines, philosophy, texts).
  2. **Community and influence:** how many people actually embraced and followed each system, and how far its views were applied in daily life and at the state level.
- **Step 5: Go back to the agent.** A comparison of the two objects can't tell you what Songtsen Gampo intended. Claims about his motives need concrete sources: historical texts and records, archaeological material, or art.

### 2. Buddhism as a doctrinal system by the mid-7th century

**Timeline framing**
- Songtsen Gampo is placed in the 7th century.
- The Buddha is dated roughly to the 6th-5th century BCE. The instructor stressed that there is no firm historical dating and that scholars' and Buddhist traditions' estimates differ. A Chinese record was mentioned, but that passage is garbled.
- Buddhism had reached China by around the 1st century CE.

**Phase 1: The Tripitaka ("Three Baskets")**
After the Buddha's death, his disciples gathered his teachings into three collections:
- **Sutra:** records of the Buddha's direct speech and discourses. They cover everything, including philosophy and logic (*pramana*).
  - Buddhism has no single central text like the Bible. Tradition speaks of "84,000" teachings, an Indian figure of speech for a countless number.
  - The instructor will assign a short sutra reading.
- **Vinaya:** monastic discipline, vows and precepts. The instructor compared it to the ritual code of Confucianism, recalling from an earlier session how the Tibetan side was impressed by the ritual conduct of the Chinese delegation during the Wencheng marriage negotiations. The Vinaya has two sides:
  - *Mental:* taking refuge in the Buddha, Dharma and Sangha, and generating *bodhicitta*, the mind set on enlightenment for the sake of becoming a Buddha. *Bodhicitta* combines compassion and wisdom.
  - *Behavioral:* shaved heads, robes in set colors, and rules on eating and conduct.
- **Abhidharma / Shastra:** *abhi* means "above, higher, superior" and *dharma* means "teaching, principle," so Abhidharma is "superior knowledge." Shastras are commentaries or treatises. They do two things:
  1. Explain the Buddha's discourses word by word.
  2. Set out Buddhist cosmology, meaning how Buddhism understands the phenomena of the world and the truths behind them.

**Monastic practice details from the Vinaya discussion**
- **Robe colors:** orange or yellow in the Theravada tradition, red in Tibet, black in Japan. The color given for China is unintelligible in the transcript. Each color carries symbolic meaning.
- **Vegetarianism is a misunderstanding.** Under the Vinaya, monks:
  1. do not eat after noon;
  2. do not choose their food. On the morning alms round they go to seven households and accept whatever is placed in the bowl, meat included.

  This practice survives in Theravada countries such as Thailand and Sri Lanka, and the instructor encouraged students to try short-term ordination there.
- **Chinese vegetarianism** comes from a devout Chinese emperor (not named in class). He reasoned that animals are sentient beings, one of the six realms, and banned meat for Chinese monks. Korea and Japan followed the Chinese practice.
- **The six realms:** gods, asuras (demigods), humans, animals, hungry ghosts, hell beings.
- **Tibet eats meat** because the high plateau makes growing plants difficult.
- **Other rules:** for example, a monk may not sit alone with a woman in a closed room. The instructor framed such rules as protection, much like rules in a military setting.
- **Number of vows:** the counts for fully ordained monks and nuns were given, but the numbers are garbled in the transcript.

**Phase 2: Mahayana (from about the 1st century BCE)**
- The earlier phase can be called "mainstream" Buddhism.
- *Maha* means "great" and *yana* means "vehicle" or "path." Mahayana expanded and developed Buddhist doctrine.
- **Two pillars:**
  - **Nagarjuna** (about 2nd-3rd century): Madhyamaka, the "Middle Way." His key idea is *shunyata*, emptiness: everything changes and nothing has a fixed, concrete nature. Emptiness was easily mistaken for "nothingness," a denial of the world, relationships and existence, and other schools attacked Buddhism on that point.
  - **Vasubandhu** (about 4th-5th century): Cittamatra, "Mind Only," which holds that only mind exists and has a real nature. The instructor noted this is not our ordinary mind. It was presented as a response to the confusion about emptiness.
- Later Buddhist doctrine largely explains and reinterprets these two systems. The instructor said the class will return to them in the Buddhism section of the course.
- **Xuanzang** was a contemporary of Songtsen Gampo. (The instructor first said "mid-6th century" and then corrected it.)
  - He is the monk in *Journey to the West*. The monkey character's prototype was said to be Hanuman.
  - He traveled to India and studied at Nalanda for almost ten years, then brought back advanced doctrine, especially Mind Only, along with many texts.
  - The texts he brought back are said to be held in a stupa in Xi'an. The Chinese government does not allow it to be excavated.
  - The Tang court welcomed him and sponsored the translation of Sanskrit texts into Chinese, and those translations went into the Chinese canon.
  - The point: Buddhism was influential in China because its doctrines were sophisticated and well developed, and the court sponsored it.

**Phase 3: Tantric Buddhism (Mantrayana, "vehicle of secret mantras," esotericism)**
- It began in India in the 7th century and reached China in the mid-7th century.
- It reached Japan around the 9th century and still survives there, although it largely declined in China.

**Bottom line on doctrine:** by Songtsen Gampo's time (mid-7th century), Buddhism had a highly advanced, sophisticated and mature doctrinal system.

### 3. Bon compared on doctrine
The instructor deliberately called Bon an "intellectual resource" and avoided calling it a "religion." Students, drawing on van Schaik, described it this way:
- It was based on ritual and practice, not on doctrine.
- It was not unified. It had no single main text and no single founder or leader.
- It varied by region and was organized by clan. Each clan had its own deities, rituals and practices, which matches the political picture of the time: the tsenpo was "more like a chieftain" over clans.
- The instructor stressed that the comparison is not a judgment about which system is better.

### 4. Comparing reach: how Buddhism spread (map exercise)
- **Sites in north India:**
  - Bodh Gaya, where the Buddha attained enlightenment.
  - Sarnath, the first teaching.
  - Kushinagar, where he passed away (the setting of the *Mahaparinirvana Sutra*).
  - Pataliputra (probable reading of the garbled name).
  - Gandhara, another major Buddhist base.
- **Nalanda:** the most influential university in Asia in the 5th-6th centuries. Xuanzang studied there, and its remains were excavated by the British and can be visited.
- **Theravada routes (green on the map):** south to Sri Lanka, then by sea to Southeast Asia (Burma, Laos, Cambodia).
- **Mahayana routes (red on the map, the most dominant):**
  - Northern route: through Central Asia to China.
  - Southern sea route: through Southeast Asia into China, via Guangzhou.
  - From China onward to Korea and Japan, and to Taiwan.
- A route across the Himalaya was also mentioned, but that passage is unclear.
- **Conclusion from the map:** by the 6th-7th century Buddhism was a transnational religion across Asia with a very large following. Bon was confined to Tibetan regions and was less influential.

### 5. Drawing (and not over-drawing) the conclusion
- **Tempting but unsupported:** "Buddhism was more influential, so Songtsen Gampo deliberately chose it to replace Bon." The comparison of the two objects can't prove the agent's intent.
- **Student points:**
  - The marriage alliance with the Chinese princess brought Buddhism in, so the adoption may have been indirect. Songtsen Gampo took advantage of it.
  - Songtsen Gampo may have wanted legitimacy on the international stage. The instructor said this is "partly right."
  - It may have served internal unification. The instructor said "this could be."
- **Instructor's conclusion:**
  - Replacing Bon is too radical a claim, because Bon still held strong influence and political power in Tibet.
  - Tibet was surrounded by Buddhist states: China, Central Asia, India, and others (one name on the map is unclear).
  - Buddhism worked as a "civilization," a "common language," and an "operating system" covering language, culture and ways of behaving. It let Tibet interact with its neighbors.
  - So Songtsen Gampo adopted Buddhism to fit Tibet into the regional geopolitical situation. He introduced and planted it, but did not use it to replace Bon.
- More on Buddhism will come in about ten minutes of Friday's class.

## Key people, places and terms
- **Songtsen Gampo:** 7th-century tsenpo who built the early empire; the name means "Songtsen the Wise." (van Schaik ch. 1, "Tibet Appears, 600-700," pp. 12-13; Kapstein ch. 3)
- **Tsenpo:** title of the Tibetan emperor, who ruled over powerful clans. (van Schaik ch. 1)
- **Bon (Kapstein: Bön):** pre-Buddhist ritual specialists and practices. In the earliest documents it is "a kind of priest or ritual, not an organised religion." (van Schaik ch. 2, pp. 24-25; Kapstein ch. 2)
- **Princess of Wencheng:** Chinese bride won for Songtsen Gampo by his minister Gar. Tibetan tradition credits her with bringing the first Buddha statue to Tibet. (van Schaik pp. 9-10; Kapstein ch. 3)
- **Tripitaka ("Three Baskets"):** the Buddhist canon of Sutra, Vinaya and Abhidharma. (Kapstein ch. 3; Dunhuang article)
- **Sutra:** a record of the Buddha's discourses. (Dunhuang article)
- **Vinaya:** the monastic code of discipline and vows; Tibetan *dülwa*. (Kapstein, chapter on the monastic colleges; van Schaik p. 110)
- **Abhidharma:** "higher" or "meta" doctrine, a systematic treatment of the teachings; Tibetan *ngönpa*. (Kapstein; Dunhuang article)
- **Shastra:** a commentary or treatise. (said in class only)
- **Pramana:** Buddhist logic and epistemology. (said in class; Kapstein discusses Dharmakirti's logical works as the core of the monastic curriculum)
- **Bodhicitta:** the mind set on enlightenment, combining compassion and wisdom. (said in class)
- **Six realms:** gods, asuras, humans, animals, hungry ghosts, hell beings. (said in class)
- **Mahayana:** the "Great Vehicle," the later expanded doctrinal tradition. (Kapstein)
- **Nagarjuna:** Indian philosopher of about the 2nd century CE who founded Madhyamaka and taught emptiness. (van Schaik pp. 107-109; Kapstein)
- **Madhyamaka:** the "Middle Way" school; Tibetan *uma*. (van Schaik pp. 107-109; Kapstein)
- **Shunyata (emptiness):** the idea that nothing has a fixed, independent nature; it does not mean "nothingness." (van Schaik p. 108)
- **Vasubandhu:** 4th-5th century figure associated with Mind Only. (said in class only; not found in the readings)
- **Cittamatra (Mind Only):** the school holding that only mind is ultimately real. (said in class only)
- **Xuanzang:** 7th-century Chinese pilgrim-monk, contemporary of Songtsen Gampo, who studied at Nalanda. (said in class only; not in the readings)
- **Nalanda:** the great Indian monastic university. (van Schaik p. 67)
- **Theravada:** the Buddhism of Sri Lanka and Southeast Asia. (van Schaik p. 110)
- **Tantric Buddhism / Mantrayana:** the esoteric "vehicle of secret mantras." Kapstein calls it Vajrayana. (Kapstein)
- **Chang'an (modern Xi'an):** the Tang capital. (van Schaik ch. 1)
- **Jokhang and Ramoche:** Lhasa temples associated with the princesses' Buddha statues. (van Schaik p. 10)

## Connections to the readings
- **van Schaik, ch. 2, "That Old-Time Religion" (pp. 23-26):** the textual basis for the class's description of Bon.
  - Before Buddhism, Tibetans "probably did not think of themselves as following any particular religion."
  - Bon in early documents "is a kind of priest or ritual, not an organised religion."
  - Each clan had its own mountain deity and warrior gods.
  - These old ways "preserved the established order: the rule of the tsenpos, the role of the clans." The same section says the spirit world "never left Tibet," which supports the instructor's point that Buddhism did not replace Bon.
- **van Schaik, ch. 1, "Tibet Appears, 600-700" (pp. 9-14):**
  - Gar's mission to Taizong, and the princess bringing the first Buddha statue to the Ramoche and Jokhang temples (p. 10).
  - "Buddhism, for the time being, was only one of many new cultural imports circulating in Tibet" (p. 11). Good evidence against the "replacement" thesis.
  - pp. 12-13 on the clans remaining "a threat to Songtsen's lineage," relevant to the internal-unification argument.
  - p. 14 on later Tibetans seeing Songtsen as the first great Buddhist king and doubting that he really created a Buddhist law code.
- **Kapstein, ch. 3 (about pp. 65-66):** "the historical record does not in fact offer a clear demonstration of the support for Buddhism with which he is credited." This is exactly the instructor's warning about claiming Songtsen Gampo's intent without concrete sources.
- **Kapstein, ch. 3 (about pp. 74-75):** later emperors used Songtsen's "tolerance of the alien faith" as a precedent. The dynasty's explicit adoption of Buddhism dates only to 762 under Trisong Detsen. Useful for separating tolerance or introduction from state adoption.
- **Kapstein, ch. 3 (p. 93):** the empire created "a unique civilizational sphere," with Tibetan as lingua franca and "the primacy of the Buddhist religion." This parallels the instructor's "Buddhism as civilization / common language" framing.
- **van Schaik, pp. 107-109:** Nagarjuna, Madhyamaka, emptiness and the two truths. Matches the Mahayana section of the lecture.
- **van Schaik, p. 110:** Tibetan monks follow the Vinaya, whose Tibetan version has slightly more rules than the Theravada one. Relates to the Vinaya discussion.
- **Dunhuang library cave article:** the Tibetan-era catalogues sort texts into "sutras, vinaya, abhidharma," and the article refers to "The Tripitaka from Dunhuang Manuscripts." A concrete example of the three baskets in practice.
- **Not confirmed in the readings:** Xuanzang, Vasubandhu, Mind Only, the Chinese emperor's vegetarian rule, the alms-round details and the robe colors. These came from the lecture only. The Gethin *Foundations of Buddhism* file in readings-text/ is empty, so it could not be checked.

## Writing and reading-skills guidance
- **Know which kind of paper you are writing.** A summary-based paper starts from sources you have collected and reports the author's main arguments and structure. A research-based paper starts from a question. The midterm and final essays must be research-based analytical papers, not summaries (syllabus; see also the UNC handout "Summary: Using it Wisely" on summary vs. analysis).
- **Don't start with the conclusion or your opinion.** Design the research first, like designing a lab experiment, and let the conclusion come from the evidence. A conclusion drawn at the start will mislead you.
- **Expect to be surprised.** Dissertation proposals always speculate about a conclusion, but after the fieldwork and study, researchers often reach the opposite one.
- **Analyze your topic sentence.** Identify the key elements (agent and objects) before planning the structure.
- **Pick a method and perspectives.** Comparison is fundamental. Decide on a main perspective and a secondary one.
- **Compare along explicit dimensions.** Here those were doctrine/theory and community/influence.
- **Don't let a comparison of objects answer a question about an agent's intent.** Claims about what a historical figure wanted need concrete evidence: historical texts, archaeological material, or art.
- **Avoid "too rushed, too radical" conclusions.** Qualify your claims. "Adopted Buddhism for geopolitical integration" is supported. "Used Buddhism to replace Bon" is not.
- **Stay neutral when comparing traditions.** Describe the situation without ranking them.
