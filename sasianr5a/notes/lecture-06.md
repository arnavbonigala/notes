## Lecture notes

### Reading and composition as an information process
- Reading collects information; composition analyzes it and presents it for a purpose. The instructor compared reading to gathering ingredients for a meal and writing to cooking it.
- The purpose of writing is a **main argument**, which counts as "new information." In the humanities, new information can be a newly discovered fact, but more often it is your own **interpretation** of materials, theories or concepts.
- Humanities scholarship treats concepts as its "data." You find logical connections between concepts and contribute a new concept that has a concrete basis, follows a logic, and is reasonable.
- Academic writing is not a place to put your emotions on paper or just describe things. It has to contribute analysis, ideas and arguments.

### Three angles for analyzing "reading"
1. **Agent (who reads):** You. In this course you read in an academic setting to build academic reading skills, not for entertainment. You gather and process information according to academic rules.
2. **Object (what you read):** "Text" in a broad sense.
   - Media: physical paper books and digital texts. The instructor prefers digital because it is easier to search and annotate, and scholars move often.
   - Genre: journal articles and books, plus personal and "micro-historical" material such as diaries and letters.
   - Other media: documentaries, films, Wikipedia, podcasts. Podcasts were raised as a platform for sharing knowledge with the public.
   - Oral accounts count once they are recorded, as long as the information is reliable.
   - In class you read what is assigned. For your own research, check with your professor about which kinds of sources, including primary sources, you can use. Primary sources will be covered in a later session.
3. **Action (how you read):** Students named several difficulties: unfamiliar geography with no map, material far from their own background, and getting lost in details. The instructor grouped these under language, terminology, and structure.

### Difficulty 1: Language
- Reading in a non-native language: you only need to learn the language as far as you actually use it. The instructor works with classical texts (classical Tibetan, Sanskrit, classical Chinese, Japanese) and does not need conversational modern fluency. Beyond that, the only method is to keep reading; there is no shortcut.
- **Language is concepts plus logic.** A word marks a concept whose boundaries have to be negotiated, and the relationships between concepts are its logic. Different languages carry different logics.
- Example: English prefers the active voice and accepts the first-person "I," which fits a Western philosophical habit of observing the world from the self. Chinese, Japanese, Korean, Sanskrit and classical Chinese often leave the agent out, and you have to infer it from context. In those traditions using "I" can seem arrogant, so the speaker is presented as one humble point in a larger whole. Chinese and Japanese also have several words for "I," and the choice of word carries meaning.
- Takeaway: to read a language well, you need to understand its logic.

### Difficulty 2: Terminology
- Unfamiliar terms are new concepts in a new discipline, and there's no way around learning them. Learning them is like learning classmates' faces over several weeks.
- Build an "archive" of terms in your "background book," meaning the knowledge you already carry. For each term, learn how to pronounce it and explain what it means in your own words.
- The instructor will write terms such as Lhasa on the board to help students build this archive.

### Difficulty 3: Structure (getting lost in details)
- A good academic work has a clear framework with three parts: **main argument**, **body** (the evidence, materials, method and theory that support it), and **conclusion**. The conclusion may gather several smaller arguments and is not identical to the main argument.
- For articles, keep a map of the argument in your head. The main argument is usually at the start or in the abstract.
- **Worked example 1: van Schaik, *Tibet: A History*.** The title gives no time period, so it covers the whole span of Tibetan history from a historical angle. "Tibet" itself is ambiguous: it could mean an ethnic group, a region, or a regime. The table of contents is organized by period, which gives you a timeline to return to as you read.
- **Worked example 2: Luciano Petech, *Central Tibet and the Mongols: The Yüan-Sa-skya Period of Tibetan History*** (shown in class, not an assigned reading). The title narrows "Tibet" to *Central* Tibet as opposed to the wider Tibetan area, and "the Mongols" places the book in the Mongol period. (The instructor said "12th century." van Schaik dates the first Mongol army entering Tibet to 1240, which is the 13th century.) You can skip the acknowledgements. The chapters run: introduction and sources; establishment of the Yüan-Sakya partnership; the institutional framework; a chapter on the period (the title was garbled in the transcript); decline and fall; concluding remarks. That list gives you a tree to hang details on.
- **Titles are not always a reliable guide.** Some authors use poetic titles, and you need to know the field to decode them.
- **AI as a lookup tool:** it's fine to ask an AI what "Yüan" or "Sakya" means as a starting point, but don't trust it too much. Its information is not always accurate.
- **Step-back habit:** after each chapter or section, pause for a minute and ask how it connects to the previous section and what the author is trying to prove. Break long chapters (around 40 pages) into smaller pieces and check your place on the map each time. This is how you remember a book a year later instead of just its title.

### From reading structure to writing structure
- An essay needs a map too. Each part should support the main argument: stating it, proving it, critiquing opposing views, or restating it in the conclusion. Don't write "here and there."

## Key people, places and terms
- **Tibet / Bod:** Tibetans use *Bod* for the central region and *Bod chen po* ("Greater Tibet") for the wider cultural area. van Schaik, Preface (p. xv).
- **Lhasa:** Originally *Rasa*, "Walled City," on the Kyichu river. It was renamed Lhasa, "Divine City," as the tsenpo's power grew. van Schaik ch. 1, "Power and the Princess" (p. 4).
- **Jokhang:** Temple in Lhasa, built on a Nepalese model. It was once called Rasa Trulnang or Tsuglagkhang; "Jokhang" is mainly a Western name. van Schaik ch. 1 (p. 6) and note 6 (p. 270).
- **Songtsen Gampo:** 7th-century tsenpo (c. 605–650 per Kapstein), central to this week's marriage prompt. van Schaik ch. 1; Kapstein ch. 3.
- **Tsenpo:** Title of the Tibetan emperor, "the embodiment of the divine in this world." van Schaik ch. 1 (p. 2).
- **Sky cord:** The cord of light by which the first tsenpos came down from heaven and returned to it; relevant to the legitimacy prompt. van Schaik ch. 1 (pp. 2–3).
- **Princess of Wencheng:** Chinese princess brought to Tibet by minister Gar Tongtsen, c. 640–641. Kapstein ch. 3 (pp. 60–61); van Schaik ch. 1 (p. 6) and note 11.
- **Bhrikuti:** Nepalese princess that later sources say Songtsen married. Some scholars doubt she existed. van Schaik note 6 (p. 270).
- **Central Tibet:** The core region around Lhasa, as opposed to the eastern regions of **Kham** and **Amdo**. van Schaik, Preface (p. xvi).
- **Yüan / Sakya (Sa-skya) period:** Mongol Yüan dynasty rule over Tibet through the Sakya school. van Schaik ch. 4, "Patrons and Priests, 1054–1315"; see van Schaik's note citing Petech (1990).

## Writing and reading-skills guidance
- Read for structure first: title, then author, then table of contents, then introduction. Put each detail in its place on your map.
- Pause after every section and ask what it proves and how it links to the last one.
- Keep a term archive with pronunciation and a definition in your own words.
- **How to read a prompt:** find the key term first (prompt 1: names; prompt 2: legitimacy; prompt 3: royal marriage as a strategy). Then notice that each prompt asks two things: **summarize**, then **analyze**.
- **Summary is not a data dump.** Organize the information in your own logic so it has a main argument, like "a salesperson" telling the reader a story. You still can't distort or make up historical facts.
- **Analysis** means drawing your own idea or critique out of the material. For the marriage prompt, for example: why marriage was used as a tool, whether it worked, and what effects it had. Pick one angle, since 500 words can't cover everything.
- **Essay shape:** introduction (the main argument condensed, often into one sentence), body, conclusion.
- **Sample essay** (a former student's "Sunday Christianity" paper, four paragraphs plus references). The header gives name, course code, assignment, instructor and date; the instructor recommends this habit so you can track drafts. The first paragraph shows how an introduction works:
  - Sentence 1 raises the topic.
  - Sentence 2 disagrees with the common view ("this critique oversimplifies…").
  - Sentence 3 gives the reason, using a scholar's theory of ritual.
  - Sentence 4 states the specific main argument.
  
  Each body paragraph opens with a topic sentence that states its sub-argument. The conclusion restates the main argument.
