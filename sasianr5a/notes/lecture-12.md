# Lecture 12: The Silk Road, Dunhuang and the Mogao Caves

*(Week 5, Wed. Sep. 23, per the syllabus schedule)*

## Summary
This session moved from the Silk Road as a whole to Dunhuang and its Buddhist cave complex. The instructor traced the route from its opening by a Han envoy in the second century BCE to its mature form under the Tang, then asked who actually traveled it: merchants, envoys, soldiers, and Buddhist monks. The point about monks was the pivot. Monks had renounced property and were barred from violence, so they needed merchant caravans for money, food, route knowledge and armed protection. That is how Buddhism reached Chang'an. The rest of the class was a slide-by-slide walk through the Dunhuang caves: the cliff face with its wooden facades, the parinirvana scene with Maitreya, the clay statues, the square block at the center of a sixth-century cave, the Buddhas of the Three Times, a tantric deity, and a narrative mural of a Central Asian monk reading the signs of a seven-tiered stupa. The last twenty minutes turned all of this into a writing exercise. The instructor posed the essay prompt "Why did people build thousands of Buddhist caves at Dunhuang?" and made the class build the structure before answering: break the prompt into its key terms, then analyze each one.

## Lecture notes

### The Silk Road: name, origin, users
- The name "Silk Road" was coined by a German geographer at the end of the nineteenth century. The instructor did not name him, and the name does not appear in the readings.
- The route itself is much older. In the second century BCE a Chinese emperor sent an envoy west to explore. The envoy returned and recorded what he had seen, and from then on the Chinese pushed the route further west, eventually reaching Europe. The envoy was not named in class.
- By the Tang, the seventh century, the road was fully mature.
- Who traveled it, from the class discussion: merchants first of all, then officials and envoys, then armies and soldiers, then religious missionaries. The instructor stressed that kings and rulers were not among them. His phrasing here was partly unintelligible, but the sense was that rulers stay in their palaces and rarely travel far.
- Missionaries on this road were mostly Buddhist monks, including monks from Central Asia.

### Why monks traveled with merchants
The instructor made the class work this out. Three reasons:
1. **Money and material support.** To be a monk means to renounce lay life, so monks held no wealth of their own.
2. **Provisions.** Monks could not supply themselves across a route that long.
3. **Protection.** Violence is prohibited by the Buddhist precepts, so monks could not defend themselves. Caravans crossed deserts and mountains, knew the roads, carried weapons, and traveled with guards.

The result: monks entered China with merchant groups and reached the capital, Chang'an, which the instructor called the heart of China.

### Dunhuang as the hub
- Dunhuang sits at the crossroads of the Silk Road. Anyone going east to west or west to east had to pass through it. That is why it matters. (This repeats and extends the map work from the end of Lecture 11.)
- The caves are not in the town itself but out in the rural area nearby.

### What the caves physically are
- Natural cliffs of rock and sandstone, cut into caves. The wooden doors and facades on the cliff face were built later by Chinese hands. The instructor was explicit that the facade is not natural.
- Cave temples have a long history in India, so the instructor treated the Dunhuang complex as an imported tradition.
- **Ajanta as the comparison.** The Ajanta caves in India began, most likely, in the second century BCE. They have two phases: an earlier one built under what the transcript renders as "Parabada" (almost certainly Theravada, or the pre-Mahayana schools more broadly), and a later Mahayana phase roughly five centuries on. The instructor showed one picture only and said Ajanta was not the day's topic. Ajanta does not appear anywhere in the course readings.
- **The material difference, and why it matters.** Ajanta rock is hard, close to marble in working quality, so it takes fine carving. The instructor used the art-historical language of fine carving in rock. Dunhuang statues are **clay**, not stone. The walls and ceilings are rock; the figures on them are modeled clay, plus painted murals.

### Inside the caves: what was on the slides
- **The parinirvana scene.** The reclining figure is Shakyamuni Buddha at his last moment, entering parinirvana. Buddhist cosmology holds many ages and many realms, with one Buddha per age; Shakyamuni is the Buddha of our age and our realm.
- **Maitreya.** The standing figure beside him is the future Buddha, the next Buddha, who will succeed Shakyamuni as teacher of the coming age. The name means kindness, and he is associated with loving kindness. His identifying posture is the giveaway: Maitreya does not sit cross-legged in meditation but stands, or sits in a chair the way modern people sit. A student's first guess in class was the compassionate Buddha; the instructor corrected it to Maitreya.
- **The 3D effect.** Statues, ceiling and wall murals together were meant to put the viewer inside the scene, as if present at the Buddha's last teaching. The instructor compared it to a 3D movie.
- **Dating range.** Excavation begins in the fourth century, under the Wei dynasty, which the instructor noted was not a Han Chinese regime but one run by northern nomadic peoples. The latest caves date to the fourteenth century. So roughly ten centuries, a thousand years of continuous construction and use.
- **The sixth-century cave and the square block.** A cave from the sixth century has a central square stone block. Student guesses: for meditation, for offerings, or a platform that once held a statue. The instructor said the last is plausible, and that some scholars argue it was for ritual. He named an unnamed "expert" who argues it was used for tantric practice and rejected that: in the sixth century tantric Buddhism did not yet exist, appearing about a century later. He said the class would come back to this.
- **Seventh and eighth century caves.** Still clay statues and murals, but a visibly different Buddha style. The instructor placed these close to Songtsen Gampo's period, slightly later than his lifetime.
- **Buddhas of the Three Times.** A mural of three Buddhas: past, present and future. The instructor noted other explanations exist, and that specific identifications such as Medicine Buddha, Amitabha and Shakyamuni are guesses unless an inscription says so. This was a methodological point: without written evidence, you are speculating.
- **A tantric deity.** Its presence dates that mural after the seventh century, most likely tenth to twelfth, once tantrism had matured. Not general Mahayana imagery.
- **The stupa narrative mural.** A monk, two more monks, an official, and a figure with palms joined worshipping a stupa of seven tiers. The class counted the tiers. Seven is a crucial symbolic number in Buddhism. The mural reads like a comic strip, telling a story: a Central Asian monk who traveled to China uses his supernormal powers to interpret auspicious or inauspicious signs of the stupa. The instructor's phrasing here was garbled and the monk's name did not survive the transcription.

### The writing exercise: turning a topic into an essay
The prompt: **Why did people at Dunhuang construct thousands of Buddhist caves?**

The instructor refused to take answers until the class did the structural step first.

1. **Establish the easy part.** Are these Buddhist caves? Yes, and you can say why from the evidence: Buddhist statues, deities, donor figures making offerings.
2. **Break the prompt into its key terms.** Two elements: **Dunhuang** and **Buddhist caves**. That gives you two lines of analysis. Why this place, and what was the purpose or function of the caves.
3. **Analyze the place.** Buddhism spread the length of the Silk Road, which is very long and full of important states, Chang'an among them. So "Buddhism was spreading" does not explain Dunhuang specifically. The instructor's own phrasing was partly lost, but he pointed out that the Buddhist kingdoms along the route disappeared from history while Dunhuang survived.
   - **Location.** Everyone had to pass through. This is the main point, not the only one.
   - **Economy.** A student's answer, which the instructor took: caravan traffic created wealth and resources, so people settled there. A good place to live.
   - **Surrounding Buddhist context.** You can also argue that Buddhism was already prevalent in the regions around Dunhuang in this period.
   - **Geography and climate.** The map shows desert on one side and green land at the oasis. Dry air preserves clay statues and murals; in humid weather pigments oxidize and fade. This is also why Dunhuang uses clay while Ajanta uses carved stone: different rock, different climate, different technique.
4. The instructor's summary: for Dunhuang alone, at least two aspects, its significance as a crossroads and its geographical features. He was interrupted mid-sentence and the class ended before the second key term, the function of the caves, was worked through.

Note that the instructor invited guesses without demanding correct answers. The exercise was about generating candidate arguments, then sorting them, not about getting it right first try.

### A dating muddle worth flagging
The transcript records the instructor saying the Dunhuang library materials date to the tenth, eleventh and twelfth centuries, and that this means "the caves should be constructed after the twelfth century." That cannot be what he meant, since he had just dated the earliest caves to the fourth century. Either the transcription dropped a qualifier or the sentence was misspoken. Rong Xinjiang's article, which the instructor told the class to have read, argues the sealing took place soon after 1002, not as late as Pelliot's 1035 date. Check this point with the instructor or a classmate before relying on it.

## Key people, places and terms
- **Silk Road**: trade network linking China to Central Asia and Europe, named by a German geographer in the late nineteenth century. (van Schaik; Kapstein ch. 4, "the Inner Asian Silk Roads")
- **Chang'an (modern Xi'an)**: Tang capital, eastern end of the route. (van Schaik pp. 1 to 2; Kapstein)
- **Dunhuang**: Silk Road town in Gansu at the convergence of the trade routes, site of the Mogao cave complex. (Kapstein ch. 5: the Gansu Corridor, "where trade routes converged before entering China proper")
- **Mogao Caves / Qianfodong, the Caves of the Thousand Buddhas**: the cave temple complex outside Dunhuang. (Rong Xinjiang, pp. 253 and 258)
- **Ajanta Caves**: Indian rock-cut cave complex, begun around the second century BCE, with a later Mahayana phase. Cited in class as the Indian precedent. (from class; not in readings-text)
- **Wei dynasty**: the regime under which the earliest Dunhuang caves were cut in the fourth century, described in class as a northern nomadic rather than Han Chinese regime. Rong mentions Northern Wei material found in refuse deposits at the caves. (Rong, p. 1181 of the extracted text)
- **Shakyamuni**: the historical Buddha, the Buddha of our age. (van Schaik; Kapstein)
- **Parinirvana**: the Buddha's final passing, the subject of the reclining-Buddha sculpture. Rong mentions the *Mahaparinirvana-sutra* among the texts found in the Library Cave. (Rong, pp. 169 and 857 of the extracted text)
- **Maitreya**: the future Buddha, identified by a standing or seated-in-a-chair posture rather than cross-legged; name connected to loving kindness. Kapstein quotes a Tibetan text calling him "sublime Maitreya, the Buddha's regent." (Kapstein)
- **Buddhas of the Three Times**: past, present and future Buddhas as a mural grouping. (from class)
- **Stupa**: reliquary monument; the mural showed a seven-tiered example. (Kapstein)
- **Tantric Buddhism**: later esoteric form; the instructor used its absence before roughly the seventh century to refute a scholar's reading of the sixth-century central block. Van Schaik dates the appearance of the tantras in India to the fifth century AD. (van Schaik ch. 3, p. 7 of that section)
- **Three Realms (Sanjie) Monastery**: the small Dunhuang monastery whose holdings Rong argues supplied most of the Library Cave's contents. Not discussed in class, but central to the assigned article. (Rong, pp. 615 onward)
- **Daoist Wang (Wang Yuanlu)**: the self-appointed guardian who broke through the wall of Cave 16 and found the Library Cave in 1900. (Rong, pp. 123, 157, 1386)
- **Hongbian**: the cave that became the Library Cave was originally his meditation site. (Rong, p. 956)

## Connections to the readings
- **Rong Xinjiang, "The Nature of the Dunhuang Library Cave and the Reasons for Its Sealing."** This was the assigned article and the instructor referred to it directly ("You read Rong Xinjiang's paper, right?"). The opening frames the 1900 discovery of Mogao Cave No. 17 in the north wall of Cave 16. Pelliot concluded the cave was sealed in 1035 against the Xi Xia invasion, based on the absence of Xi Xia writings and the chaotic piling of documents. Rong argues instead that the sealing happened soon after 1002, prompted by news reaching the monks of the Three Realms Monastery. He also argues against Stein's "waste repository" reading: material from a small monastery awaiting repair is not refuse.
- **Rong, pp. 258 and following.** Directly supports the lecture's account of Dunhuang as a Buddhist center. Buddhism flourished by the early Tang; under Tibetan rule and the massive patronage of Buddhism the number of monasteries, monks and nuns increased, and monasteries grew from a handful under the Tibetans to seventeen or eighteen. Longxing was the largest, Three Realms one of the smallest. Use this if you write the "why Dunhuang" essay: it gives you institutional evidence, not just geography.
- **Rong, pp. 340 to 350.** Stein's numbering used "Ch'ien-fo-tung" (Qianfodong, cave of the thousand Buddhas) and small Roman numerals for the "orderly bundles," so the original arrangement of 1130 bundles (1050 Chinese plus 80 Tibetan) can be partly reconstructed. Relevant to the instructor's repeated methodological point: without written or documentary evidence, identification is guesswork.
- **Kapstein, ch. 5, on the Gansu Corridor**: "where trade routes converged before entering China proper." This is the sentence behind the instructor's map argument about Dunhuang as the hub.
- **Kapstein, ch. 4**: "Throughout late antiquity and the early middle ages, the Inner Asian Silk Roads..." and the description of Silk Road territories as "the heart of the so-called Silk Road linking China to the West," including wealthy states. Useful for the point that Dunhuang was one node among many, which is exactly the objection the instructor raised against a lazy answer.
- **Kapstein, ch. 5**: the Tibetan conquest of Dunhuang, "the major Silk Road entrepôt and center of Chinese Buddhism," and the roughly three-quarters of a century of Tibetan occupation. This is the bridge from today's art-historical material back to the course's actual subject.
- **van Schaik, ch. 3**: the Buddhist tantras "had begun to appear in India in the fifth century AD," with the goal of Mahayana unchanged but faster methods introduced. This is close to but not identical with the instructor's classroom dating (tantrism arriving about a century after the sixth). If you use the argument against the tantric reading of the central block, use van Schaik's date and be precise about what "appear" means.
- **van Schaik, ch. 1, p. 1**: Chang'an as a cosmopolitan capital. Background for the instructor's "heart of China" line and for why monks were headed there.
- **van Schaik, image caption**: "ninth century, Dunhuang Cave 159." A reminder that van Schaik uses Dunhuang cave art as evidence too.

## Writing and reading-skills guidance
- **Structure before content.** When handed a prompt, take one step back and identify the key terms before you start answering. "Why did people build Buddhist caves at Dunhuang" has two terms, Dunhuang and Buddhist caves, and each one is a section of the essay.
- **Test whether your reason is specific to your subject.** "Buddhism was spreading along the Silk Road" does not explain Dunhuang, because Buddhism spread everywhere along the road. A reason that would explain any node explains none of them. This is the single most transferable move from today's class.
- **Stack multiple causes rather than picking one.** Location, economy, surrounding religious context, and climate all contribute. The instructor kept asking "any other possibilities?" after every accepted answer.
- **Material evidence can carry an argument.** The clay-versus-carved-stone contrast between Dunhuang and Ajanta is an argument about geology and climate that you can see in a photograph. Look at what a source is made of, not only what it depicts.
- **Do not identify beyond your evidence.** On the Three Buddhas mural: "if there is no written words to indicate, we can guess." Mark a guess as a guess. The same applies to the parallel UNC handout point about not overstating what your evidence shows.
- **Refute with dating.** The instructor dismissed a published scholar's reading of the central block by showing the practice he invoked did not yet exist. Chronology is a clean way to attack a claim.
