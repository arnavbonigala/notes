# Lecture 27: Building Buddhism in Tibet — Shantarakshita, Padmasambhava and the Making of Samye

## Summary

The session continued the analysis of Trisong Detsen as a dharma king, a title he earned for spreading Buddhism in Tibet. The instructor framed the whole class around one practical question: to establish Buddhism, what do you actually need? The answer given was people and a physical base. The people were the Indian abbot Shantarakshita, invited twice because ministers blocked the first attempt, and the tantric adept Padmasambhava, brought in when ritual power was needed. The base was Samye monastery, roughly forty kilometers from Lhasa and not in Lhasa, built on the plan of the Buddhist universe with Mount Meru at the center, four continents in the four directions and an outer wall for the rim of the world. The instructor argued Samye is the first authentic Buddhist monastery in Tibetan history because it was the first site with all Three Jewels present, including a living Vinaya tradition and ordained Tibetan monks, which the earlier Lhasa temples lacked. The class closed on the contrast between the scholar and the yogi as two permanent types of Tibetan Buddhist, and on the shocking imagery of tantric Buddhism, with the question of why Tibetans accepted tantra and Chinese Buddhists largely did not left open for students to carry.

## Lecture notes

### Recap: Trisong Detsen and the question of how Buddhism gets established

The class opened mid-recap on Trisong Detsen as a dharma king, so named because he greatly spread Buddhism in Tibet. The instructor asked students to analyze the mechanism rather than accept the label: how was Buddhism installed, and which Buddhism was it? Was it the same thing as what arrived with Songtsen Gampo's queens, officers and diplomats from China and neighboring countries, the Mahayana tradition, or something else? The working answer for this session: establishing a religion requires people first, then a foundation, a place to develop and organize.

### Shantarakshita, invited twice

Trisong Detsen sent for Shantarakshita, described in class as a great abbot of the university, to come teach Buddhism in Tibet. He came, but the first attempt was strongly opposed by the ministers, so the king had no choice and sent him back. Twelve or fifteen years later, grown up and holding more power, Trisong Detsen was able to invite him a second time, and Shantarakshita returned. This time the king asked him to build a real monastery so that activities could be properly organized. Shantarakshita designed the monastery and guided its construction.

Legend says the work went up in the daytime and was destroyed overnight by demons. The instructor offered two readings. One takes the demons as real local spirits and local deities. The more likely reading, in the instructor's view, is that the "demons" were the powerful anti-Buddhist ministers. This surfaces a tension between Buddhism and the ministers, and the instructor posed a question he explicitly refused to answer: when a minister opposes Buddhism, is that a conflict over religion only, or something else? Students raised hands and were told to hold the question. The reminder attached to it: this course is a political and religious history of Tibet at the same time, so a religious conflict is rarely only religious.

### Padmasambhava: the name and the birth story

Shantarakshita's strategy was to have a second person invited. The instructor used the name as a Sanskrit lesson, since Sanskrit compounds can be split into roots and each root read on its own. Padma means lotus, specifically the white lotus, distinguished by color from other lotus terms. Sambhava comes from the root meaning to exist or to come into being, so the compound means "born from the lotus." Grammatically the instructor read it as a possessive or descriptive compound: the person born from a white lotus.

The accompanying legend: a king went out to a lake, saw a white lotus in the middle of it, the flower opened, and there was an infant inside. He took the infant back to the palace. Padmasambhava's homeland is the Swat valley in modern Pakistan, which the instructor stressed is very important in Buddhist archaeology, with excavated statues and paintings from the early and pre-imperial period. A former professor here, now in France, showed images from the valley in class in earlier years. The instructor said there was no time to develop this.

### Who is more powerful, and why

Two questions were put to the class. First, who is Padmasambhava? Second, why would Shantarakshita, himself a learned and renowned Buddhist, judge Padmasambhava more capable or powerful for this particular job? Student answers: he practices a different kind of practice; he came from a royal household, was raised to be an heir, and renounced it. The instructor supplied the technical term: yogi, again parsed from Sanskrit, from the root yuj, to join or combine. What is joined is practice, experience, body and mind, so yogi became a noun for a person who practices, a practitioner rather than a textual scholar.

Padmasambhava then traveled across India looking for the right masters and eventually reached Nalanda University.

### Nalanda, Xuanzang and how the ruins were found

The instructor asked what other famous figure is associated with Nalanda, and after the class offered Tang Taizong, corrected the reference to Xuanzang, the monk who is the basis for the main figure of Journey to the West and who traveled slightly earlier than, or around the era of, the imperial Tibetan kings. The point of the detour: Nalanda was the center of Buddhist scholastic society in the medieval period, it collapsed, and no record of it survives in Indian historical materials. The site was excavated by a British archaeologist, who located it by reading Xuanzang's travelogue, which described the site, its location and its organization clearly enough to find it.

The instructor then walked through site photographs. A small square structure, which a student guessed was a seat or altar, was identified as a monk's cell, the monks' dormitory, with the stairs and the ruin of a stupa visible nearby. He recommended visiting in person.

### Rewalsar and the demonstration of magical power

Padmasambhava studied at Nalanda and traveled to other Indian places. The instructor returned to a site mentioned in the previous class, giving the Tibetan name as Tso Pema (lotus lake) and the modern name, read off the map, as Rewalsar. He showed its location and the lake, which he described as small, more a pond than a lake. The story as told in class was partly unintelligible on the recording: it involves a local king or princess, Padmasambhava practicing yoga there in the mountain, and his use of magic to produce or transform the lake. The usable point is the one the instructor drew: before Tibet, he was already exhibiting and demonstrating magical power, and the place is still a holy site for Buddhist practitioners.

### Subduing the demons and the naming of Samye

In Tibet, Padmasambhava set up an altar on a small hill in front of the monastery site. The hill is still there and is short compared with other Tibetan mountains, but is regarded as holy because of that altar, and a small building stands on top of it at the supposed spot. There he practiced tantric chant and mantras and subdued the demons, which allowed the monastery to be completed.

The naming story: when the king first came and saw the finished monastery, he bowed, was so amazed he could not control himself, and exclaimed a word the instructor rendered as "sam ye." It has no propositional meaning, closer to "how splendid, how magnificent," so the monastery took the name Samye.

### Why Samye is not in Lhasa

Asked whether Samye is in Lhasa, the class said no. One student reasoned that Lhasa would have been the natural center from which to spread Buddhism. The instructor's correction to the assumption behind the question: Lhasa is the capital today, but it was not the center of the imperial period in the way students imagine. The city was built under Songtsen Gampo, abandoned after the collapse of the empire, and its later glory comes from the Dalai Lamas' government. The Buddhist center of the imperial period lay elsewhere, in the southern valleys. On the map he showed that Samye sits roughly forty kilometers from Lhasa, and he connected the site to an imperial summer residence. A few words here were garbled.

### Reading the plan of Samye as a model of the universe

The instructor moved through photographs of the complex, distinguishing the main shrine from the whole site: the shrine at the center, a circular enclosing wall, and four stupas, white, green, red and yellow, in the four directions.

He asked what the outer circle recalls. Students offered samsara and then mandala, which he confirmed, referring back to the mandala he had shown on Monday. The reading built up in class, element by element:

- The outer round wall represents the rim or edge of the universe.
- The four colored stupas in the four directions represent the four continents, our own world being the southern one, Jambudvipa.
- The main shrine at the center, square-ish and multi-storied, represents Mount Sumeru, the axis of the world.

So Samye is a scale model of Buddhist cosmology. The instructor pushed the inference: a king who builds the universe in miniature and owns it is making a claim about himself. The student answer he accepted was that the king positions himself as a universal monarch at the center of the world, which the instructor affirmed, calling him the owner of this monastery and so of this universe.

### Three architectural styles in one building

Looking at the layers of the main shrine, the instructor identified:

- The lower, outer layers: white walls, windows with angular trapezoidal frames painted black, which he called typical Tibetan architecture.
- The middle storey: square with a tiled sloping roof, which he called very traditional Chinese architectural style.
- The top: four small towers or finials, which he called Indian, and which he connected to a great Indian monastic university whose name he could not recall during class. Van Schaik supplies the name, Odantapuri, as the model for Samye's layout.

He noted that van Schaik mentions an outside influence on the style and that he has no further evidence to confirm it himself. He emphasized that this combination is an innovation with no precedent and no successor: no other Tibetan Buddhist architecture combines these three styles, before or after Samye.

Asked why combine rather than develop a purely local style or simply copy India, the class and instructor arrived at an answer about sources. Later regimes, after the collapse of imperial Tibet, had reasons to cover or hide the connections between imperial Tibet and China, and the surviving written record is thin. The building itself is evidence that survived that editing. It shows that Tibetan Buddhism was not sourced from India alone: Chinese Buddhism was a real component, arriving with the Chinese princess who brought a monk, scriptures, a Buddha statue and ritual materials, and with the later Chinese princess who supported monks and translation activity. The structure therefore reads as a statement: Tibetan style as the foundation and base layer, with Indian and Chinese sources converging above it into a new Buddhism rooted in Tibet.

### The building today

Samye still stands. The instructor said the original building was damaged or destroyed during the Cultural Revolution and called this sad. After China reopened in 1979 or 1980, the Bhutanese royal family, closely connected to the Nyingma tradition, played a large part in funding and pressing for the rebuilding, with a queen mother petitioning the Chinese authorities for permission. The name of the Nyingma head he credited was not recoverable from the recording. He said three pillars inside are original, with some original stonework.

For visitors: on the ground floor there is a narrow dark corridor circling the main shrine with no interior light, so you walk through darkness, which he likened to samsara, before reaching the second floor and the Buddhas. Inside the main shrine is a very small room behind a door only waist or chest high, easy to miss if nobody points it out, said to be where Padmasambhava gave tantric initiation to Trisong Detsen and his chief ministers.

### Why Samye counts as the first authentic monastery

The instructor asked students to remember two things: the structure and style of Samye and what they represent, and Samye's significance as the first authentic Buddhist monastery in Tibetan history. The emphasis fell on "authentic," since Lhasa already had the Jokhang and Ramoche.

The argument: in Songtsen Gampo's period the monks at those temples were all foreigners, from China, Central Asia, India, Nepal and Khotan. There were no local, indigenous Tibetan monks. Of the three divisions of the canon, Vinaya, sutra and commentary, the Vinaya was absent as a living tradition, and without a living Vinaya there can be no ordination and therefore no Tibetan monks. So under Songtsen Gampo, Buddhism was imported as culture rather than established as a religious institution. At Samye the Three Jewels were all present: Buddha in the statues, Dharma in the texts, and Sangha in ordained Tibetan monks.

### The ordination of ministers

The first Tibetans ordained at Samye came from the aristocracy, the upper level of society, not from common or grassroots backgrounds, and some of them were the king's own ministers. The instructor dwelt on how strange this is as a political act: imagine being a king and asking your right hand, your most important officials, to join the monastic order. He asked students to reflect on it as they read, and again declined to answer: why did Trisong Detsen want his ministers ordained? A student answered that he trusted them, which the instructor accepted as a first layer, then pressed further: if trust is the reason, why not keep them as ministers with their secular identity intact? Another student suggested it was about joining politics and religion. The instructor left this open as a question to carry.

### The translation project

Starting from Samye, Trisong Detsen organized a large-scale translation project, collecting Buddhist texts from China, Central Asia and India, in Chinese, Sanskrit and Middle Indic languages. This was the first time a Tibetan court organized translation at that scale. The instructor noted that the organizational model was itself borrowed from China, from Xuanzang's translation bureau, and used this to make a methodological point: outsiders tend to treat Chinese Buddhism and Tibetan Buddhism as separate things, but looked at closely the traditions are organically connected, India to China, China to Japan, China to Tibet, India to Tibet.

### Scholar and yogi as two types

Returning to his second question, the instructor drew the contrast he had been building toward. Shantarakshita is the scholastic type: deeply learned in doctrine and theory, renowned for his scholarship, and unable to handle the demons. Padmasambhava is not renowned for scholarship or composition but is powerful in practice, in mantra and ritual, and is for that reason called an adept. Hence two main types of Buddhist in Tibet:

- Scholars, typically monastic and ordained.
- Yogis or practitioners, not necessarily ordained, who can be monks but can equally be lay practitioners.

He gave Tibetan terms for each, neither of which came through clearly on the recording. Van Schaik's term for the lay tantric practitioner is ngagpa. The instructor argued this pairing organized Tibetan Buddhism from the start and persisted into the later monasteries of the lamas and figures like Sakya Pandita: one group oriented to learning and status, another dedicated to practice, with a few people good at both and most people falling to one side.

### Recap: why the life story was told

The instructor closed by telling students what the point of the Padmasambhava material was. It is not so that they can tell a friend a cool story about a monk, and not the biography for its own sake. He pointed to the iconography instead. Asked why the statue looks the way it does, a student said he does not look peaceful, and the instructor agreed: wrathful rather than serene, which is distinctive of Tibetan tantric Buddhism.

He then showed two images of tantric deities. In the first, a male and female figure embrace. Students offered love, then marriage, which he rejected as imprecise, then sex, which he confirmed: sexual union is one major element of Tibetan tantra, with the male figure representing compassion and the female representing wisdom. He stated that in the earlier period, from roughly the seventh and eighth centuries through the eleventh and possibly later, this was practiced literally as part of attaining realization and empowerment, not only symbolically.

The second image was a wrathful deity trampling a human figure, associated with consuming human flesh and organs. The instructor named the apparent contradiction directly: Buddhism is supposed to be peaceful and non-violent, and this is genuinely disturbing. Tantric Buddhism interprets the sexual and violent imagery in its own way.

He then set up the comparison he wanted students to sit with. Tantric Buddhism arose around the middle of the seventh century and was imported into China in the mid-seventh century; about a decade later a Japanese monk carried that tantra from China to Japan, where the tradition survives. But that transmission belongs to what he called the lower or outer tantras, different from Tibetan tantra. The tantra transmitted to Tibet arrived in the middle of the eighth century, through Padmasambhava, who is regarded as its founder there. Final questions, left unanswered with two minutes remaining: why did Tibetans accept this esoteric, wrathful, violent form of Buddhism while Chinese Buddhists largely did not, and why was Padmasambhava able to subdue the demons?

## Key people, places and terms

- **Trisong Detsen**: the tsenpo who invited Shantarakshita, founded Samye and launched the translation project. Rendered in the transcript as Chusun-gyu-Tzen, Chisholm Giu-Tan and similar.
- **Shantarakshita**: Indian Buddhist abbot and philosopher, the scholastic pole of the pairing. Invited twice; ordained the first officially recognized Tibetan monks.
- **Padmasambhava**: tantric adept from the Swat valley in modern Pakistan, invited on Shantarakshita's recommendation to subdue the local spirits. Later known by the honorific Guru Rinpoche, the Precious Teacher.
- **Samye**: Tibet's first monastery, in the Tsangpo valley roughly forty kilometers from Lhasa. Van Schaik translates the name as "The Inconceivable" and dates completion to the 780s; Kapstein gives c. 779.
- **Odantapuri**: the Indian monastic complex in Bihar on which Samye's layout was based, the name the instructor could not recall in class.
- **Nalanda**: the great Indian monastic university where Padmasambhava studied, located in modern times by reading Xuanzang's travel account.
- **Xuanzang**: Chinese monk-traveler whose travelogue preserved the record of Nalanda, and the historical basis for the protagonist of Journey to the West.
- **Tso Pema / Rewalsar**: lake site in the Indian Himalaya associated with Padmasambhava's display of magical power.
- **Mount Sumeru, the four continents, Jambudvipa**: the cosmological scheme that Samye's plan reproduces, with our own world as the southern continent.
- **Mandala**: the circular-and-square diagram of the cosmos that the Samye ground plan follows.
- **Yogi**: from Sanskrit yuj, to join or combine, so a practitioner, as opposed to a textual scholar.
- **Padma and sambhava**: lotus, specifically white lotus, and coming into being, giving "lotus-born."
- **Three Jewels**: Buddha, Dharma, Sangha, all three present at Samye for the first time in Tibet. Kapstein renders the imperial-era phrase as the Three Precious Jewels.
- **Vinaya**: the monastic disciplinary code, whose absence as a living tradition meant no Tibetan ordinations were possible before Samye.
- **Nyingma**: the tradition the instructor traced to Samye and connected to the Bhutanese royal family's role in the rebuilding.
- **Jokhang and Ramoche**: the Lhasa temples of Songtsen Gampo's era, staffed by foreign monks, which the instructor used as the foil for Samye's claim to be the first authentic monastery.

## Connections to the readings

- **van Schaik, chapter "The Holy Buddhist Empire, 700–797," pp. 33–36**: the whole Shantarakshita and Padmasambhava narrative. Van Schaik adds detail the lecture skipped: the Lhasa noble Ba Selnang, who converted after a Buddhist death ritual, was the man sent to Nepal to invite Shantarakshita; on arrival the abbot was quarantined in the Jokhang for months while a translator was found, the exiled Brahmin Ananta, and was cross-examined by a minister before the tsenpo would hear him. He was sent back to Nepal after a flooded temple and a lightning strike on the royal castle were blamed on his presence. This is the documentary substance behind what the instructor compressed into "opposed strongly by his prime ministers."
- **van Schaik, p. 34**: the demon-subduing scene, with mirror divination, summoning the spirits by name and clan, Padmasambhava threatening them and Shantarakshita teaching them karma through a Tibetan translator. Van Schaik also records what the lecture did not: Padmasambhava was disliked at court, taught Mahayoga, Anuyoga and Atiyoga, pushed irrigation schemes, was accused in a whispering campaign of planning to seize power, and was eventually asked to leave, with archers sent after him. Useful if the "demons as ministers" reading interests you, since the political hostility is explicit in the text.
- **van Schaik, pp. 35–36**: the architectural and cosmological description. Note a real discrepancy with the lecture. Van Schaik says the three-storey main building had its first storey in Indian style, the second in Chinese style and the third in Khotanese style. The instructor read the lower layers as Tibetan and the top as Indian, and said he had no further evidence for van Schaik's attribution. Flag this rather than smoothing it over; it is exactly the kind of gap a short essay can be built on.
- **van Schaik, p. 36**: the Samye pillar edict, quoted in full, in which the tsenpo, his sons, lords and ministers swear never to abandon the supports for the Three Jewels at Rasa, Dragmar and elsewhere. This is the best primary-source anchor for the lecture's claim about the Three Jewels and for the ministers' involvement.
- **van Schaik, p. 38**: the translation project, Trisong Detsen inviting Indian and Nepalese teachers to work with Sanskrit-trained Tibetans, building a canon that later filled some three hundred volumes and required inventing Tibetan technical vocabulary.
- **Kapstein, The Tibetans, chapter 3, pp. 75–78**: the complementary account. Kapstein dates Samye to c. 779 and states that Shantarakshita ordained the first Tibetan monks officially recognized by the court, with the caveat that some Tibetans may have been ordained earlier into the Khotanese sangha established by the princess of Jincheng. He also quotes the Testament of Ba on the 150 households assigned to support the community, with annual allotments of barley, butter, robes, horses, paper and ink, and raises the possibility that the cost of maintaining the religion became unsustainable by Relpachen's reign. Plate 11 shows Samye from Mount Hepo with exactly the Mount Meru plus four continents plus outer wall reading the instructor developed from photographs.
- **Kapstein, p. 77**: the trinity of patron, monk and tantric adept, Trisong Detsen, Shantarakshita and Padmasambhava, revered as the three constituents of the Tibetan religious world. This is the scholarly version of the lecture's scholar-versus-yogi typology, with the royal patron added as a third term.
- **Kapstein, pp. 77–78**: the caution the lecture did not raise. Padmasambhava is so obscure historically that some have proposed he never lived, and the legends were elaborated in terma revealed from the twelfth century onward, centuries after the events. Kapstein notes that the earliest documentation does not foreground his tantric feats at all. If you write about Padmasambhava, this is the methodological problem you have to handle.
- **Where the lecture went beyond the readings**: Xuanzang and the British excavation of Nalanda, the Rewalsar story, the Bhutanese royal family's role in rebuilding Samye, and the transmission of outer tantra to Japan are not covered in the assigned texts. On Nyingma, van Schaik presents the name as a later retrospective label, "The Old Ones," applied to lineages holding the tantras translated in the earlier period, rather than as a school founded at Samye. Keep the lecture's framing and the reading's framing distinct in your own writing.

## Writing and reading-skills guidance

No formal writing instruction was given this session, but the lecture modeled several moves the UNC handouts ask for.

- **Etymology as close reading.** The instructor's treatment of Padmasambhava and yogi was a demonstration of reading a term by splitting it into roots and reconstructing meaning from them, rather than accepting a gloss. The same habit applies to translated technical vocabulary in both course texts.
- **Reading a building as evidence.** The architecture of Samye was treated as a source that survives when written records have been edited or suppressed. UNC's Evidence handout makes the matching point about visual evidence: photographs, illustrations and visual representations of fact are all open to interpretation, so you must guide your reader through the interpretation rather than present an image and let it speak. The instructor's step-by-step walk from outer circle to four stupas to central shrine to political claim is a usable template for that kind of paragraph.
- **Hold open questions instead of closing them.** Four questions were deliberately left unanswered: whether anti-Buddhist ministers were objecting to religion or to something else; why Trisong Detsen wanted his own ministers ordained rather than kept as secular officials; why Tibetans accepted wrathful tantra when Chinese Buddhists largely did not; and why Padmasambhava rather than Shantarakshita could subdue the demons. These are essay prompts in disguise. Each one asks for an argument, not a summary, which is what both the midterm and final essay specifications require.
- **Distinguish legend from history.** The instructor consistently separated what "legend says" from what he thought likely, as with the demons. Kapstein does the same explicitly for Padmasambhava and names the source problem. Your own prose should keep the distinction visible with phrasing, not leave it to the reader to infer.
- **Resist the separate-traditions assumption.** The instructor's point that Chinese, Tibetan and Japanese Buddhism look separate from outside but are organically connected on close inspection is an argumentative move worth copying: name the common assumption, then show the connection that undermines it.
