# Lecture 7: What Princess Wencheng's Marriage Meant for Tibet (Clothing, Ritual, "Operating Systems," and the First Arrival of Buddhism)

## Summary
This lecture continued last week's topic of Songtsen Gampo's marriage alliances. It focused on the Chinese princess Wencheng and asked what the marriage meant beyond diplomacy. The instructor listed what she brought to Tibet: Buddhist monks, texts and a Buddha statue, plus craftsmen, officials and material goods. The class then did a close reading of a Tang Annals passage in which Songtsen feels ashamed of his own clothing and ritual when he meets the Chinese delegation. Clothing was read as a sign of technology (silk), social wealth and aesthetic values. Ritual was read as a sign of a society's ideology, which the instructor compared to a computer's "operating system," with long examples from Confucian East Asia and modern Korea. The class ended on Buddhism: the first Buddha statues, the demoness legend, and the argument that in Songtsen's time Buddhism stayed within elite circles.

## Lecture notes

### Course plan
- After the first chapter of van Schaik (the first emperor), the course turns to Buddhism using Rupert Gethin, *The Foundations of Buddhism*. The class has already read the introduction, which covers Buddhist traditions in different regions and their features.
- Next week: Gethin, chapter 1, on the Buddha as a historical person and as a figure in Buddhist culture. After that, some basic Buddhist doctrines.

### Recap: marriage as strategy
- Songtsen Gampo used marriage to consolidate power inside his kingdom and to build alliances with neighbors, especially Nepal and China.
- Sources: the Tibetan side has almost nothing. The transcript says the marriage appears only as a very short, single sentence (probably a reference to the Old Tibetan Annals, but this part is garbled). The Chinese side has much more, mainly the Tang Annals. Later Tibetan histories also tell the story at length.
- Question for the day: was this only a diplomatic event, or did it have wider effects on Tibetan history and culture?

### What Wencheng brought with her
- **Buddhism:** Buddhist monks, Buddhist texts, and a Buddha statue.
- **People:** officials who escorted her (they probably went back to China once the mission was done) and craftsmen. The instructor mentioned people with agricultural skills and other artisans. The part about porcelain and printing is garbled, though the instructor did say printing had not been developed yet.
- **Materials:** many goods, clothing and textiles in particular.

### Close reading: the Tang Annals account of the meeting
- According to the Chinese record, Songtsen and his ministers went out to meet the delegation. He was amazed by how fine and elegant their dress was and felt ashamed of his own clothing. He was also impressed by their ritual behavior and felt ashamed of how his own ministers conducted themselves.
- **Source criticism:** this is a Chinese source, so it may be self-celebration. The instructor said that flavor is "highly possible." The next step is to ask what else the passage can tell us.
- **Why clothing and ritual?** The record does not mention how large the delegation was or how rich its goods were. It names only dress and behavior. Those are what anyone notices first about a stranger, before knowing anything of their background.

### Clothing as evidence
1. **Technology.** Silk is not a natural material. It takes specialized techniques, from raising silkworms to reeling thread from cocoons. China held a monopoly on silk for a long time, which is why it became a trade good along the Silk Road. Silk signals a civilization with advanced techniques in many areas beyond cloth.
2. **Social wealth.** A society near subsistence has no surplus to spend on luxury goods. A peasant would not wear silk because it is fragile. Silk clothing shows that Tang China had accumulated a lot of wealth.
3. **Aesthetic value.** Taste cannot be measured, but it grows out of a person's culture, class, education and access to resources. The delegation's dress displayed China's aesthetic values.

### Ritual as evidence, and the "operating system" metaphor
- Ritual here means more than religious ceremony. It means everyday patterns of behavior. The instructor's examples: in China a student shows respect to a teacher in certain ways, and people never address parents or superiors by their given names. In the US people sometimes do, which would be offensive in China, Japan or Korea.
- Behind behavior patterns sit social values and culture. Behind those sits an intellectual system or ideology.
- **State and culture need each other.** The metaphor: a state's population, army and territory are the *hardware*. Ideology or culture is the *operating system*. Laws, administration, ethics and social norms are the *software* that runs on it. Different operating systems (Windows or macOS, Android or iOS) can sometimes run the same apps and sometimes cannot, and data does not always carry over between them.
- **China's operating system: Confucianism.** Many schools of thought competed before the Han emperor Wudi made Confucianism the state ideology. Later dynasties kept "updating" it to fit their times, so an ideology is never static.
- **The Chinese cultural circle.** "China" can mean two things. One is territorial China. The other is a Confucian and Chinese-character cultural sphere that included Korea, Vietnam and the Ryukyu Kingdom. Japan is a partial case: it borrowed heavily in the Tang period, then developed its own culture on that basis, and never took Confucianism as its main operating system. (This follows up last week's lesson on defining the boundaries of a concept like "China.")
- **Extended example: Korea changing operating systems.**
  - Korea long saw China as the center of the world and adopted Chinese ideology, administration and writing.
  - After Japan defeated China in the first Sino-Japanese War, Japan became the dominant power in East Asia. Japan had westernized under the slogan "civilization and enlightenment," reforming its dress, writing and social norms.
  - Korea debated whether to drop Confucianism entirely or combine it with Western ideas. Japanese colonization then discredited the Japanese model for Koreans, and no third system took hold.
  - After World War II, North Korea adopted a Marxist system close to the Soviet one and South Korea a Western one: two "operating systems" meeting at a Cold War front line. The instructor added that South Korea has become heavily Christianized.
  - The point: ideology is the framework that lets a state's laws, administration and norms function. Without it, things become chaotic.
- **Back to Tibet.** Songtsen's shame about dress and ritual shows that Tibet was a young state that was not yet highly developed. It had no writing system yet, so it could have no developed ideology, only basic rules and customs. Seen as an investment, the marriage paid off: Tibet gained materials, techniques, and a model of an operating system.
- **A clarification the instructor stressed:** this does not mean Tibet became Confucian. Tibet learned from China but went on to build its own operating system. Buddhism became Tibet's main ideology only later ("starting from the 10th century," in the instructor's words). In the mid-7th century Buddhism was still new and unfamiliar.

### Buddhism arrives
- The marriage gives the first official record of Buddhism being brought to Tibet.
- **Two statues.** Tradition holds that two Buddha statues were consecrated by the Buddha himself: one belonged to Songtsen's Nepalese wife and one to Wencheng.
- The instructor called the consecration story a later Buddhist fabrication. In the Buddha's time there were no Buddha statues. Early Buddhism stressed emptiness (*shunyata*), and form was treated as a basis for attachment. Early objects of worship were the tree, scriptures, and a monument marking the Buddha's remains (the transcript is garbled here, but a stupa fits). Buddha images came much later.
- **Why belief still matters:** a religion's power comes from people's belief, the same way a constitution has authority because people accept it.
- **Wencheng's activities:** she installed the statue in a temple, probably copied and spread scriptures, taught Buddhism, and told Buddhist stories.
- **The demoness legend:** using astrological or geomantic calculation, she found that the land of Tibet had the shape of a supine demoness. Temples were built to pin her down and pacify the land. The instructor noted that later Tibetan Buddhist intellectuals don't like to talk about this story. The history of how the statues were later exchanged between temples is complicated and was left to the reading.
- **Bon was already there.** The priests who gave legitimacy to the ruler were Bon priests, not Buddhists. There was already a religious system in place, not fully systematized but long established. That raises the question of why the king still wanted Buddhism.

### Who had access to Buddhism?
- The class debated whether Buddhism was limited to the royal family, open to everyone, or somewhere in between. The instructor's answer was in between: it stayed within elite circles (political, intellectual, possibly economic elites).
- **Two transmission routes:**
  - North, by land: India to Central Asia to China to Korea to Japan. The instructor said Japan began sending envoys to China after a defeat by the Sui dynasty, the dynasty before the Tang.
  - South, by sea: India to Sri Lanka, Southeast Asia (Thailand, Indonesia) and on to China. Parts of this passage are garbled.
- Monks traveled with traders but aimed their teaching at elites. Reasons the class gave:
  1. They treated the teaching as precious, like a jewel.
  2. Education: most commoners could not read, so they could not "download" the system.
  3. Elites were rich and influential, so they could protect and fund Buddhism.
- Chinese intellectual elites, for example, filled their poetry and literature with Buddhist elements.
- **Songtsen as dharma king:** later Tibetan Buddhists called him the first dharma king, meaning a king who protects Buddhism. According to the instructor, he actually converted very late, probably shortly before his death.

### Unfinished
The marriage's effects covered so far are techniques, economic wealth and the operating system. The last factor, Buddhism as a religion, continues next session.

## Key people, places and terms
- **Songtsen Gampo:** Tibetan emperor (tsenpo) of the 7th century who built the empire and married foreign princesses. van Schaik ch. 1; Kapstein ch. 3.
- **Princess Wencheng:** Tang princess who came to Tibet in 641 escorted by Gar Tongtsen. Kapstein notes she was first intended for crown prince Gungsong Gungtsen. Kapstein pp. 61–64; van Schaik pp. 9–10 (not named there, called "the princess").
- **Gar Tongtsen:** Songtsen's prime minister, who negotiated the marriage at Chang'an. van Schaik pp. 6–9; Kapstein p. 61.
- **Bhrikuti (Tritsün):** the Nepalese princess of later tradition. Scholars question whether she existed. van Schaik p. 270 n.6; Kapstein p. 62.
- **Taizong:** Tang emperor who gave Wencheng in marriage. van Schaik pp. 7–9.
- **Tang Annals:** Chinese dynastic histories (Old and New Tang Annals) and the main Chinese source for the marriage. van Schaik notes; Kapstein p. 61 ("Chinese annals").
- **Old Tibetan Annals:** early Tibetan source, which suggests the princess was meant for Songtsen's son. van Schaik p. 270 n.11.
- **Jowo (Jowo Shakyamuni):** the Buddha statue said to have come with Wencheng. Kapstein pp. 61, 64.
- **Ramoche:** Lhasa temple where Wencheng's statue was first placed. van Schaik p. 9; Kapstein p. 63.
- **Jokhang (Rasa Trulnang, Tsuglagkhang):** Tibet's oldest Buddhist temple, built on a Nepalese model, where the statue now stands. van Schaik pp. 5, 9, p. 270 n.6.
- **Rasa / Lhasa:** Songtsen's central town, later renamed Lhasa. van Schaik ch. 1.
- **Supine demoness:** the Tibetan landscape pictured as a demoness pinned down by thirteen temples. The myth comes from the *Mani Kambum*. van Schaik p. 270 n.12.
- **Bon:** in the earliest documents, a kind of priest or ritual rather than an organized religion. van Schaik pp. 23–24.
- **Dharma king:** a Buddhist ruler who protects the teaching. Later tradition names three for Tibet. van Schaik, "dharma kings," index pp. 30–33.
- **"Red-faced barbarians":** Tibetans' self-deprecating name for their ancestors, from the custom of red face paint that the princess reportedly disliked. van Schaik pp. 9–10; Kapstein p. 61.
- **Shunyata (emptiness):** core Buddhist concept, used in class to explain why early Buddhism had no Buddha images. From the lecture; the Gethin text file is empty, so this could not be confirmed.
- **Silk Road:** trade routes linking China to the West. Kapstein ch. 3.
- **Han Wudi, Confucianism, Ryukyu, Sino-Japanese War, "civilization and enlightenment":** comparative background from the lecture only. None of these appear in the course readings.

## Writing and reading-skills guidance
- **Ask who wrote a source and why.** The meeting account comes from the Chinese side and may be self-celebration. Name that bias, then keep going.
- **Look at what the text chooses to mention and what it leaves out.** The record talks about dress and ritual, not the delegation's size or wealth. Ask why those two details, and what each one stands for.
- **Unpack a detail layer by layer.** Clothing pointed to technology, then wealth, then aesthetic values. Ritual pointed to behavior patterns, then values and culture, then ideology.
- **Use analogies to make abstract relationships concrete,** as with the hardware / operating system / software comparison for state and ideology.
- **Define your concepts and their boundaries.** This repeats last week's lesson: "China" as territory versus "China" as a cultural circle.
- **Separate history from later tradition.** The statues consecrated by the Buddha and Songtsen as the first dharma king are later Buddhist constructions. They still matter because people believe in them.
